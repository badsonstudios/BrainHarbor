## Do this now

1. **Stay with them.** Look at a clock and start timing it. Say their name and
   talk calmly. Most seizures stop on their own in a few minutes.
2. **Move things out of the way.** Push back chairs, tables and anything hard or
   sharp. If they are walking around and confused, steer them away from stairs,
   the road, or anything they could fall from.
3. **Help them to the floor** if they look like they are falling. Put something
   soft and flat under the head, like a folded coat. Take off their glasses.
   Loosen anything tight around the neck.
4. **Turn them on their side**, with the mouth pointing at the ground. This
   keeps spit from blocking the airway and helps them breathe.
5. **Look for a medical ID** on the wrist or neck, or on their phone. If they
   have a rescue medicine their doctor gave them for this, give it the way the
   doctor said.
6. **Stay until they are fully awake.** Then tell them, simply, what happened.

## Call an ambulance if

Most seizures do not need one. Call **911** (or your local emergency number) if:

- the seizure lasts **longer than 5 minutes**
- another seizure starts before they have come round from the first
- they are having trouble breathing, or they seem to be choking
- they do not wake up after it stops
- they got hurt
- **it happened in water**
- this is their **first ever** seizure
- they are pregnant
- they have diabetes and do not come round
- they ask you to

If you are frightened and you are not sure, call. That is what the number is
for.

## Do not

- **Do not put anything in their mouth.** Not your fingers, not a spoon, not a
  wallet. They cannot swallow their tongue. The jaw clamps hard, so you can be
  bitten and they can break a tooth.
- **Do not hold them down** or try to stop the shaking. It does not end the
  seizure. It can hurt them, and someone who is confused may fight back.
- **Do not give food, drink or pills** until they are properly awake. It can go
  into the lungs.
- **Do not start rescue breathing** during the seizure. Breathing often looks
  like it has stopped while the chest muscles are tight, and it starts again on
  its own. If they are still not breathing once the seizure is over, start CPR
  and call an ambulance.

## If it happens in water

A seizure in water is different. It can kill, and it needs an ambulance every
time.

1. Hold their head so the face stays above the water.
2. Get them out of the water as fast as you can.
3. Check whether they are breathing. If they are not, start CPR.
4. Call an ambulance, even if they seem completely fine afterwards. Water that
   got into the lungs can cause trouble later, and it is treatable when someone
   is looking for it.

This is the one situation where you call every single time.

## After it stops

Coming round takes a while. They may be muddled, exhausted, upset, or sore. They
may not remember any of it. All of that is normal, and it is not a sign that
something has gone wrong.

Ask them their name, where they are, and what day it is. If they cannot say,
tell them, kindly, and say they are safe. Do not leave them alone until they can
answer you, they are breathing normally, and you can wake them if they doze off.

Then offer to sit with them, or to call someone who can get them home.

## What to write down

They probably cannot tell anyone what happened, because they were not there for
it. You were. What you write down is the thing most likely to change their
treatment.

- What time it started, and how long it lasted.
- What they were doing just before.
- What you saw first: staring, a jerk in one arm, a noise, a fall.
- Which parts of the body moved, and whether it was one side or both.
- Whether they were awake or aware at any point.
- How long it took them to come back to themselves.
- Anything different that day: a missed dose, a bad night's sleep, being ill,
  alcohol, heat.

Take it to the next appointment. If they keep a
[seizure diary](/seizures/living-with), add it to that.

## Why this happens with a brain tumor

A seizure is a burst of unusual electrical activity in the brain. A tumor, and
the irritated brain around it, can set one off. Seizures are one of the most
common symptoms of a glioma, and for many people a seizure is the thing that
led to the tumor being found in the first place.

That means a seizure is not automatically bad news about the tumor. It is
information for the care team, and it is often something medicine can control.

## If this is likely to happen again

- Write a short **seizure action plan**: what a seizure looks like for this
  person, what to do, whether they have rescue medicine, and who to call. Give a
  copy to family, work and school.
- Get a **medical ID** bracelet, or put the details in the phone's medical ID so
  a stranger can find them.
- **Never change or stop seizure medicine on your own.** Stopping it suddenly
  can bring on a seizure. Somebody who has never had a seizure is
  sometimes put on it only around an operation, and meant to stop soon after.
  Somebody who has had one usually stays on it for years. Which of those you are
  is a question for the care team, and never one to settle at home.
  [Anti-seizure medicines](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) goes through the
  rest of it, including what a common one does to mood and temper.
- The rest of daily life has its own page:
  [living with seizures](/seizures/living-with).

## What to ask your team

- What do this person's seizures usually look like?
- Should we have a rescue medicine at home, and when would I use it?
- When do you want me to call you, and when should I call an ambulance instead?
- What should I write down when one happens?
- Who do we call out of hours?
