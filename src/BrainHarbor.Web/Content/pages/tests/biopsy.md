## The short version

A biopsy takes a small piece of the tumor so a lab can say what it is. It can be
done through a needle, using a scan as a map, or as part of an operation to take
the tumor out. It is not a treatment. It is how the tumor gets its name, and the
name is what your treatment is chosen from. Most of the time it gives an answer.
Sometimes it does not, and this page says what happens then.

## What is a biopsy?

!%stereotactic biopsy%!%neuronavigation%!%frozen section%It is taking a small
piece of the tumor out so it can be looked at and tested.

A scan can show where a growth is and how big it is. Often it can give your
doctors a good idea of what type it is. It cannot make that certain. Only the
tissue itself can do that.

There are two main ways to get it.

**Through a needle.** The surgeon makes a small cut in your scalp and drills a
small hole in the skull. A hollow needle goes through the hole and into the
tumor, and takes out small samples. Surgeons call the small hole a burr hole.
This whole thing is called a stereotactic biopsy, or a needle biopsy.

**During an operation.** If the plan is to take the tumor out anyway, the tissue
is taken then, and no separate biopsy is needed. That operation is a craniotomy.
[A craniotomy, step by step](/treatments/craniotomy) goes through it.

So a biopsy can be a procedure on its own, or it can be part of surgery.

## Why am I having one?

A needle biopsy is used when a sample is needed but taking the tumor out would
be too risky. That is usually because of where the tumor sits: deep in the
brain, or right next to something you would not want damaged.

**A needle biopsy is not an attempt to remove the tumor.** It is there to get an
answer, and that is all it is there for. If you were expecting the tumor to come
out, this is worth being clear about before the day.

It is a fair question to put to your team, and they are used to being asked it.
Ask which of the two you are having, and why they chose it.

## What happens, step by step

The rest of this page is about the needle biopsy. If your tissue is being taken
during an operation to remove the tumor, [a craniotomy, step by
step](/treatments/craniotomy) is your page instead: the day, the stay and the
recovery are all different.

1. **A scan first.** You have an MRI or a CT that the surgeon will navigate by.
   Small markers may be stuck on your scalp for it. A computer turns the pictures
   into a map of the inside of your head, and that map is called neuronavigation.
   [The scan just before surgery](/tests/mri#navigation-scan) goes through that
   appointment.
2. **Asleep, or awake.** A needle biopsy can be done under general anesthesia,
   so you are asleep. It can also be done with you awake, with the skin numbed
   where the cut goes. Ask which yours will be.
3. **Your head is kept still.** In one method a rigid frame is fixed to your
   head and the scan is used along with it. In the other there is no frame, and
   the computer map does the guiding. Studies that have compared the two have
   not found much between them, and the frame is used less often than it used to
   be.
4. **The cut and the hole.** A small cut in the scalp, then a small hole in the
   skull.
5. **The samples.** The needle is passed to the tumor and takes small pieces of
   it. Several samples are taken, not one.
6. **A quick look, often right there.** A piece is often sent straight to the
   lab while you are still in the room, to check that there is tumor in it. If
   there is not, the surgeon can take more before you leave. Your team may call
   that quick check a frozen section.
7. **Closing up.** The cut in the scalp is closed.
8. **A scan afterwards.** Your head is scanned again, usually within a few
   hours, to check that nothing is bleeding.

## How long does it take?

Ask your own team about the day itself, because it depends on where the tumor is
and on how your hospital runs. What follows is the shape of it.

- **The scan for the map:** a separate appointment before the biopsy. Ask when
  yours is.
- **The check scan afterwards:** usually a few hours later.
- **Going home:** some people go home the same day. Some stay the night. Which
  one you get depends on how your hospital is set up and on whether you fit the
  checklist they use, more than on how the biopsy went. Ask beforehand, so
  somebody knows whether to plan for a night.
- **The first word on what it is:** sometimes the same day, from the quick look.
- **The full answer:** at least a few days, and often longer, because gene tests
  are usually run on the tissue as well.

That last wait is the long one, and it has its own page.
[Waiting for your pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results) explains what is
happening to your tissue in that gap, and why the name you are given can change
while you wait.

## What does it feel like?

If you are asleep, you feel none of it.

If you are awake, the skin where the cut goes is numbed first. **The skull and
the brain do not feel pain.** There is nothing in the brain itself to feel the
needle with. That is strange to read and it is true, and it is why this can be
done at all with someone awake.

Afterwards, you may have a headache, and you may feel tired. It does not usually
hurt much, and there is pain medicine if you need it. Ask for it rather than
waiting to be offered.

The wound is small. It is one small cut, not the long one people picture when
they hear the word surgery.

## What can go wrong?

Your team will go through your own risks with you before you sign anything, and
that conversation is the one that is about you.

There is no percentage here, and there is a reason worth knowing. The published
rates for bleeding after a biopsy run from very low to very high, and most of
that gap is about what each study counted as bleeding, not about how risky the
procedure is. A number pulled out of that spread would tell you less than
nothing.

**Bleeding is the main one.** A small amount of bleeding where the needle went
is common, and most of it causes no symptoms at all. It is picked up on the scan
afterwards, and often nothing needs doing about it. Looking for it is what that
scan is for.

The other risks include an infection, a seizure, and bruising and swelling
around the spot. Bigger bleeding, and a new problem with speech, movement or
strength, are **not common**, which is the honest shape of it without a number.
The consent form names the rarer and more serious ones as well, and going
through it with you is what that conversation before the day is for.

Two things make bleeding more likely, and neither of them is about you. One is a
small target, because a small one is harder to hit cleanly. The other is
bleeding during the procedure itself, which your surgeon can see happening.

You may be given steroid medicine before or after, to settle swelling in the
brain.

That is what the watching afterwards is for. Someone checks on you, you get the
scan, and you are told what to look out for at home. The list is further down
this page, and it is worth reading with whoever is taking you home.

## The hard parts, and what helps

**Waiting on an answer you thought you were about to get.** The biopsy is the
step that gets the answer, so it is easy to expect the answer that day. Usually
what you get that day is "there is enough tissue", and the name comes later. Ask
before you leave: when will I hear, and who is going to call me?

**Being awake for it, if you are.** Nobody has to guess what that will be like.
Ask what you will be able to feel, what you will hear, and what they will do to
keep you comfortable. Ask what happens if you want it stopped.

**The frame, if you are having one.** A frame fixed to your head is the part
people find hardest to picture. Ask your team whether you are having one at all,
and if you are, ask them to talk you through how it goes on and how long it
stays on.

**Not knowing what to hope for.** A lot of people come out of this not sure
whether they want the answer to come quickly. That is an ordinary way to feel
and it does not need fixing. If it is sitting on you, there are people you can
say it out loud to today. [Get help now](/get-help-now) has the numbers.

## Is there a way to find out without one?

Usually not. For most brain tumors, tissue is the only thing that can give the
tumor its name, and the name is what treatment is chosen from.

There is one exception worth knowing. If a tumor sits somewhere that would make
it hard to take a sample from, such as the brain stem, a scan may sometimes give
enough information on its own. That is rare, and it is your team's call, not
something to ask for.

If you are worried about having the procedure at all, say that to your team
rather than to nobody. There may be more than one way forward, and the
conversation is a normal one to have.

## What you need first, and what to bring

Before the day:

- **The scan for the map.** If markers are stuck on your head for it, leave them
  alone until you are told otherwise.
- **Tell them everything you take.** Prescriptions, things you buy yourself,
  vitamins, the lot.
- **Ask which medicines to keep taking, and which to stop.** Take what you are
  prescribed exactly as you are told, and do not stop anything on your own.
- **Ask what time to stop eating and drinking**, and follow the times your own
  hospital gives you rather than any you read anywhere else.
- **Ask whether any hair will be shaved**, and where. It varies, and it is
  easier to know beforehand.

Bring:

- A list of your medicines, with the doses.
- Your questions, written down.
- Someone to get you home. **You must not drive after a brain biopsy.** That is
  not only the drive home. Your surgeon tells you how long it lasts and when it
  is safe to start again, so ask before the day rather than after it.
- Someone who can stay with you. If you go home the same day, an adult stays
  with you for the first day and night.

## For the person going with them

[CAREGIVER]

For this one day, your job is small and specific.

**Getting them home, and staying.** Ask before the day whether they are going
home afterwards or staying in. If they are coming home the same day, an adult
stays with them for the first day and night, so work out early who that is
rather than at nine at night.

**No driving.** Not that day, and not after it either, until their team says it
is allowed. Ask how long, and ask in front of them, because this is the rule
people bend first.

**The wound.** It is small, and it still needs looking after. Ask how it is
closed, whether the stitches dissolve or need taking out, and if they do, make
sure that appointment exists before you leave.

**What normal looks like for the next day or two.** A headache. Being tired.
Wanting to sleep a lot more than usual. None of that means anything has gone
wrong.

**Call the team the same day if:**

- The wound opens, or gets more painful, red, warm or swollen.
- Anything leaks from the wound.
- There is a fever, or a stiff neck.
- The headache is new, or worse, or comes with throwing up.
- They are confused, or cannot think clearly.
- There is a sudden change in vision.
- They are sleeping much more than they are awake.
- A fall, or a hit to the head.
- Pain that the pain medicine is not touching.

**Call an ambulance. These cannot wait:**

- They are not breathing properly.
- You cannot wake them.
- A seizure that is their first, or that lasts more than five minutes, or that
  runs straight into another. (Not every seizure needs an ambulance. The seizure
  page above says which do.)
- They suddenly cannot speak, move, or see.
- Chest pain, or coughing up blood.

If you are not sure which list this is, call.

## Why am I having another one?

Being told you need another biopsy is a hard thing to hear, and it is not a sign
that anything was done badly.

Most of the time a biopsy gives an answer. Sometimes the piece that came out
does not hold enough tumor to name, and then the question is how to get more.
The most likely reason is simply that the target was small. In one study of
frameless biopsies, the size of the tumor was the thing that decided whether the
sample gave an answer, more than anything else about the patient.

This is also why the surgeon often has a piece checked while you are still in
the room. If that check comes back empty, more can be taken there and then,
which is the whole point of doing it at that moment.

If you are told the sample was not enough, the useful questions are: what would
another one tell us that we do not know now, and what happens to my treatment
while we get it.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result?

Your tissue goes to a pathologist, or to a neuropathologist, which is a
pathologist who works on the nervous system.

The report goes back to the doctor who ordered it, usually your surgeon or the
specialist looking after you, and that doctor is the one who explains it to you.

One worry is worth answering here. A needle sample is small, and people ask
whether it is enough for the gene tests. In one study of eighty of these
biopsies, every sample was enough for the usual gene tests to be run on it. A
wider test was tried on a few of them and worked on nearly all of those.

You may be told part of it quickly and the rest later. The quick look during the
procedure can give a first idea the same day. The full answer takes longer,
because the tissue has to be prepared and the gene tests have to be run.
[Waiting for your pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is about that
gap. When the report itself arrives,
[your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report) walks down one.

Before you leave, ask who will call you and roughly when, and ask for that
appointment to be booked while you are standing there.

## What to ask your team

- Is this a biopsy only, or an attempt to take the tumor out?
- Why this way rather than the other one?
- Will I be asleep or awake?
- Will I have a frame on my head?
- Will any of my hair be shaved, and where?
- Which of my medicines do I stop, and which do I keep taking?
- Am I going home the same day, or staying in?
- Does somebody need to be with me overnight?
- How is the cut closed, and does anything need taking out later?
- Which tests will be run on my tissue, and where are they sent?
- When will I hear, and who is going to call me?
- What happens if the sample does not give an answer?
