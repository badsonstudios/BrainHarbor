## The short version

Your tissue is in a lab, and it is being worked on in a set order. The answer
comes back in layers, not all at once: a quick look during surgery, then the
microscope about a week later, then the gene tests a few weeks after that. The
name of your tumor can change as those layers come in. That is the system
working, not someone changing their story.

## What is being done with your tissue?

The piece of tumor that was taken out has gone to a lab. A doctor called a
neuropathologist works on it. That is a doctor trained to look at brain tissue
and say what it is.

They do two kinds of work. They look at the cells under a microscope. They also
test the genes inside those cells. The answer you are waiting for is both of
those, put together into one report.

## Why does it have to go to a lab at all?

A scan shows where a growth is, how big it is, and what it is pressing on. It
cannot tell you what the cells are. Only the tissue can do that.

The rules for naming brain tumors changed in 2021. For many types, the name is
no longer settled by what the cells look like on their own. The gene results are
part of the name. Two tumors that look the same down a microscope can end up
with different names, and different treatment, because their genes are
different.

That is truest for gliomas, where the gene tests are required before the tumor
can be named. For some other kinds, the microscope answer can stand on its own.
Ask your team which tests yours is waiting on, because that is what sets the
length of your wait.

## What happens to your tissue, step by step

1. **A quick look, while you are still asleep.** Whether the tissue came out in
   an operation or through a needle, a small piece is often checked right away,
   while you are still in the room. It is frozen, sliced and stained on the
   spot. This is called a frozen section, and it usually takes twenty to thirty
   minutes. It tells the surgeon they have the right tissue.
2. **The rest goes into a fixative.** The tissue sits in a liquid called
   formalin, which holds it steady so it can be cut and stained later. It needs
   about 6 to 24 hours in it. Nothing else can start until that is done.
3. **It is turned into a block.** The water in the tissue is taken out and
   replaced with wax. This is machine work, and it takes hours. How long depends
   on the size of the piece and on the machine.
4. **It is sliced and stained.** The wax block is cut into slices far thinner
   than a hair. They go on glass slides and are stained so the cells show up.
5. **The neuropathologist reads the slides.**
6. **Extra stains.** Special stains show whether particular proteins are present
   in the cells. Each round is another set of slides.
7. **The gene tests.** DNA is taken out of the tissue and tested. Some hospitals
   do this themselves. Some send it to another lab.
8. **It is all put together.** The neuropathologist writes one report that
   combines what they saw with the gene results. That is the integrated
   diagnosis.
9. **The meeting.** Often the finished report is discussed at a tumor board
   before your plan is set.

Most of that list is chemistry and machine time. It cannot be hurried by asking,
and it is not a line you have been put at the back of.

## How long does it take?

Ask your own team, because the answer is different at every hospital and they
are the only people who know how yours works. What follows is the shape of the
wait, not a promise about yours.

- **The quick look during surgery:** twenty to thirty minutes. Your surgeon may
  have it before you wake up.
- **The report from the microscope:** often about seven to ten days.
- **The gene tests:** usually a few weeks.
- **The full answer, with everything in it:** a few weeks from your operation.

Those last two are worth putting a real number beside. One national study looked
at 21 brain tumor centers in the United Kingdom. Gene panel tests and
methylation tests each took about three weeks on average. For half of those
centers the full answer took three weeks or less, and the wait had grown longer
over three years rather than shorter, as more testing was added.

The centers in that study are measured against a national target of 14 days for
the full answer. Most of them did not meet it. So if your wait is longer than
the number you were first given, you have not been forgotten and your case is
not strange.
Ask your team where yours has got to. They can usually find out in a phone call.

## Why some of it takes weeks

Two reasons, and neither of them is about you.

**The gene tests are run in batches.** One of them, called methylation
profiling, is run with about eight samples at a time. It takes around seven or
eight working days once it runs. The batching, the cost of each sample and the
small number of labs able to do it are all known drawbacks of this test, and
between them they are why it is counted in weeks.

**Some tests are sent somewhere else.** Not every hospital runs every test, so
tissue or DNA gets posted to a lab that does, often in another city. That is
normal and it is not a bad sign. It does add time. In the United Kingdom, one
national standard gives a lab 14 days when it does the test itself and 28 days
when it sends it out.

It is fair to ask which of your tests are being sent away, and where they have
gone.

## What the wait is like

This is the part with nothing in it. There is no appointment to get to and no
decision to make, and that emptiness is its own kind of hard.

Two things about it are worth saying out loud.

You do not have to have an answer ready for people who ask. "They are still
testing it, and I will know more soon" is a whole answer, and you can give it to
everyone.

And you may be told part of the answer before you are told all of it. That is
the next section, and it is the thing most worth knowing before it happens.

## The name of your tumor can change, and that is normal

If the name you are given changes, the tumor has not changed. What changed is
how much of it has been looked at.

The layers arrive in this order.

- **The quick look during surgery** is there to guide the operation, not to name
  the tumor. It matches the final answer about nine times out of ten. When it
  does not, sometimes the grade changes and sometimes the tumor turns out to be
  a different kind altogether. It is also less reliable for slow-growing tumors
  than for fast-growing ones.
- **The microscope answer** is the more accurate one, and it comes days later.
- **The gene results** can change the name again, because for many brain tumors
  the genes are part of the name.

So a name can gain words, lose words, or change grade while you wait. Reports
are laid out in that same shape, and
[your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report#where-the-answer-sits-on-the-page)
walks down one.

This is worth knowing in advance, because when it happens it feels like being
told two different things by the same team. Almost always it is not a mistake
being fixed. It is the answer arriving in the order the tests finish.

One question makes the whole thing easier to live with. Every time you are told
something, ask: **is this the final answer, or are we still waiting on tests?**

## What if the tests cannot give a clear answer?

Usually they can. In one large study, nearly all of the needle biopsies done for
a suspected glioma gave a complete diagnosis.

Sometimes they do not. The piece that was taken may not hold enough tumor to
test, and your team may need to talk to you about getting more tissue. Or the
answer may come back less exact than you hoped, with a general name rather than
a precise one. Neither of those means anyone has given up on the question.

If you are told either of these things, the useful questions are the same two:
what would the next step tell us that we do not know now, and what happens to my
treatment while we do it.

## Why is there a second report?

Sometimes the pathologist is sure of the diagnosis before all the testing is
finished. Rather than sit on it, they issue the report with what they know. When
the rest of the results arrive, they get added to the same report. The added
part is called an addendum.

An addendum is not a correction. It is the rest of the answer.

There is a second thing that can happen, and it is a different thing: a report
is amended. That means something already in it has been changed. An amended
report is labelled as one, and it normally says what was changed and why.

The commonest reason is a correction, such as a typing mistake or a measurement
that was wrong. Sometimes it is more than that, and something learned later,
from further testing or from a second opinion, changes the diagnosis itself. So
what an amendment means for you depends entirely on which of those it was. Ask
what changed. A corrected typing mistake may change nothing at all about your
care.

Being handed an amended report is unsettling, and it is worth saying what it
actually shows. The checking worked. Something was caught and put right instead
of being left to stand.

If someone hands you a report, it is fair to ask whether it is the complete one
and whether anything is still to come.

## Can someone else look at my slides?

Yes, and asking is a normal thing to do.

Naming these tumors is genuinely difficult, and a second opinion is usually
given by a neuropathologist who does this all day. They look at the glass
slides, not at you, so you do not have to travel to get one. Sometimes they run
extra gene tests as part of the review.

The practical side is ordinary. Your team can send the slides, or you can call
the other center and ask what they need. Expect some paperwork to sign. Ask
about the cost first, because that depends on your insurance.

Two things people worry about, answered. Asking does not offend anyone, and care
teams arrange this often. And it does not usually mean going without treatment.
Treatment for a brain tumor should not be put off or ignored, but there is
normally room to have someone else look first. If the delay is what worries you,
ask your team how much time you have. That is a question they are used to.

## What you can do while you wait

Not much of this is under your control. These parts are.

- **Ask who is going to call you, and when.** Then ask for that appointment to
  be booked now, so you are not sitting by the phone.
- **Ask whether the report will appear online before that call.** See the next
  section. It is worth deciding in advance.
- **Ask for a copy of the report** when it comes, and keep it somewhere you can
  find it. You will be asked for it more than once.
- **Write your questions down as you think of them.** Waiting produces
  questions at three in the morning and they are gone by the appointment.
- **Search carefully, or not at all.** If you look up a name you were given
  early, remember it may not be your final name. It is another reason to ask
  whether the answer you have is the finished one.

If someone is waiting alongside you, this is hard on them too, and they are
allowed to ask their own questions of your team. [Get help now](/get-help-now)
has the phone lines, including ones they can call themselves.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result?

The neuropathologist writes the report. It goes to the doctor who ordered it,
usually your surgeon or the specialist looking after you, and that doctor is the
one who explains it to you.

Your case may also go to a tumor board.

[TUMOR-BOARD]

There is one more thing to know if you are in the United States. Since April
2021, the law says your records have to be released to you as soon as they are
ready. In practice that means your pathology report can appear in your online
account before anyone has phoned you. In a survey of brain tumor specialists,
nearly all of them agreed that some patients are badly upset by reading results
this way, on their own, with no one there to explain them.

So decide now, while it is still a choice. Do you want to open it the moment it
lands, or wait for the call? Both are reasonable. If you would rather not see it
first, ask your team whether the release can be held until you have spoken.

And if you are reading this because you already opened it on your own and it
knocked the wind out of you: there are people who will talk it through with you
today, and [get help now](/get-help-now) has their numbers. Your own team will
go through the report with you as well, and you can ask them to start again from
the top.

## What to ask your team

- Is this the final answer, or are we still waiting on tests?
- Which tests are being run on my tissue?
- Is any of it being sent to another lab, and to which one?
- When should the full result be ready, and who will call me?
- Can we book that appointment now?
- Will the report show up in my online records before we speak?
- Can I have a copy of the report?
- Would you go through it with me line by line?
- Would you send my slides for a second opinion?
- Is my case going to a tumor board?
