## The short version

Your pathology report is the document that names your tumor. A doctor called a
neuropathologist wrote it, once your tissue had been looked at and its genes
tested. The answer is usually at the top, and everything under it shows how they got
there. If the name on the report is not the name you were given first, this page
explains why that happens.

## What is a pathology report?

It is a written report about the piece of tissue that was taken out of you.

A pathologist is a doctor who works out what a disease is by looking at cells
and tissue under a microscope, and by reading lab tests. For a brain tumor that
work is done by a neuropathologist, who is trained in brain tissue.

For a brain tumor the report says where in the body the tissue came from, what
type of tumor it is, its grade, which stains were used, and what the gene tests
found.

It has two jobs: to name the tumor, and to help your team choose treatment. It
is one of the most important documents in your care, and your treatment plan is
built on it.

What it holds is a description of the tumor. Deciding what to do about it is the
next conversation, and that one is yours and your team's.

## Where the answer sits on the page

Most reports put the final diagnosis at the top. That is the pathologist's
answer, and it is the part your team acts on.

Underneath come the layers that led to it: what the cells looked like, the
grade, and what the gene tests found. For brain tumors that order is on purpose.
The rules ask for the combined answer first, with the reasons set out under it.

So the top line is the conclusion, not the beginning. Reading from the top down
means reading the answer first and the evidence second.

Not every report is laid out the same way, and some put the diagnosis further
down. Look for the word "diagnosis" rather than for a fixed place on the page.

## Your report, part by part

Reports differ, and yours may not have all of these. These are the parts you are
most likely to see.

1. **Your details and the sample.** Your name, your date of birth, the date of
   your surgery or biopsy, a hospital number, and a number the lab gave the
   sample itself. Contact details for the lab and the pathologist are often
   here too.
2. **Clinical information.** What the doctor who took the tissue told the lab:
   your history, the reason the tissue was taken, and anything they asked the
   lab to look for.
3. **The diagnosis.** The name of the tumor and its grade. This is the part your
   team plans from. On a brain tumor report it is often headed the integrated
   diagnosis, because it puts what the cells showed and what the genes showed
   into one answer.
4. **Gross description.** What the pathologist saw with the naked eye, before
   any microscope: size, color, how the tissue felt, how many pieces there
   were. This part can be hard to read, because it describes your tissue the
   way it would describe any object. That is what it is for: a record of
   exactly what reached the lab.
5. **Microscopic description.** What the cells look like under the microscope.
   How abnormal they are, how they are arranged, and how they behave.
6. **Stains.** Special stains show whether certain proteins are in the cells. On
   a brain tumor report you may see one called GFAP. The results may sit in
   their own part of the report or inside the microscopic description.
7. **Gene results.** What the tests on the DNA found. Words like IDH, MGMT and
   EGFR belong here. These results may arrive as their own report, linked to
   this one. [The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes
   them one at a time.
8. **Comment.** Anything the pathologist wants the treating doctor to know. It
   may raise a concern, suggest more testing, or set out other possible
   answers. It also names the tests that have not come back yet, which is often
   the answer to "why does my report look unfinished".
9. **Addendum.** Results that were not ready when the report was first sent
   out, added on afterwards.

A missing part is not always a mistake. Some reports have no microscopic
section at all. If you cannot find something you expected, ask, rather than
assuming it was left out by accident.

## When is the report finished?

Ask your own team, because the answer is different at every hospital.

The shape of it is this. The part that comes from the microscope usually reaches
your doctor within about ten days of your surgery or biopsy. The gene results
take longer, and they often arrive after that first report has already been
sent.

That is why a report can turn up in pieces.
[Waiting for your pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the
whole wait: what is being done to your tissue, why some of it takes weeks, and
what you can do while it is going on.

## Why the name may not be the one you were told

If the name has changed, the tumor has not changed. The rules for naming brain
tumors changed in 2021, and gene results are now part of the name itself.

Before then the name came from what the cells looked like. Now, for many brain
tumors, the lab has to know what the genes say before it can finish naming it.
Two tumors that look the same under a microscope can end up with different
names.

So a name can gain words, lose words, or change grade while you wait.
"Astrocytoma" can become "astrocytoma, IDH-mutant". Most often those extra words
are the rest of the answer, arriving in the order the tests finish. Sometimes a
gene result changes the grade as well, and
[what the grade means](#what-the-grade-means) sets out how that happens.

It also means older paperwork and older websites carry names that the rules have
since dropped. That is worth knowing before you go looking.

If you are holding two reports with different names on them, ask your team which
name is the current one, and which tests it rests on.

## What the grade means

The grade is a number from 1 to 4 for how the tumor is expected to behave. It is
worked out from what the cells look like and from what the gene tests found, and
your team uses it as one part of planning treatment.

Two things about grades changed in 2021, and both of them cause confusion.

**The numbers are written differently.** Grades used to be written as Roman
numerals: I, II, III, IV. They are now written 1, 2, 3, 4. The reason is plain.
A II and a III, or a III and a IV, are easy to mistake for each other, and an
uncaught typing error in a grade can change someone's care. Your report may say
"CNS WHO grade 3". An older report, or an older website, may say "grade III" for
the same thing.

**A grade now belongs to a type of tumor.** Grades are given within a tumor
type, not across all of them. A grade 3 of one type and a grade 3 of another are
not the same thing, so putting them side by side does not tell you much.

There is one more thing worth knowing. A grade used to come only from what the
cells looked like. Now a gene result can set the grade on its own, even when the
cells under the microscope look lower grade. If your grade does not seem to
match what you were told about the cells, that is the likely reason, and it is a
fair thing to ask about.

## What NOS and NEC mean

Two short codes can turn up after the name of your tumor. Both mean the same
plain thing: the name could not be finished in the standard way. Neither is a
grade, and neither of them says the tumor is worse.

**NOS** is short for "not otherwise specified". It means a test that was needed
for the standard name is missing. It may not have been done yet, or it may have
failed and need repeating. It is a flag to your team that something is
outstanding.

**NEC** is short for "not elsewhere classified". It means the testing was done
and it worked, but the results do not add up to one of the standard names. The
pathologist then describes the tumor in their own words instead.

Both are doing a useful job, because they tell your team exactly where the
answer has got to. If you see either one, ask what is missing, or what did not
fit, and whether anything more can be done about it.

## What you can do with your copy

- **Ask for a copy, and keep it somewhere you can find it.** You will be asked
  for it more than once.
- **Write the name down with all of its words.** "Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS
  WHO grade 3" is a different search from "astrocytoma", and the extra words
  are the ones that matter.
- **Check the date and the report number** before you read it, so you know which
  report you are holding.
- **Ask which parts are still to come.** The comment usually says so, but asking
  is quicker.
- **Take it to appointments, and ask for it to be read through with you line by
  line.** That is a normal thing to ask for.
- **Look up the whole name, not single words from it.** One word out of the gene
  results will bring back papers written for doctors.
  [The glossary](/glossary) explains the words on the report in plain English.

## Who else sees this report?

The pathologist sends the report to the doctor who ordered it, usually your
surgeon or the specialist looking after you, and that doctor is the one who goes
through it with you. Everyone on your care team can see it.

Your report may also be discussed at a tumor board before your treatment is
settled. [Waiting for your pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results#who-reads-it-and-how-do-i-get-the-result)
explains what that meeting is, and who is in the room.

There is one more thing to know if you are in the United States. Your report can
appear in your online records before anyone has phoned you about it. That same
page covers what to do about that, and it is worth deciding in advance whether
you want to open it on your own.

## What to ask your team

- Is this the final report, or is something still to come?
- Would you read the report through with me, line by line?
- What is the full name of my tumor, with every word?
- What grade is it, and what set the grade?
- Does my report follow the 2021 naming rules?
- Does my report say NOS or NEC, and if it does, what is missing?
- Which gene tests were run, and are any of them still pending?
- Has the name changed since the last time we spoke?
- Can I have a copy of the report?
- Do any of these results open up a treatment or a trial I should know about?
