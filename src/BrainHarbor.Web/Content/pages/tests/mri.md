## The short version

An MRI takes very detailed pictures of your brain. It uses a big magnet and
radio waves. There is no radiation, and the scan itself does not hurt. It is
loud, and you have to lie still. Most people also get a dye put into a vein
part way through. You will have this scan more than once over time. That is
normal, and it is how your team keeps track of what is going on.

## What is an MRI?

An MRI is a scan that makes detailed pictures of the inside of your head. It
uses a strong magnet, radio waves and a computer. It does not use x-rays or any
other radiation.

For brain tumors it is the best scan there is, and it is usually the first one
done.

A scan can show where a growth is, how big it is, and what it is doing to the
tissue around it. It can often give your team a good idea of what type it is.
What it cannot do is give you the name for certain. Only a piece of the tumor
itself, looked at in a lab, can do that. If someone tells you they need tissue
before they can say more, this is why.

## Why am I having this scan?

There are three jobs, and the same machine does all three.

- **To find out what is going on.** This is the scan that shows the growth in
  the first place.
- **To plan.** Before surgery or radiation, your team needs a very exact
  picture of where it is.
- **To keep watch.** After treatment, scans are compared with each other over
  time to see what has changed.

The room, the machine and the noise are the same every time. What changes is
what your team is looking for.

## What happens, step by step

1. **The safety form.** Someone asks you a long list of questions about metal,
   devices and past surgery. Answer all of it, even the parts that seem silly.
   This is the step that keeps you safe.
2. **Getting changed.** You are usually given a gown. You take off jewelry, your
   watch, hearing aids, hair clips, coins, keys and anything with a magnetic
   strip.
3. **The line in your arm.** If you are having dye, a nurse or technologist puts
   a thin tube into a vein in your arm or hand with a small needle, the same as
   for a blood test.
4. **Lying down.** You lie on a padded table. A frame goes around your head. It
   looks like a cage or a helmet, and it is what picks up the pictures.
5. **Ear protection.** You are given earplugs or headphones. You need them.
6. **Into the scanner.** The table slides you into a tube that is open at both
   ends. You are alone in the room, but the staff watch you the whole time. They
   can talk to you, you can talk back, and you are given a button to press.
7. **The dye.** If you are having it, it goes in through the tube in your arm
   part way through. Sometimes a machine does it. Sometimes someone comes in and
   slides the table out to do it by hand.
8. **Afterwards.** The staff may check the pictures before you get dressed, in
   case they need more. Then you can go, and you can eat, drink and get on with
   your day as normal.

Unless you are told otherwise, take your usual medicines that day. Rules about
eating and drinking before a scan are different at different places, so go by
what your center tells you.

## How long does it take?

Most MRI scans take somewhere between 15 and 45 minutes. Some take an hour or
more. If you are having dye, it takes longer, because two sets of pictures are
taken: one before the dye and one after.

The person running the scan can tell you before you start how long yours should
take.

You do not have to lie perfectly still for the whole hour. You have to be still
while each set of pictures is being taken. Between sets you can often move a
little. Ask about that before you go in. Knowing you get breaks makes the whole
thing easier.

## What does it feel like?

Loud, mostly. The scanner bangs, knocks and buzzes while it works. The noise is
normal and it does not mean anything is wrong. That is what the earplugs are
for.

The scan itself does not hurt. You lie on your back on a padded table and stay
still. Some people find it boring. Some people find it long. Neither is a sign
that anything has gone wrong.

If you are having dye, the needle for the line in your arm feels like having
blood taken.

## If small spaces frighten you

Say so, early. This is a normal thing to be frightened of, and there is a lot
your center can do about it. What there is not, is any need to get through it on
your own.

- **You can ask your doctor for a mild calming medicine** before the scan. Ask
  in advance, not on the day. If you take one, arrange a lift home, and check
  with the staff about driving.
- **Some centers let a person you know come into the room with you**, or let you
  bring something of your own in. It helps more than people expect.
- **Ask about music.** You are given headphones anyway, so it is worth asking
  whether they can play something through them.
- **Some centers have more than one machine**, and one of them may be less
  closed in. Ask what they have. Machines differ in what they can show, so let
  the center tell you which of theirs suits your scan.
- **Knowing exactly what will happen helps.** That is most of what this page is
  for. Ask the staff to walk you through it first.

When people are prepared and know what to expect, it is almost always possible
to get the scan finished. Tell them you are worried. They have seen it many
times and they would much rather know.

## The dye: what people ask about it

The dye is a liquid called gadolinium. It goes into a vein and makes some
tissue stand out, which helps show a tumor more clearly.

**Is it the same as the dye for a CT scan?** No. It has no iodine in it, and it
only rarely causes a reaction of any kind.

**Who needs to speak up first?** Tell the staff before the scan if you have
kidney disease, kidney failure, a kidney transplant or liver disease, or if you
have ever reacted to this dye before.

**Does it stay in my body?** Small amounts can stay after many scans. There are
no known health effects from this. It is most likely in people who have a lot of
scans over their lifetime, which includes people with a brain tumor. It is a
fair thing to ask your team about, and it is worth asking once so it stops
sitting at the back of your mind.

**What if I am pregnant, or might be?** Tell them at the safety form. There is
no known risk to a baby from the scan itself, and MRI is usually safer than an
x-ray or a CT scan. Most centers avoid the dye during pregnancy to be careful.

**What if I am breastfeeding?** Tell the clinic when you book. Doctors take it
as safe to carry on feeding after the dye. Some parents choose to pump some milk
beforehand and use it for the next day instead. Either choice is fine, and the
clinic can talk it through with you.

## Metal, implants and your device card

If you have a device fitted, bring its card. That one thing solves most of it.

Many newer devices are fine in an MRI, including some pacemakers, heart
defibrillators, nerve stimulators, cochlear implants and medicine pumps. What
the staff need is the exact make and model, which is what the card gives them.
Without it they may have to be cautious for no reason.

A few things do stop a scan. Some clips used on blood vessels in the brain are
made of metal that a magnet pulls on, so the team checks which kind you have.
Metal near the eye has to be checked first too, and that is more likely if you
have worked with metal.

Some other things to know:

- Clothes with metal threads in them, including some sports clothes, can heat up
  and burn your skin. A gown avoids the whole problem, which is why you are
  usually given one.
- Tattoos can sometimes sting or feel hot. Tell the staff if you have them.
- Metal in your body does not always stop the scan, but it can smudge the
  pictures. If the staff know it is there, they can work around it.

If a center tells you it cannot scan you, ask why, and ask what else would show
the same thing. It is also fair to ask another center. Some have machines or
staff that can handle devices others cannot.

## Why am I having another scan?

Two scans catch almost everyone out. Both are normal, and neither means
something has gone wrong.

### The scan just before surgery

This one is a map, not a repeat.

A scan can be done before an operation to map exactly where the tumor sits,
which matters most when it is deep in the brain. Your team may call it the
navigation scan. For some procedures small markers are stuck on your scalp
first, or the shape of your own face is used instead, so the pictures can be
lined up with your real head in the room.

So being sent for another MRI days after the last one is not a sign that the
first was wrong or that something has changed. It is the surgeon getting the map
they work from.

### The scan just after surgery

This one is usually done within a day or two, and it is booked that soon on
purpose.

It does two jobs. It shows how much of the tumor was taken out. It also becomes
the picture that every later scan gets compared with, which is why it matters so
much.

The reason for the rush is simple. Healing itself shows up on a scan, and the
longer you wait, the more of it there is to see and the harder it is to tell
healing from tumor. Getting the scan early gives your team a clean picture to
measure everything else against.

## Who reads my scan, and how do I get the result?

How long it takes depends on where you are, so ask before you leave. What
follows is who does what, so you know what the wait is made of.

A radiologist reads the pictures. That is a doctor trained to read scans. They
send a signed report to the doctor who ordered it, and that doctor tells you.

For a brain tumor, your scan may also go to a tumor board. That is a meeting
where specialists look at your case together: surgeons, cancer doctors, the lab,
and the people who read scans. It tends to happen when a case is complicated or
a scan is hard to read. You do not go to the meeting. Afterwards the doctor who
knows you best explains what was said.

What they decide is advice, not an order. The choice is still yours.

Because you are not in the room, tell your team what matters to you before the
meeting happens, so it gets taken into account.

The wait is the part most people find hardest. You can shorten the not-knowing a
little by asking, before you leave the scan: when should the result be ready,
and who is going to call me?

## What to ask your team

- Is this scan with dye, without, or both, and why?
- I have this device. Here is the card. Is it safe, and does anything need
  changing first?
- I get very anxious in small spaces. What can you offer me?
- If I have a calming medicine, can I drive myself home?
- How long will the scan take, and how long do I need to stay still at a time?
- Can someone come in the room with me?
- When will the result be ready, and who will call me?
- Will my scan go to a tumor board before I hear anything?
- Can I get a copy of my pictures, for my own records or for a second opinion?
