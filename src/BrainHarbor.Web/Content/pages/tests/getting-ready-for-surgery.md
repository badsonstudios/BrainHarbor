## The short version {#short-version}

Before a brain operation you have a set of checks. Blood tests, a visit with the
doctor who puts you to sleep, and sometimes a heart trace or a chest x-ray. The
checks are picked for you, so your list may be shorter than somebody else's. Two
things here are worth reading today. **Your hospital gives you its own times for
stopping food and drink, and those are the ones to follow.** Within that, you
may not have to go all night without a drink of water, and it is worth asking.
And you must not stop a blood thinner on your own.

## What is it? {#what-it-is}

!%anesthesiologist%!%advance directive%It is a set of checks that make sure it
is safe to put you to sleep and operate on you.

Your team may call it your pre-op appointment, your pre-operative assessment, or
your clearance. They all mean the same thing.

Most of it is talking. Somebody goes through your health history with you, looks
at your notes, and examines you. The tests around that conversation are there to
fill in the parts your history cannot answer.

The doctor at the middle of it is an anesthesiologist. That is the doctor who
puts you to sleep for the operation and looks after you while you are under.

## Why am I having all this? {#why}

An anesthetic and a brain operation both ask a lot of your heart and your lungs.
Your team wants to know how yours are doing before the day rather than on it.

**There is no fixed list of tests.** They are chosen from your own health
history and from the operation you are having. Somebody with a heart condition
gets checks that somebody without one does not need. Your age on its own may not
be a reason for a heart trace.

So if a friend had a test you have not been offered, that is usually not
something going wrong. It is a fair thing to put to your team: *why that one,
and why not this one?*

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

For an operation like this one, the anesthesia check is meant to happen **before
the day of surgery**, not on the morning.

1. **They read your notes.** Your records go to the team before you get there.
2. **They ask you questions.** Your health, the operations you have had, your
   allergies, everything you take, and how you have been with anesthetics
   before.
3. **They examine you.** A short exam: the way into your lungs, your lungs
   themselves, your heart, and your blood pressure and pulse.
4. **Blood tests, if there is a reason.** These are rarely about the tumor. They
   may be done to check how well your liver, your kidneys and some other organs
   are working.
5. **A heart trace, if there is a reason.** Sticky sensors go on your arms, your
   legs and your chest, wired to a machine that records the signals your heart
   makes as it beats. It is called an electrocardiogram, and on your paperwork it
   may be written ECG or EKG. You may have one if your team has something about
   your heart they want to look at.
6. **A chest x-ray, if there is a reason.** It looks at your lungs, your heart
   and your chest wall. It is not automatic, and things like smoking, a recent
   cold or a long-term lung condition are among what your team weighs up. It
   uses a very small dose of radiation, which is the reason it is not given to
   everybody as a matter of course.
7. **The talk about your medicines.** This is the reason to bring your list, and
   it has its own section below.
8. **Your instructions.** You are given what to do and what not to do in the day
   or two before. Ask for it in writing.

Then, on the day itself, you change into a gown, take off jewelry and contact
lenses, and have an IV line put into a vein for fluids and medicines. You meet
the anesthesiologist, who goes through your worries and any allergies. You meet
your surgeon, who goes through the plan and the risks, and answers any
last-minute questions.

## When does all of it happen? {#when}

Ask your own team about the dates, because this part depends on how your
hospital runs. What follows is the shape of it.

- **The pre-op appointment:** a separate visit, before the day of surgery. Ask
  when yours is, and ask how long to allow for it.
- **A heart trace:** a few minutes.
- **A chest x-ray:** the whole visit is usually done inside about fifteen
  minutes.
- **Your instructions:** they cover roughly the day or two before the operation.
- **The scan the surgeon navigates by:** its own appointment.
  [The scan just before surgery](/tests/mri#navigation-scan) goes through it.

Hospitals do this differently, and the instructions you are given are the ones
that count. If anything on this page does not match your sheet, follow your
sheet and ask about the difference.

## Will any of it hurt? {#does-it-hurt}

Almost none of it.

- **Blood tests** are a needle in your arm. Of the checks in the list above,
  that is the only needle.
- **A heart trace** does not hurt. The sensors stick to your skin and the
  machine only listens. No electricity goes into you. The skin may be a little
  sore where the stickers come off.
- **A chest x-ray** does not hurt either. You stand with your chest against a
  plate, hold still and hold your breath for a few seconds.

## The hard parts, and what helps {#hard-parts}

**Worrying that a test will stop the operation.** That fear is a normal one, and
it is worth saying out loud rather than carrying it home. Ask at the appointment
what happens if a result needs looking at first, who calls you, and how quickly.

**Telling your story more than once.** You will be asked some of the same
questions by different people, on different days. Bring your written list and
your medicine list, so you are reading rather than remembering.

**Going without food and water for longer than you needed to.** It happens a
lot, it is worth asking about, and the next section is about it.

**Feeling like paperwork while you are frightened.** Getting ready for surgery
turns something enormous into a list of appointments, and that can be a strange
and lonely few weeks. If it is sitting on you, there are people you can say it
to today. [Get help now](/get-help-now) has the numbers.

## When do I stop eating and drinking? {#fasting}

**Your hospital gives you your own times, and those are the ones to follow.**
Nothing here replaces them.

But it is worth asking about them, because the times people are given are often
longer than the guideline asks for.

The guideline anesthesia teams work to says **clear drinks can go up to two
hours before you are put to sleep**. Food needs longer, about six hours. Clear
drinks means water, and other drinks you can see through, with no milk in them
and no alcohol.

The reason for the difference is simple. A clear drink leaves the stomach fast,
and about nine tenths of one is gone from the stomach inside the first hour.
Food has to be broken up first, so it sits there much longer.

**That guideline is written for a planned operation, in people whose stomachs
empty normally.** Some conditions and some medicines slow that down, and the
weight-loss and diabetes injections a lot of people are on now are the ones most
talked about. If any of that is you, your own time may genuinely be longer, and
it is not your hospital being careless. Say what you take and let them decide.

What happens in practice is a long way from the guideline. In a study across
forty-six centers in twelve European countries, the middle patient had gone
**twelve hours** without a clear drink before their anesthetic, and fewer than
one in a hundred had a drink in the last two hours.

Going that long dry is not harmless. It leaves people thirsty, dry-mouthed,
hungry, restless and anxious, and if you drink coffee you can get a withdrawal
headache on top of it.

So ask, plainly: **how late can I drink water?** If the answer is nothing after
midnight, it is fair to ask whether that is the time for you or the one they
give everybody. Get the answer in writing, and then follow it exactly.

## Which of my medicines do I keep taking? {#medicines}

Some of what you take will carry on, and some of it will stop. Which is which is
the whole point of this conversation, so do not assume either way about any of
it until somebody has told you.

**Bring the whole list.** Prescriptions, anything you buy for yourself, vitamins
and herbal things, with the doses. The things people leave off a list are
usually the ones bought off a shelf, and those count too.

**Your seizure medicine.** For somebody who has seizures, the usual plan is to
keep the medicine as close to normal as it can be around the operation, and to
get back onto it as soon as possible afterwards. On the morning you may be told
to take your dose with a sip of water. Ask whether that is your plan, because
there are reasons a team sometimes wants it done differently.

**Your steroid.** If you are on dexamethasone or another steroid, this is not a
medicine to stop on your own. A steroid is normally brought down in steps rather
than stopped, and stopping one suddenly can make you seriously ill. Your team
tells you what to do with it around the operation.
[A craniotomy, step by step](/treatments/craniotomy) covers what happens to it
afterwards.

## Blood thinners: do not stop one on your own {#blood-thinners}

!%blood thinner%If you take a medicine that makes it harder for your blood to
clot, **that decision is not yours to make alone, and it is not one to make off
anything you read, including this page.** People call these medicines blood
thinners. Aspirin is one, and there are several others.

It is usually settled between the doctor who prescribed it and your surgeon,
with the anesthesiologist in the conversation and with you in it too. Ask who is
deciding, because each of them can assume somebody else has done it.

Some of these medicines are stopped some days before an operation. **It is not
the same number of days for every one**, and for some of them it is around a
week. That is why this conversation needs to happen early rather than the night
before.

Some people are told to carry on taking theirs. Aspirin is the clearest example:
whether it stops depends on why you are on it in the first place. Somebody
taking it after a heart attack or a stroke, or because they have a stent, may
well be told to keep going. That is a decision about that person, not a rule you
can apply to yourself.

What to do with all of that:

- Ask for the **exact date** you stop each one, and get it in writing.
- Ask who is deciding, and get a name.
- If nobody has raised it and your operation is close, phone and ask. It is a
  normal thing to chase.

## What to bring, and what to leave at home {#what-to-bring}

The operation page has the list for the day you are admitted. This is the
shorter one, for the appointment before it.

**To the pre-op appointment, bring:**

- Your medicine list, with the doses.
- Anything you have been given in writing already, including the letter with
  your operation date on it.
- Your advance directive, if you have one. That is a living will, or the form
  naming the person who speaks for you if you cannot speak for yourself.

**On the day of the operation**, you will be asked to take off jewelry and
contact lenses, so leave the jewelry at home and bring your glasses and a lens
case. [A craniotomy, step by step](/treatments/craniotomy) covers the rest of
what to pack.

## For the person going with them {#caregiver}

[CAREGIVER]

Before the operation, there are four jobs here that are genuinely yours, and
none of them are the ones people expect.

**The medicine list.** Write it out before the appointment. Everything, with
doses, including whatever they buy for themselves. This is the single most
useful piece of paper either of you will bring, and it is much easier to do at
the kitchen table than from memory in a clinic.

**The blood thinner, if there is one.** Make sure it has actually been settled,
and not just mentioned. Ask for the date they stop it and who decided, and ask
in front of them so you both hear the same answer. **Do not let anybody stop one
on the strength of something they read.** That includes this page.

**The eating and drinking times.** Get them in writing and put them somewhere
you can both see them. Then ask the question in the section above: how late can
they drink water? Somebody has to ask it, and it is easier for you than for
them.

**Ask what to do if they get ill before the day.** A cold or a temperature in
the week before is a normal thing to happen and a normal thing to ask about.
Find out now who you would call.

What you are **not** doing yet is the aftercare. That is a different and much
bigger job, and it starts on the other side.
[A craniotomy, step by step](/treatments/craniotomy) has the section for it, and
it is worth reading before the day rather than on the ward.

## Who reads all of this, and what if something is not right? {#results}

The results go to the anesthesiologist. That is the doctor whose job it is to
check that an anesthetic is safe for you, and it is the reason the appointment
exists at all. Your surgeon sees them too.

Timings vary from hospital to hospital, so the honest answer to *when will I
hear* is to ask before you leave. Ask it as two questions: who calls me if
something needs following up, and what number do I call if I think of something
before the day?

Hearing nothing is not a signal on its own. Ask whether you should expect to
hear anything before the day, so you know what silence means and are not sitting
at home reading something into it.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- When exactly do I stop eating, and when do I stop drinking?
- Can I have water closer to the time than the sheet says?
- What do I do if I eat or drink something by mistake?
- Which of my medicines do I stop, and on which date exactly?
- Do I keep taking my seizure medicine and my steroid?
- Who decides about my blood thinner, you or the doctor who prescribed it?
- Do I need a heart trace or a chest x-ray, and why?
- Which blood tests am I having?
- When is my pre-op appointment, and how long should I allow for it?
- What time do I arrive on the day, and who can stay with me?
- What happens if a result needs following up first?
- What do I do if I get a cold or a fever in the days before?
