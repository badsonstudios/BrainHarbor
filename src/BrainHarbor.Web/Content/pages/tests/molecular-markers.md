## The short version

Your report may carry short words like IDH, MGMT or 1p/19q. They are results of
tests done on the tumor tissue. This page says what each one measures and what
your team uses it for. It does not say what your own result means. That answer
belongs to your team, who can see your whole report and know the rest of your
story.

## What these tests are

The lab tests the tumor tissue itself.

Genes are small pieces of DNA. They are the instructions inside cells. The lab
looks for changes in those instructions in the tumor cells, and for other marks
it can stain for.

Under the rules brought in for 2021, many of these results are part of the name
of the tumor. They are not extra notes at the end. That is why the name on a
report can gain words while you wait.

So these are labels on the tumor. They say what the lab found. Reading them is
not the same as knowing what will happen to you.

## Are these results passed on to my children?

No. The lab looked at the tumor, not at you.

Changes found this way are called somatic. They happened in the tumor cells
during your life. The National Cancer Institute puts it plainly: somatic changes
cause most cancers and cannot be passed on to family members.

This kind of testing has its own name. Across cancer groups it is called
biomarker testing, and the agreed meaning is testing for markers that are **not**
inherited. Looking for something that runs in a family is a different test.

Once in a while a tumor test does turn up a change a person was born with. If
that happens your team would tell you, and they would offer you a second test and
someone to talk it through with. It is not something that gets decided without
you.

## Where these words sit on your report

They usually sit in their own part of the report, under a heading about gene or
molecular results. Sometimes they arrive as a separate report of their own, days
or weeks after the first one.

[Your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report#your-report-part-by-part)
walks through the whole document and where each part sits.

### IDH {#idh}

**What is measured.** Whether the tumor cells carry a change in one of two genes,
IDH1 or IDH2. A stain finds the most common single change. If the stain shows
nothing, the lab reads the gene itself, because rarer changes do not show up on
the stain.

**What your team does with it.** It has to be tested on every diffuse glioma, and
it is usually the first answer they wait for. The result becomes part of the name
of the tumor.

**What it does not tell you.** The words mutant and wildtype are names, not
scores. They tell you which set of naming rules your tumor falls under, and
nothing on their own about you.

### 1p/19q co-deletion {#p-1p-19q}

**What is measured.** Chromosomes are the packages DNA is stored in. This test
asks whether the tumor cells have lost one whole arm of chromosome 1 and one
whole arm of chromosome 19, both together.

**What your team does with it.** In a glioma that also has an IDH change, this is
the finding that makes the tumor an oligodendroglioma. The name follows the test.

**What it does not tell you.** Whole arms are the point. A test that finds only
part of an arm missing is a different result, and your report may say so. Some
methods can only see part of an arm, which is why the lab may repeat it a
different way.

### ATRX {#atrx}

**What is measured.** Whether the tumor cells still make the ATRX protein. A
stain shows it as kept or lost. The gene can also be read directly.

**What your team does with it.** It helps tell one kind of glioma from another.
In oligodendroglioma the protein is usually kept. In astrocytoma it is often
lost.

**What it does not tell you.** By itself it names nothing. It is one of the
findings your team lines up beside the IDH result.

### TERT promoter {#tert}

**What is measured.** A promoter is the switch that turns a gene on. This test
asks whether the switch for the TERT gene carries a change.

**What your team does with it.** When the IDH test shows no change, this is one
of three findings that can point to a glioblastoma even when the cells look
like a lower grade under the microscope. In a tumor that does have an IDH
change, it is read differently.

**What it does not tell you.** It turns up in more than one kind of tumor, so on
its own it does not name one.

### CDKN2A/B {#cdkn2a-b}

**What is measured.** Whether both copies of two neighboring genes are gone.
Everyone has two copies of most genes. Losing both is written homozygous
deletion.

**What your team does with it.** In some gliomas this finding sets the grade on
its own, even when the cells look like a lower grade.

**What it does not tell you.** A grade is a description of the tumor, not a
countdown. [What the grade means](/tests/pathology-report#what-the-grade-means)
explains how grades are worked out and why the numbering changed in 2021.

### EGFR {#egfr}

**What is measured.** Whether the tumor cells carry extra copies of the EGFR
gene. Extra copies are called amplification.

**What your team does with it.** When the IDH test shows no change, this is the
second of three findings that can point to a glioblastoma even when the cells
look like a lower grade.

**What it does not tell you.** Extra copies in tumor cells are not a change in
the genes you were born with. They happened in the tumor.

### Chromosome 7 and chromosome 10 {#chromosome-7-and-10}

**What is measured.** Whether the tumor cells have gained a whole copy of
chromosome 7 and lost a whole copy of chromosome 10. It is often written +7/-10.

**What your team does with it.** When the IDH test shows no change, this is the
third of three findings that can point to a glioblastoma even when the cells
look like a lower grade.

**What it does not tell you.** This is a count of whole chromosomes rather than a
test of one gene, so it will not show up in a list of genes.

### H3 K27-altered {#h3-k27}

**What is measured.** Histones are the spools that DNA winds around. The lab
looks for the loss of a chemical mark on one of them, or for a change in the gene
that makes it. The change is written K27M.

**What your team does with it.** In a diffuse glioma sitting in the middle of the
brain or the spine, this finding names it a diffuse midline glioma.

**What it does not tell you.** You may see this written two ways. Older reports
say H3 K27M, after the one change. The 2021 rules say H3 K27-altered, because
more than one route leads to the same place. Both may be describing the same
tumor.

### H3 G34 {#h3-g34}

**What is measured.** A change at a different spot on the same kind of histone
gene, written G34.

**What your team does with it.** It names a diffuse hemispheric glioma. It is
usually seen along with a loss of ATRX and a p53 stain that lights up.

**What it does not tell you.** It is a separate finding from H3 K27, not a
version of it. The two point to different tumors that sit in different places.

### BRAF {#braf}

**What is measured.** Two different things can be looked for. One is a single
change written V600E. The other is BRAF joined onto a neighboring gene, which is
called a fusion.

**What your team does with it.** It helps name some tumors. There are also drugs
made to act on BRAF changes, so your team may test for it with treatment in mind.

**What it does not tell you.** Finding a change does not mean a drug is right for
you. Whether one is an option depends on the whole picture, and it is a
conversation to have with your team.

### MGMT promoter methylation {#mgmt}

**What is measured.** MGMT is a gene. Its protein repairs one kind of damage that
some chemotherapy drugs do to DNA. Methylation is a chemical tag that can quiet
the switch that turns a gene on. The lab measures how many places on that switch
carry the tag, so the answer is a quantity rather than a plain yes or no.

**What your team does with it.** Most of the results on this page are used to
name the tumor. This one is different. They use it as one input when they are
choosing chemotherapy for a glioblastoma.

**What it does not tell you.** The line between methylated and unmethylated is
not settled. Labs use different tests and different cut-off points, and when two
of those tests disagree about the same sample it is not known which one to go
with. The result is also only used this way for one kind of tumor. So it is
something your team weighs up rather than an answer on its own, and what yours
means is a question for them.

### Ki-67 {#ki-67}

**What is measured.** A stain that marks cells that were dividing at the moment
the sample was taken. It is reported as the share of tumor cells that took up the
stain, written as a percentage. You may see it called MIB-1.

**What your team does with it.** They read it next to everything else on the
report.

**What it does not tell you.** There is no agreed way to count it. Nobody has
settled which cells to count or which part of the slide to pick, so the same
slide can be scored differently by different people. A small difference between
one report and another is not something to read anything into.

### TP53 {#tp53}

**What is measured.** Whether the TP53 gene in the tumor carries a change. A
stain for the protein it makes is sometimes used instead.

**What your team does with it.** It is one of the findings that fits with an
astrocytoma that also has an IDH change.

**What it does not tell you.** Finding it in the tumor is not the same as
inheriting it. Changes you were born with are looked for by a different test, and
your team would talk to you first.

### Gene panels {#gene-panel}

**What is measured.** One test that reads many genes at once from the tumor
sample. Panels that read DNA find small changes and missing or extra copies.
Panels that read RNA are better at finding two genes joined together.

**What your team does with it.** One test instead of several, with the results
arriving as a list.

**What it does not tell you.** The report lists every gene the test looked at,
not only the ones that had changed. A long list is a list of what was checked.

### Methylation profiling {#methylation-profiling}

**What is measured.** The same kind of chemical tag as the one in the MGMT test,
but read right across the tumor's DNA, at about 850,000 places at once. The
pattern is then compared with a library of thousands of brain tumors that have
already been named.

**What your team does with it.** It helps place a tumor with a type. The report
comes back with a score for how closely the pattern matched. The same test also
shows whole chromosomes that have been gained or lost.

**What it does not tell you.** A low score means the pattern did not match any
group closely enough to call it. It is a statement about the match, not a finding
about the tumor.

## How long do these take?

Longer than the part that comes from the microscope. For most of them it is
weeks rather than days. That is why a report can turn up in pieces, and why the
first version of yours may say that some results are still to come.

[Waiting for your pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the whole
wait: what is being done to your tissue, why some of it takes weeks, and what you
can do in the meantime.

## Who explains my result?

Your own team. This page says what each test measures. It cannot say what your
result means, because that depends on the rest of your report and on you.

The doctor who ordered the tests is the one who goes through them with you. If a
word on your report does not match a word here, ask about it. Reports are written
for doctors, and the same finding can be written more than one way from one lab
to the next.

## What to ask your team

- Which of these tests were run on my tumor?
- Are any of them still to come?
- Did any of the results change the name of my tumor?
- Is anything on my report marked as pending, failed or not tested?
- Was any of my testing sent to another lab?
- If a result is unclear, what happens next?
- Do any of these results open up a treatment or a
  [trial](/treatments/clinical-trials) I should know about?
- Would you go through the gene results with me line by line?
