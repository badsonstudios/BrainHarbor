## The short version

**Chordoma is a rare bone cancer.** It grows in the bones down the middle of the
body: the base of the skull, the spine, and the tailbone.

**It starts from cells left over from before you were born.** Those cells helped
build your spine. In most people they disappear. In a few people some stay, and
very rarely one turns into this tumor.

**It usually grows slowly.** That is why it is often found late. Unless your team
has told you that something is urgent, it is also why most people have time to
get to the right team before deciding anything.

**The first treatment matters more than it would for most tumors.** Getting to a
center that treats chordoma, **before** a biopsy, changes what is possible later.

Some signs mean getting help the same day, and a few mean going in now. They
have their own section below.

## What is a chordoma?

**It is cancer.** The word written on your paperwork will be malignant. Cleveland
Clinic puts it plainly: every kind of chordoma counts as cancer.

**It belongs to a family called sarcoma.** Those are cancers that start in bone
or the soft tissue around it, rather than in an organ.

**It starts from the notochord.** Before you were born, a soft rod ran down the
middle of your body. It helped your spine form. It normally disappears well
before birth. In a small number of people a few of those cells stay behind, in
the bones of the skull base or the spine.

**That is why this tumor sits in the middle.** The rod was the midline, so its
leftovers are in the midline, so the tumor is too. Almost everything else on this
page follows from that one fact.

**It is rare.** About 300 people are told they have one each year in the United
States, which works out at about one person in a million.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes, it is cancer. And there is no simple grade number for it.**

That surprises people, because grade is the first thing many readers look for.
Here is the honest picture.

**The book that names brain and spine tumors lists chordoma and prints no grade
beside it.** That book is the WHO classification. If your report says WHO, it
means the same book. It sorts this tumor into a family called notochordal
tumors. It does not give it a 1, 2, 3 or 4 the way it does for many others.

**Clinical references call it low grade.** That phrase describes the appearance
of the cells under a microscope, and nothing else. Cells resembling normal ones
earn that label.

**Low grade here does not mean mild, and this is the part that matters.** The
same references that call it low grade say in the same sentence that it grows
into the bone and tissue right beside it, and that it often comes back. Slow is
not the same as harmless.

**So grade is not the useful number on your report.** What your team actually
works from is where it sits, how much of it can be taken out, and what is next to
it. Those come further down.

**If you see a Roman numeral on older paperwork**, that is only an older way of
writing a grade. The rules changed in 2021 and ordinary numbers are used now.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**Three places, all down the middle.** The base of the skull, the spine, and the
sacrum, which is the bone at the bottom of your spine behind your hips.

**About a third start at the skull base, about a third at the tailbone end, and
the rest in the spine in between.** Different studies count these a little
differently. Treat them as rough shares.

**Your symptoms come from what the tumor is pressed against, not from the tumor
itself.** That is the whole of it. A tumor this size in a roomy place might cause
nothing for years. The same tumor against a nerve causes trouble early.

**Looking it up by place instead of by name.** [Where your tumor is, and what
that changes](/where-your-tumor-is) starts from the spot rather than the
diagnosis. Two parts of it are written for the head: its list of places, and the
signs it says to call about. It says so both times. The rest of it works wherever
your tumor is. If yours is at the base of your skull, the entry called the floor
of the skull is the one that names the word your report may use.

### At the base of the skull

**The bone it grows in sits in a crowded place.** It is called the clivus. It
lies in front of the brain stem and behind the back of your throat. The nerves
that move your eyes, carry feeling to your face, and run your voice and
swallowing all pass close by.

**Double vision is often the first thing people notice, and there is a reason it
is that one.** One of the nerves that moves the eye runs a long way along the
surface of that exact bone before it gets where it is going. A long exposed run
means it is often the first to be squeezed. That nerve turns one eye outward, so
when it stops working properly you see two of things, usually worse when you look
to the side.

**Other things come from the same crowd of nerves**: headache, numbness or pain
in the face, changes in hearing, a voice that changes, and trouble swallowing. A
blocked or stuffy nose that does not clear up can be part of it too.

**Sometimes it blocks the fluid around the brain.** The brain sits in a watery
fluid that flows past it and drains away. A tumor sitting across that path can
block the drain, and the pressure inside your head then climbs. That can bring
headaches, changes in how you think or behave, and trouble focusing your eyes.

### In the spine and at the tailbone

**Lower down, the tumor presses on the cord and on the nerve roots coming off
it.** That brings pain in the back or neck, pain running down a leg, numbness or
tingling, and weakness.

**At the tailbone end, the nerves it meets are the ones that run your bladder,
your bowel, and sexual function.** So the symptoms there are different, and they
are ones people find hard to raise: trouble peeing or pooping, numbness through
the area you sit on, and changes in sex. Sometimes a lump can be felt.

**Tumors down there are often quiet until they are large**, because there is more
room before anything gets pressed.

## What symptoms does it cause?

**They are rarely obvious, and that is not your fault.** One reference says
plainly that the symptoms of chordoma are not specific. They look like ordinary
problems: back pain, a stuffy nose, vision that is a bit off.

The ones people most often notice:

- **Double vision**, or other changes in how your eyes work together.
- **Headache**, often felt at the back of the head or behind an eye.
- **Numbness or pain in the face.**
- **Changes in hearing, voice, or swallowing.**
- **A blocked nose that does not clear.**
- **Pain in the back or neck**, or pain running down a leg.
- **Numbness, tingling, or weakness** in an arm or a leg.
- **Trouble controlling peeing or pooping**, or numbness where you sit.

**Many people are unwell for months before anyone lands on the answer.** That is
normal for this tumor and it is written about openly. It is slow, it is rare, and
its early signs belong to a dozen more common things.

**Something being slow is not a reason to leave it.** If any of these are new, or
are getting worse, the Chordoma Foundation's advice is simply to tell your
doctors.

### The chordoma signs that mean getting help now {#getting-help-now}

**Where this advice comes from is worth knowing before you read it.** We looked
through the chordoma organizations, and the chordoma pages on the big hospital
sites, and **not one page about this tumor says when to head for an emergency
room**. So nothing below is a chordoma rule. Each one comes from a page about the
warning sign itself, and each is credited to the people who wrote it.

**New weakness in your legs, numbness through your legs or belly, or losing
control of peeing or pooping is an emergency.** Call 911, or get to the closest
emergency room. Pressure on the spinal cord is treated as an emergency in cancer
care, and the American Cancer Society warns that without fast treatment a person
can be left paralyzed. Of everything here, this is where waiting costs most.

**The same signs at the tailbone end have their own name, and the same answer.**
Doctors call it cauda equina syndrome, and it is worth saying that name out loud
if you ever have to describe this at a front desk. When the nerves at the bottom
of the spine are squeezed, Cleveland Clinic says to
visit an emergency room immediately. Getting treated sooner lowers the chance of
lasting trouble. More than one in four people with a tumor in the
sacrum have either muscle weakness or this nerve picture, so it is not a rare
thing to warn about.

**Double vision that comes on suddenly is different from double vision that
creeps in.** Cleveland Clinic says to go to an emergency room for double vision
that does not settle within a few hours. The same goes for double vision that
comes with eye pain, dizziness, weakness, slurred speech or confusion. With this
tumor the slow pattern is the usual one, and that means phoning your team
instead. Both halves are true, and
they are not the same situation.

**Signs of rising pressure inside the head are an emergency in their own right.**
Cleveland Clinic says so plainly and says to go to the nearest hospital. Bad
headaches that are worse in the morning or lying down, throwing up with them,
being much less alert than usual, or new trouble with your vision all belong on
that list.

**Before you read the list below, two things about it.** It is written for brain
tumors in general, so some of it will not be your situation: this tumor does not
usually cause seizures, and it does not swell the brain around it the way a tumor
growing inside the brain does. If you have ever had a seizure, that ambulance
rule still covers you. The parts about pressure building up, and about a fever
while you are having chemotherapy, do apply. So does the fever rule for the weeks
after an operation, even though yours may have gone through your nose rather
than through an opening in the skull.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this?

**A scan can suggest it. Only tissue can name it.**

**You will usually have both an MRI and a CT.** They answer different questions.
The [MRI](/tests/mri) is the best way to see the tumor itself and what it is
touching, such as nerves and blood vessels. The [CT scan](/tests/ct-scan) shows
what it has done to the bone. Teams normally want both.

**Then a small piece is taken and looked at.** That is a
[biopsy](/tests/biopsy), and for this tumor **how it is done matters as much as
that it is done**.

**If you read one paragraph here, read this one.** Chordoma can shed cells along
the track a needle makes. The Chordoma Foundation says a
biopsy for a tumor in the spine or sacrum should be done from the back, with the
needle inside a tube, to stop cells spreading along its path. Surgeons call that
seeding. The track itself is normally cut out later, during the operation.

**The Chordoma Foundation also says to avoid having a biopsy or surgery outside a
referral center.** A biopsy that is not carefully planned can make the tumor
harder to treat later.

**So the single most useful move is about where you go, not how fast.** The
advice is to get to a hospital or network that has experience with chordoma as
soon as it is suspected. That is not an instruction to rush to an emergency room.
It is an instruction about which door to walk through first.

**One lab test names it.** Chordoma cells carry a protein called brachyury (say:
bra-KEE-ur-ee). Nearly all chordomas have it and the look-alikes do not, so
staining for it is what separates this tumor from the things it resembles.

## What do the words on my report mean?

**Your report describes cells, not your future.** Here are the words most likely
to be on it.

**Physaliphorous cells.** A long word for a simple observation. Cleveland Clinic
describes them as looking bubbly. Seeing them is part of how the diagnosis is
made.

**Brachyury, or TBXT.** The protein above, and the gene that makes it. A positive
result supports chordoma and is the main thing that rules out its look-alikes.

**Triple positive.** An older shorthand. This tumor was first described as
staining for three things at once. Brachyury is the modern, decisive one.

**Margin.** The healthy tissue taken out around the tumor. Your report may say:

- **Wide.** The whole tumor came out with at least a millimeter of healthy tissue
  all round it. That is the best result.
- **Marginal.** Less than a millimeter of healthy tissue, but nothing visible left
  behind.
- **Intralesional.** Visible tumor was left, or the tumor was cut into and cells
  spilled.

**The type.** Most chordomas are the conventional kind. Two rarer kinds behave
differently: one called poorly differentiated, and one called dedifferentiated.

**One gene result changes the plan, and it is worth asking about.** The poorly
differentiated kind has lost a gene called SMARCB1, also written INI1. The
Chordoma Foundation's medical advisory board suggests testing for that loss in
anyone under 35, or whose tumor is growing unusually fast. Those tumors are
sometimes treated differently.

[CROSSWALK]

### Older names, and names that are not this tumor

**Chondroid chordoma is not a separate diagnosis any more.** It was moved to sit
inside conventional chordoma. If that phrase is on your paperwork, nothing about
your tumor changed. The way of grouping it did.

**You may see "chordoma, NOS" or "malignant chordoma" on a form.** Neither is a
different disease. All chordoma is cancer, so "malignant" adds nothing, and the
NOS part is the one explained just above.

**Parachordoma is an old name and it is not this tumor.** It was used decades ago
for something that is now grouped with a different family altogether. It does not
carry brachyury, which is how the two are told apart.

**Some names sound like this one and are something else entirely:**

- **Chondrosarcoma.** The important one. It also turns up at the skull base and
  can look similar. It starts in cartilage rather than notochord leftovers, it
  usually sits **off** to the side rather than in the midline, and it does not
  carry brachyury.
- **Chordoid meningioma** and **chordoid glioma.** Both share part of the word and
  neither is a chordoma. One is a kind of meningioma; the other grows in a fluid
  space inside the brain.
- **Ecchordosis physaliphora** and **benign notochordal cell tumor.** Both are
  non-cancerous leftovers of the same rod this tumor comes from. The first is
  described as a benign lesion, and the second is usually just watched rather
  than treated. Being told you have a small notochord remnant is not being told
  you have cancer.

**If two of your reports disagree, bring them both to your team.** That is a good
question to raise, not an awkward one.

## How is it usually treated?

**For most people it is surgery, then radiation.** Drugs have a smaller part in
this tumor than in most cancers.

**Where the tumor is changes what surgery can aim for, and the two are genuinely
different.** This is the part most likely to be flattened into one rule, so it is
split here.

### If it is in your spine or at your tailbone

**The aim is to take it out in one piece**, with a rim of healthy tissue all the
way round. Surgeons call that en bloc removal.

**The reason is the seeding above.** Taking it out in pieces, or cutting into it,
can spill cells into the space around it, and those cells can grow.

**At the tailbone end this is a hard trade and you should hear it early.** The
nerves that run your bladder, bowel and sexual function pass right through there.
How high up the tumor sits decides how much of that function is at risk, and your
team can usually tell you beforehand. The Chordoma Foundation is direct about it:
people can be left with loss of bladder and bowel control, changes in sexual
function, nerve pain, and trouble moving.

**That is not the end of the story.** Some of it can come back over time, and
there are specialists whose whole job is helping with exactly these problems.
Ask to meet them early rather than late.

### If it is at the base of your skull

**Here, taking it out in one piece is often not possible, and that is not a
failure.** The expert consensus group says so directly: at the skull base,
removal may have to be piecemeal. There is brain stem, there are big blood
vessels, and there are the nerves for your eyes, face and swallowing, all in the
way.

**Most of these operations go through the nose.** A surgeon passes a small scope
through the nostril and works through that natural corridor. Some tumors are
reached from the side of the head instead.

### Radiation, for both

**Chordoma needs an unusually high dose**, and that single fact explains most of
what follows.

**The trouble is what sits next to it.** The dose this tumor needs is more than
the brain stem, the nerves to your eyes, or the spinal cord can safely take. So
the beam has to be shaped very tightly, or it has to stop.

**That is the real argument for proton beams.** Protons put most of their energy
at a set depth and then stop, instead of carrying on through. Carbon ion beams
work in a similar way.

**Be careful with the word better.** The Chordoma Foundation recommends the same
dose target **for particle beams and for X-ray treatments alike**. The
requirement is the dose, not the machine. Reviews comparing them rate the
evidence as low or very low quality, and found no clear difference in how long
people lived. [Proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy) goes through that
question properly.

**Carbon ion is not offered in many places.** The Chordoma Foundation's own list
of countries offering it does not include the United States. That is one
organization's list, and it may not be current, so ask your team if it matters
to you.

**Practically, it is a long haul.** Around 35 to 40 sessions over six to eight
weeks is usual. For many people that means moving near a center, or a long daily
commute, for about two months.
[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers what the daily visits
are actually like.

### Drugs, and trials

**Standard chemotherapy does not usually work on this tumor**, and no drug has
been approved specifically for it. Doctors sometimes use drugs approved for other
cancers.

**When a drug does help, help usually means the tumor stops growing.** It rarely
means the tumor shrinks. That is worth knowing before you start, because
otherwise a stable scan reads like failure when it is the goal.

**The rare poorly differentiated kind is the exception**, and chemotherapy is
sometimes part of the plan there.

**[Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) matter more here than for more
common cancers**, simply because the standard drug options are thin.!%tumor board%

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

**After surgery through the nose.** Your nose will be blocked, crusty and sore
for a while, and that is expected. Rinses and follow-up visits with an ear, nose
and throat specialist are part of the plan rather than a sign something is wrong.

**One thing after that operation needs its own rule.** The fluid around your
brain sits just behind where the surgeon worked, and it can leak.

- **Clear, watery fluid running from your nose or down the back of your throat
  means calling your surgery team**, especially in the first week or two. It is
  not mucus, and people often notice a salty taste, or that it is worse when they
  stand up.
- **Do not sit on it**, because a leak that keeps going can let infection reach
  the fluid around the brain. Cleveland Clinic calls that infection a medical
  emergency that needs treating immediately. A fever, a stiff neck, or bright
  light hurting your eyes means going to an emergency room, not waiting to see
  how the night goes.

**After surgery lower down**, expect weeks of limited movement, and a
rehabilitation plan rather than a single recovery date. If bladder or bowel
function changed, a specialist should be involved from the start.

**During radiation**, tiredness builds slowly over the weeks and is usually
worst near the end and for a while afterwards. Skin over the treated area can get
sore. Where the beam passes near your ears, mouth or throat, expect effects
there.

**Scans do not happen straight after radiation.** Teams normally wait a couple of
months before imaging, because the area needs time to settle before a picture
means anything.

## Everyday life

**Tiredness catches people out.** It is not the same as being short of sleep, and
rest does not entirely fix it. Studies have shown that physical activity, during
treatment and after it, can bring it down.

**Pain is the symptom people report most often, and it comes in two kinds.** Pain
from damaged nerves feels like burning, tingling or numbness, and it needs
different medicines from ordinary pain. Say which kind yours feels like, because
it changes the answer. If numbness or weakness is new, that is a different thing:
it is the rule in the section further up.

**If nerves near the skull base were affected**, there is a specialist for each
problem: eye specialists for double vision, including prisms and exercises;
speech and swallowing therapists; audiologists for hearing.

**If nerves lower down were affected**, bladder and bowel programs exist and
work. So does pelvic floor physical therapy. These are not things you have to
work out alone, and they are not things to be embarrassed about raising.

**Work.** Some people go back to the job they had, some change what they do, and
some cannot go back. In the United States, cancer counts as a disability under
the law, which means you can ask for reasonable adjustments. You do not have to
explain your diagnosis to ask. Saying you have a medical condition that may need
adjustments is enough to start that conversation.

**Money and travel are a real part of this**, particularly if treatment means
living near a proton center for two months. The Chordoma Foundation's navigators
help with exactly that, and their number is at the bottom of this page.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

**Follow-up for this tumor goes on for years, often fifteen or twenty of them.**
That is not because anyone expects the worst. It is because chordoma can come back
late, and a late return that is found early is a much better problem.

**We are not going to print a schedule**, and the reason is honest: the published
ones disagree. The Chordoma Foundation's own two pages give different intervals,
and the expert consensus group says plainly there is not enough data to recommend
one. Ask your team what yours is, and ask them to write it down.

**Waiting for scan results is its own thing.** Nearly everyone finds it hard and
it tends to get worse in the days just before.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) and
[waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) cover what helps.

## If it comes back

**More than half of these tumors grow again after the first round of treatment.**
Better to read that here, calmly, than to meet it unprepared later on.

**Usually it returns to the very spot it started from**, and there is a reason
that is worth understanding. This tumor spreads by pushing into what it is touching,
rather than by traveling through the blood or the lymph system. So it turns up
where it was, not somewhere new.

**It can spread further afield**, to places such as the lungs, but that is
usually not the first thing that happens.

**Coming back does not mean nothing can be done.** If it is in one spot, surgery
and high dose radiation may both be options again. If it is in several, the aim
changes to holding it steady and keeping you comfortable, and there are several
ways to do that. Once it has spread widely, doctors do not expect to get rid of
it completely, and they say so; what they work on then is control and how you
feel.

**Ask what the aim of each treatment is.** For this tumor the answer is often
control rather than removal, and knowing which one you are being offered changes
how you read the result.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**For this tumor there are two things worth adding.**

**Almost nobody inherits this.** A handful of families worldwide carry an extra
copy of the brachyury gene, and even there the wording matters: MedlinePlus says
people with that duplication inherit an increased risk of the condition, **not
the condition itself**.

**A common gene finding is not the same thing.** More than 95 percent of people
with chordoma carry a tiny common variation in that same gene. It is far too
common to be a cause on its own, and the Chordoma Foundation says outright that
it does not by itself cause chordoma. It is not something you passed on, and it
is not something you did.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
**Chordoma usually grows slowly, and that changes what the next few weeks need to
look like.** Unless your team has told you that something is urgent, there is
normally time to get a second opinion and to reach a center that treats this
tumor often. Using that time well is one of the few things in this that is
actually within your control.

**Numbers you find online are about groups of people, not about you.** They are
worked out by following large numbers of people and seeing what happened. If you
meet a middle figure, it means half of that group came out above it and half
below, and those above it can be a very long way above. Such a figure describes a
crowd. It predicts nothing about any one person in it.

**Those numbers are also older than they look.** To know what happened five years
after treatment, you have to study people treated at least five years ago. So the
figures describe treatment as it was then, not as it is now.

**The things that really shape this are far more particular than any published
number, and your own team already has every one of them in front of them**: the
site, how much came out, what the edges showed, and what sits alongside it.

Ask your own team when you are ready to. Leaving it for now is a real choice too,
and it stays open.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

**Chordoma adds three things to that list.**

**You may be having a harder time than they are, and that is documented.** The
Chordoma Foundation reports that caregivers say they struggle emotionally at a
higher rate than patients do. In a survey of families living with this tumor, the
thing caregivers reported most often was grief. Not stress. Grief. If that lands,
it is because it is common, and it is not a sign you are doing this badly.

**There is a support group for you specifically.** The Chordoma Foundation runs a
monthly online group for caregivers, separate from the one for patients. It is
free and someone trained runs it. Being in a room with people who already
understand saves a lot of explaining.

**The most useful thing early on is often logistics, not comfort.** Getting to an
experienced center, before a biopsy, is the thing that changes this person's
options most. If you have the energy to make phone calls when they do not, that
is where it counts.

## What to ask your team

- Where exactly is my tumor, and what is it touching?
- Have you treated chordoma before, and how often does this center see it?
- Should I be seen at a specialist center before anything else is done?
- If I need a biopsy, who will do it, and how will you avoid seeding the track?
- Is the goal of surgery to take it out in one piece? If not, why not?
- What does the edge, or margin, need to look like for you to be satisfied?
- If the tumor is near my tailbone, what will this cost me in bladder, bowel and
  sexual function, and can you predict that beforehand?
- Will I need radiation, what kind, and where would I have to go for it?
- How many weeks of treatment, and will I need to move to be near it?
- What is this treatment trying to achieve: removing it, or controlling it?
- Should my tumor be tested for the SMARCB1, or INI1, gene?
- How often will I be scanned, and for how many years?
- Which changes should I report to you between visits?
- Who is my first call if something changes?
