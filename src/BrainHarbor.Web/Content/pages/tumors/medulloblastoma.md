## The short version

**A medulloblastoma is a fast-growing tumor that starts at the back of the
brain.** It is the most common cancerous brain tumor in children, and adults get
it too. It can spread through the fluid around the brain and spine, so the whole
spine is checked at the start. **Treatment is usually surgery, then radiation to
the brain and spine, then chemotherapy**, with the radiation left out for the
youngest children. This tumor is grade 4, and that word means something
different here than it does for some other brain tumors.

## What is a medulloblastoma?

It starts in the **cerebellum**, at the back and bottom of the brain, most often
in the middle of it beside a fluid space called the fourth ventricle. The
cerebellum handles balance and coordination, which is why balance is often the
first thing that changes.

It is an **embryonal tumor**. That means it grows from early forms of nerve cells,
not from the supporting cells that
[gliomas](/tumors/glioma) come from.

It is most common in children under 16. Adults get it, and in adults it is rare.

### The name on older paperwork

Doctors used to call this tumor a **primitive neuroectodermal tumor**, or
**PNET**. That name is no longer used. You may still see it in an older letter,
or in a paper written some years ago.

You may also see **PNET5**, **PNET4** or **PNET3** with a number after them.
Those are names of research studies, not names of the tumor.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes. A medulloblastoma is cancer.** The word your team will use is
**malignant**.

**It is grade 4, and every medulloblastoma is.** Grade 4 is the top of the scale,
and here it describes this type of tumor as a whole rather than sorting one child's
tumor from another's.

**Grade 4 does not mean the same thing for every tumor.** The World Health
Organization says so about this tumor in particular. It warns that calling the
WNT kind grade 4, the same as brain tumors that cannot be treated, risks giving
families a false picture when treatment is discussed. Every medulloblastoma is
grade 4 whatever its kind, so the number cannot tell you how one child will do.

So the grade is not the part that tells you the most. **The kind, and whether it
has spread, matter more.** Both are on your report, and the next sections explain
them.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

It grows at the back of the brain, beside the space the brain's fluid runs
through, and **most of the early symptoms come from that fluid backing up**.

**It can also travel in that fluid.** Cells can settle further along the brain or
down the spine. That is why the whole spine is scanned and the fluid is tested,
and it is why treatment usually covers the whole brain and spine rather than one
spot.

## What symptoms does it cause?

Most of these come from the fluid backing up, and they often build over a few
weeks:

- Headaches, often worse in the morning, sometimes better after throwing up.
- Throwing up, often in the morning.
- Trouble with balance or walking, or falling backwards.
- Clumsiness, or handwriting that has changed.
- Double vision, or eyes that do not move together.
- Being much more tired, or not seeming like themselves.

New or worse trouble with balance or walking is a same-day call, or a right-away
call if there is a shunt or if the tumor has spread to the spine.

In a baby, the head can grow faster than it should and the soft spot on top can
swell. If you see that in a baby with this tumor, call your team the same day, or
right away if there is a shunt.

If the tumor has spread down the spine, there can be back pain, trouble walking,
and trouble with the bladder or bowel. For a tumor that has spread to the spine,
those signs have their own rule, at the end of the section on when to call for
help, below.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri) of the brain, and one of the whole spine.** The
  spine scan is best done before the operation, because after surgery the pictures
  can show marks that look like spread and are not.
- **The operation.** The tissue that names the tumor usually comes from the
  surgery to remove it.
- **Gene tests on that tissue.** They decide which kind it is, and the kind
  changes the treatment plan. One test reads a chemical pattern across the
  tumor's DNA, and it is the test that tells groups 3 and 4 apart.
- **A test of the fluid.** A thin needle in the lower back takes a small amount
  of the fluid, and the lab looks for tumor cells in it.

**The fluid test waits, and that wait is deliberate.** It is not done before
surgery, because the pressure in the head makes it unsafe. In the first days
after surgery, blood in the fluid makes the result hard to read. So the test is done
about two weeks later. Guidance differs a little on the exact day: one national
standard says at least 14 days in one place and 10 to 14 days in another. Ask
your team when yours is planned.

**What the result changes.** Tumor cells in the fluid, or spread seen on a scan,
put the tumor in the higher risk group, and that changes how much treatment is
given. Your team may call this staging, or use an M and a number.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

**This report has two parts, and both matter.** One says how the cells look. The
other says which molecular group the tumor is in. Your team puts them together
into one diagnosis.

**How the cells look**, one of four patterns:

- **Classic.**
- **Desmoplastic or nodular.**
- **With extensive nodularity**, seen in very young children.
- **Large cell, or anaplastic.**

**Which group it is in**, one of four:

- **WNT-activated.**
- **SHH-activated, TP53-wildtype.**
- **SHH-activated, TP53-mutant.**
- **Non-WNT, non-SHH.** Inside this one, your report may say **group 3** or
  **group 4**. Both names are still used.

**Which group it is in helps decide how much treatment is given, and which trials
fit.**

The two parts line up more often than not. Nearly all WNT tumors look classic,
and the desmoplastic and nodular ones are nearly all SHH.

Below the four groups there are finer groupings, fourteen of them, used mostly in
research. A report may name one.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the document
itself.

## How is it usually treated?

[TUMOR-BOARD]

**Surgery comes first, and the aim is to take all of it out.**
[How much came out](/treatments/craniotomy#how-much-came-out) explains the words
the team will use afterwards.

**If the fluid is blocked, that may be treated first**, and quickly. That can be
a temporary drain, [a shunt](/treatments/shunts), or a small operation inside the
brain that makes a new path for the fluid.

**[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy) to the whole brain and spine**
usually follows, with an extra dose aimed at the place the tumor was. Treating
the whole brain and spine is deliberate: this tumor travels in the fluid, so the
treatment follows the fluid. Your team may call it craniospinal radiation.

- **Not for the youngest children.** St. Jude does not use radiation under age 3,
  and Cancer Research UK says every child under 3 is in the higher risk group and
  radiation to the whole brain and spine is usually avoided. Chemotherapy is used
  instead, to hold the tumor while the child grows. **Where the line sits is
  changing**: newer studies use age 4 or 5 in some plans. Ask your team where they
  draw it.
- **[Proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy)** is used for some children.
  When the spine is treated, it can keep more of the dose away from the chest and
  belly. It is not available everywhere.

**[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy)** is usually given as well, after
radiation, or on its own for the youngest children. The combination usually
includes cisplatin, vincristine and cyclophosphamide, and some plans add
lomustine. Doses of each are held down
on purpose: to protect the nerves, the hearing and the chance of having children
later.

**Risk groups decide how much treatment is given.** A tumor is put in the higher
risk group if it has spread, if the cells are the large cell or anaplastic kind,
if certain gene results are there, or if the child is under 3. It also goes there
if more than a certain amount is left behind: your team measures what is left on the scan
after surgery, so ask which side of the line yours is on. Everyone else is in the
average risk group, which gets less.

**Ask about [clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials).** Several studies are
testing whether children with WNT tumors can safely have less radiation. Those
results are not published yet, so nobody can tell you today what they will show.

### After surgery at the back of the brain {#posterior-fossa-syndrome}

**This is common after surgery for a medulloblastoma.** It can follow surgery for
other tumors at the back of the brain too. Doctors call it
!%posterior fossa%**posterior fossa syndrome**.

[POSTERIOR-FOSSA-SYNDROME]

**Some of it can last.** A study that followed survivors for years found that
children who had it were more likely to have lasting trouble with attention and
with how fast thinking works, even after the early symptoms settled.

**The risk is lower with a surgeon who does many of these operations**, and it is
higher the younger the child is. Asking how often your surgeon operates here is
fair, and it is a question teams expect.

## Everyday life: school, hearing, growth and tiredness

- **School and learning.** Radiation to a young brain, and this treatment as a
  whole, can affect memory, attention and how fast school work goes. Ask early
  who at the hospital helps with school, and ask what help can be put in place.
- **Hearing.** Cisplatin and radiation to the head can both affect hearing, more
  so in a young child. Hearing tests find it early, and they belong in the regular
  check-ups.
- **Growth and hormones.** Radiation near the gland at the base of the brain can
  change the hormones that run growth and other body systems. This is checked for
  years, and it is treatable.
- **Tiredness** can come from the treatment, and it can also come from the tumor
  or from fluid that is not draining. Sleeping much more than being awake is a
  same-day call, or a right-away call if there is a shunt.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

**Scans go on for years**, and they spread further apart as time passes.
Follow-up is not only scans: it includes hearing tests, eye tests, blood tests
for hormones, and a check of balance and strength. A curve in the spine can
develop after treatment, so that is watched too.

[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes through what each scan is compared
with, and [waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the gap before
the answer.

## If it comes back, or changes

**There is no single agreed plan for a medulloblastoma that comes back**, and
sources say so plainly. What is offered depends on what was given the first time.

Your team may talk about surgery again, radiation for a child who did not have it
the first time, chemotherapy, or a clinical trial. **The tumor can change**, so
your team may test a new sample rather than rely on the first result.

**Ask what each option is for**, and write the answers down.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**A small number are linked to an inherited condition.** The ones linked with
this tumor are **Gorlin syndrome**, **Li-Fraumeni syndrome** and **familial
adenomatous polyposis**, which older letters may call Turcot syndrome. That name
is not used now.

**Which kind the tumor is decides who gets offered testing.** Teams offer genetic
counseling to families of children with SHH tumors whatever the family history,
and to some others depending on the gene result. If your family has one of these
conditions, say so early, because children with Gorlin syndrome are unusually
sensitive to radiation and it can change which treatment is chosen. Tell your team
before treatment is planned.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes talk about outlook using numbers. It helps to know what those
numbers are before you choose whether to look at them.

The numbers describe children treated years ago. The groups were also sorted
differently then, so an older figure may mix kinds that behave very differently
now.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. It is
not a prediction about any one person.

With this tumor the kind matters a great deal. Treatment works well enough for
the WNT kind that doctors are testing whether those children can have less of it. The outlook is harder when the tumor has spread at diagnosis,
when a lot is left after surgery, and for babies and very young children. It is
also harder once the tumor has come back, and sources say there is no agreed plan
for that.

Your own team knows your situation, and a page written for everybody does not.
They can talk it through with you whenever you want to.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three things specific to this diagnosis.

**Most people caring for someone with this tumor are parents of a child.**
Everything above still applies, and it is fine to ask the team to explain things
more than once.

**After surgery at the back of the brain, watch talking and swallowing.** If your
child comes home still having trouble swallowing, ask to be shown how to give food
and drink before you leave. New or worse trouble swallowing after you are home is
a same-day call, or a right-away call if there is a shunt. Choking, or trouble
breathing, is an ambulance call.

**Keep the check-up dates somewhere safe.** Hearing, hormones, eyes and school
support are all followed for years, by different people, and you may be the one
holding the whole picture.

## What to ask your team

- What kind is it, and what does the report say about the group and the cells?
- Has the whole spine been scanned, and when will the fluid be tested?
- Was all of it taken out? What did the scan after surgery show?
- Is this average risk or higher risk, and what does that change?
- Will radiation cover the brain and spine, and is proton therapy an option?
- If my child is very young, what is used instead of radiation?
- What is the chemotherapy for, and which side effects are watched for?
- Before surgery: how often do you do this operation?
- Who checks hearing, hormones and school progress, and how often?
- Should our family be offered genetic counseling?
- Is there a clinical trial for this kind?
- Who do we call after hours, and what number is it?
