## The short version

**This is a rare tumor that grows deep in the middle of the brain.** Doctors
think it starts from cells that were meant to become eggs or sperm, and that
ended up in the brain before birth.

**Most people who get one are older children, teens or young adults.** If you are
a parent reading this, the page mostly speaks to your child as "you", and some
parts speak to you directly.

**There are two main kinds, and they are treated differently.** One is called a
germinoma. The others are grouped together under a longer name. Your team will
tell you which kind yours is, and this page says which kind it means each time
it matters.

**Blood tests, and a test of the fluid around the brain and spine, are a big
part of this one.** Some of these tumors put substances into that fluid and into
the blood, and those results can change the whole plan.

**One choice has to be made before treatment starts:** whether to save eggs or
sperm for later. It is at the start of the treatment section.

**A few warning signs here need help fast.** They are in the symptoms section,
and they are easier to learn now than on a bad night.

## What is a germ cell tumor in the brain?

**Doctors think it grows from cells that ended up in the wrong place before
birth.**

Very early in life, before you were born, a small group of cells travels to
where the ovaries or testicles will be. Those are the cells that later make eggs
or sperm. They are called germ cells. The American Cancer Society says that
sometimes a few of them do not move where they should, and end up in places such
as the brain. Doctors think this tumor can grow from cells like that, many years
later.

**It has cousins outside the brain.** Under a microscope, a germinoma looks a
lot like some tumors of the testicles and ovaries. It is the same family of
tumor, living somewhere different.

**It is mostly found around the teenage years.** St. Jude says about 9 in 10 are
found in people younger than 20. It is more common in boys and young men,
especially near the pineal gland.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Most of these tumors are cancer. One kind usually is not.**

A germinoma is a cancer. The World Health Organization, which sets the names
used for brain tumors, calls it malignant, which is the medical word for cancer.
Most of the other kinds are cancers too.

**The clearest exception is a mature teratoma.** Children's Minnesota does not count it
among the ones that are cancer. It can still press on the brain around it, so it
still needs treating, and an operation is usually how.

**For these tumors, the type matters more than any grade.** Many brain tumors
carry a grade from 1 to 4. Here, what steers treatment is which kind it is: a
germinoma and the other kinds are treated in different ways, as the treatment
section shows.

**If a report does say grade 4, here is what the WHO itself says about that.** A
tumor like a germinoma can be given grade 4 even when it now has treatments that
work well. A grade describes how a tumor like this would behave if nobody
treated it. It is not a prediction about you.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**Nearly all of these tumors grow along the middle of the brain.** Most are in
one of two spots, and each spot causes its own kind of trouble.

**Near the pineal gland.** This small gland sits deep in the middle of the
brain, toward the back. It is the most common spot. A tumor here sits right by
a narrow channel that the fluid around the brain drains through. It also sits
near the part of the brain that moves your eyes up and down.

**Just above the hormone gland.** The pituitary is a small gland under the
brain, behind the eyes, joined to the brain above by a thin stalk. A tumor here
can damage that stalk, and the stalk carries the hormone that tells your body to
hold on to water. It also sits just under the nerves for sight.

**In both spots at once.** Doctors call this bifocal. An expert group of doctors
from Europe and the US says a tumor in both of these spots is not counted as one
that has spread.

**Less often, deeper and off to one side.** There it tends to cause weakness on
one side of the body that builds slowly, and changes in thinking.

**Some reach the spine.** These tumors can travel down through the fluid that
runs around the brain and spinal cord. That is why the spine is scanned as well
as the head.

**Before the general part below.** It was written for every kind of brain tumor,
not for this one. Two things to take into it. The piece about fluid backing up
is the one most likely to be about you. And in its list of brain areas, two
entries are about where these grow: the deep middle one, and the one about
behind the eyes. The deep middle entry talks about pressure rather than the
one-sided weakness above, which comes from the spot off to one side instead.
For pressure signs, the rules in the symptoms section are stronger than the
advice here, and those are the ones to follow.

## What symptoms does it cause?

**They depend on where the tumor is, so they are set out that way.**

**Near the pineal gland**, most signs come from fluid backing up: headaches,
throwing up, and being far more tired or sleepy than usual. Many people also have
trouble looking up, or see double.

**Above the hormone gland**, the first sign is often a thirst that keeps
coming back, and peeing far more than usual. Growth can slow down. Puberty can come early, or late.
Sight can get worse.

**Puberty that starts too early in a boy** can come from a hormone some of these
tumors make, which the body reads as the signal to start puberty. This one is
mostly a sign in boys.

**Deeper and off to one side**, the sign to watch for is weakness down one side
of the body that gets worse slowly. Slow does not mean it can wait. New weakness
is a same-day call, and weakness down one side that comes on suddenly is an
ambulance call, as the lists further down say.

### Why the thirst can come years before the scan shows anything

This one confuses families, so it is worth knowing about.

The expert group mentioned above writes that this kind of thirst, when all a
scan shows is a thicker stalk, can come **years** before the tumor is found. A
study at the University of California, San Francisco looked at nine young people
who were later found to have a germinoma. Some of them had a first scan that was
read as normal. In some, a spinal fluid test showed a tumor substance before the
scan did.

So when nobody can find a cause for this kind of thirst, doctors repeat the scan
over time. A clear first scan is where the checking starts. It is not the end
of it.

### When to get help fast

**Something to know before the list below.** We read ten pages written about
germ cell tumors, from children's hospitals and brain tumor groups. Two more
would not let us in, so we count them as unknown rather than silent. None of the
pages about this tumor that we could read says when to call 911 or go to an
emergency room.

**So none of the rules here is a rule about the tumor itself.** Each one is tied
to a warning sign, and each one names who gives it. Some of the strongest come
from pages about the sign, not about this tumor.

[ESCALATION]

[SPINAL-CORD]

**This tumor adds four rules of its own.**

**If you have had an ETV.** That is an operation that makes a small opening deep
in the brain, so fluid can get around a blockage. The Hydrocephalus Association
says an ETV can close without warning. If the signs of fluid backing up come
back, it says that needs urgent care, from your doctor or the emergency
department, even when the signs seem to have nothing to do with the ETV. Someone who cannot be woken is an
ambulance call, as the list above says.!%hydrocephalus%

**If you cannot drink enough to keep up, or no longer feel thirsty.** Drinking
is how the body copes with this kind of thirst. Cleveland Clinic says that if someone with
it stops drinking, they can dry out quickly. The signs include feeling dizzy or
faint, feeling very tired, a dry mouth, and feeling sick to your stomach.
Cleveland Clinic says extreme thirst with a lot of peeing, or signs of drying
out, mean going to the hospital as soon as possible.

**If you take the medicine for the thirst, too much water is a danger too.** That
medicine works by making the body hold on to water. Cleveland Clinic says that
taking too much of it, or drinking too much while on it, can lower the salt in
your blood. The signs are a bad headache or one that will not go away,
confusion, and throwing up. For those, Cleveland Clinic's advice is 911, or the
closest emergency room, as soon as possible.

**If your body no longer makes enough of its own stress hormone.** A tumor near the
hormone gland can stop the body making cortisol, even without an operation. So
can radiation to the brain. Cortisol is what the body leans on to get through
an illness. St. Jude says that when a fever, an infection, an injury or an
operation hits, the signs can include throwing up, loose stools, drying out and
fainting. It says they can become life threatening and need emergency treatment
right away. In a crisis, St. Jude says the emergency dose, which is a shot, is
needed right away. It is for when someone is too ill to keep medicine down. [Steroids](/treatments/steroids) goes through the medicine and what to do
on sick days. Ask your team for your own written plan before you need it.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

**Usually a scan, blood tests, and a test of the fluid around the brain and
spine.**

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** of the brain, and of the whole spine.
- **Blood tests for tumor markers.** St. Jude describes these as substances made
  by cancer cells. Two matter here. One is called **AFP** and the other
  **beta-hCG**. Some of these tumors release one or both into the blood.
- **A spinal tap.** A fine needle goes into the lower back and draws off a little
  of the fluid around the spine. The lab checks it for the same markers, and looks for tumor
  cells. Cleveland Clinic says the levels in the blood and in this fluid help
  tell a germinoma apart from the other kinds.
- **Blood tests of your hormones**, because this part of the brain so often
  affects them.

**A scan comes before a spinal tap.** The American Cancer Society says doctors
have to be sure the test will not cause a big drop in pressure inside the head,
so a scan is usually done first. Your team can tell you when yours will be.

**What a spinal tap is like.** MedlinePlus says you lie on your side with your
knees pulled up toward your chest. A numbing shot comes first, and it stings for
a moment. You will feel a strong push as the needle goes in. Afterwards, some
people get a headache that lasts a few hours or a few days. Tell your team if
you do. There is no separate page on this site about the spinal tap yet, so this
is the short version.

### When the markers can name the tumor without an operation

**If the markers are high enough, they can be enough to name the tumor.** Very
high markers point to one of the other kinds. For those kinds, the expert group
says doctors in Europe and North America now often rely on the markers alone,
without taking a piece of the tumor.

**How high counts as high enough is not the same everywhere.** A germinoma can
raise beta-hCG a little too, and the expert group says there is no agreed line
between it and one of the other kinds. The main research groups in the US and in
Europe draw that line in different places. So this page
does not print a number. Your team will tell you what your own results mean.

**If the markers are not raised, a small piece of tissue is usually needed.** The
expert group says only tumors without raised markers should have a piece taken.
There is one exception it names: a tumor in both of the usual spots, with
markers that are not raised, is treated as a germinoma if the scan fits.

**The piece can often be taken in the same operation that clears the fluid.** A
review of how these tumors are handled says doing both together is safe, and is
generally recommended where it can be done.

## What do the words on my report mean?

**Germinoma.** The most common kind.

**Non-germinomatous germ cell tumor, often shortened to NGGCT.** This is the
name for all the other kinds, grouped together because they are treated
differently from a germinoma. It is a grouping used to choose treatment, not one
of the WHO's own type names.

**The WHO's own names** for the kinds are: germinoma, embryonal carcinoma, yolk
sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, mixed germ cell tumor, and three kinds of teratoma.
A mixed tumor has more than one kind in it.

**Yolk sac tumor, and endodermal sinus tumor.** Two names for the same kind.

**AFP and beta-hCG.** The two tumor markers from the blood and spinal fluid
tests.

**Secreting, and non-secreting.** These words are used more in Europe. They say
whether the tumor puts markers into the blood or fluid at a high level. As above,
different groups draw that line in different places.

**Bifocal.** The tumor is in both usual spots at once. It is not counted as
spread.

**Pinealoma.** An older, loose word. The US National Cancer Institute's
dictionary uses it for tumors that form in or around the pineal gland, and that
includes tumors that are not germ cell tumors at all. If you see it, ask which tumor it
really means.

**Ectopic pinealoma, and atypical teratoma.** Names used decades ago for what is
now called a germinoma. Neither is on the WHO's current list.

**Atypical teratoma is not ATRT.** [ATRT](/tumors/atrt) is a different tumor, and
its long name looks a lot like this old one. If a search for the old name took
you there, that page is about something else.

**Diabetes insipidus, and arginine vasopressin deficiency.** Both names mean the
thirst and the peeing described above. It is not the diabetes most people have
heard of. Cleveland Clinic says the name was changed because sharing the word
was causing confusion. [Pituitary tumor](/tumors/pituitary-tumor) tells why that
mattered.

**A grade on your report.** If it is a WHO grade, the grade section above says
what the WHO means by it for these tumors. A report on an immature teratoma may give
it a grade on a different scale. Ask your team what that one means for you.

## How is it usually treated?

**The plan depends most on which kind it is.** A germinoma and the other kinds
are treated in different ways, so each has its own part below.

### Before treatment starts: saving eggs or sperm

**Treatment can affect whether you can have children later, so this comes
first.** The American Cancer Society names some chemotherapy drugs, including the
platinum ones such as carboplatin, and surgery or radiation to the brain,
especially near the hormone gland, among the treatments that can affect it. The Alliance for
Fertility Preservation says these choices often need to be made quickly, before
treatment begins. It says freezing sperm can often happen quickly, while freezing
eggs may take 1 to 2 weeks.

**If the person with the tumor is a teenager, they belong in this
conversation.** The American Cancer Society says children and teens should take
part as soon as they are old enough to understand. It says boys usually make
enough sperm to save by about age 12. There is not yet a proven way to save
fertility in boys who have not gone through puberty. For a girl, the choices
depend on whether she has.

**One kind of fertility problem can often be fixed later.** Radiation to the
brain can damage the signals the brain sends to the ovaries or testicles. The
American Cancer Society says that when the ovaries or testicles themselves are
not damaged, hormone treatment helps fix this.

### A germinoma

**Radiation works well on a germinoma, and chemotherapy is usually added so less
radiation is needed.** Children's Minnesota explains it like this: radiation on
its own works on this tumor, and chemotherapy is added for children so the
radiation can be lower and cover less of the brain.

**Why less radiation matters.** St. Jude says radiation to the brain has been
linked to long-term side effects, including learning problems and trouble with
hormones. So the usual plan now is chemotherapy first, then radiation. When the
tumor has not spread, Boston Children's says it does not need radiation to the
whole brain or the spine.

**Not every hospital does it the same way.** The American Cancer Society still
describes radiation on its own as a main way to treat a germinoma, with
chemotherapy added in some cases. A review of these tumors
says chemotherapy alone is not the standard, because in earlier trials the tumor
often came back.

**An operation to take it out is usually not needed.** Boston Children's says
removing a germinoma is generally not needed. Any operation is mostly to get a
small piece for testing, or to help the fluid drain.

### The other kinds

**Chemotherapy comes first, then an operation on anything left, then
radiation.** Children's Minnesota says all of these need chemotherapy first to
shrink the tumor. If some is left after that, an operation takes out what it can.
Radiation is then given to kill any tumor cells that remain.

**What is left after chemotherapy is often not the cancer part.** The expert
group says what remains after chemotherapy is mostly teratoma. That still has to
come out, so the operation matters either way, and it is how doctors find out
what is there.

**Radiation often covers the brain and the spine.** St. Jude says that in
earlier studies, when radiation was given to only part of the brain, the cancer
often came back in the spine. Plans still differ between countries, and experts
say the best plan when the tumor has not spread is not yet settled.

### A mature teratoma

**An operation is the main treatment.** A review of these tumors says germ cell
tumors respond well to radiation and chemotherapy, except for mature teratomas,
and that mature teratomas can often be treated well with surgery.

### If the tumor grows during treatment

**A tumor that grows during chemotherapy has not always stopped responding.**
Doctors call one form of this **growing teratoma syndrome**. A report on it
explains that the chemotherapy clears away the parts it can reach, while the
teratoma part is left behind and keeps growing. The Childhood Brain Tumor
Foundation says this is **not** a sign that chemotherapy has failed. The teratoma
is taken out, and then the first plan carries on.

**Falling marker levels never cancel new symptoms.** The same report warns that
markers going down should not reassure anyone if symptoms are getting worse or
the tumor is getting bigger. If something new starts, tell your team, even if
the blood tests look better.

### The fluid

If the fluid is backed up, it may need to be drained. That can be an ETV, which
lets the fluid find a way around the blockage, or a thin tube called a shunt.
Children's Minnesota names both. [Shunts and hydrocephalus](/treatments/shunts)
covers life with a shunt.

### Radiation, and protons

[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers the course itself.
Some centers use [proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy). A 2025 review
found that for a germinoma, protons worked about as well as standard radiation. It could
not yet compare the long-term side effects. So protons have not been shown to be
better here. Whether they cause fewer problems later is still being studied.

**Clinical trials** are how the plans above keep changing. One current US
study is testing lower-dose radiation after chemotherapy for a germinoma.
[Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) explains how to ask about one.!%tumor board%

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy) goes through the treatment itself,
including the fever rule.

### Hormones, sometimes for good

**Hormone problems are common here, and some last for life.** The expert group
says the thirst problem is especially common, and that other hormone problems
are common too. St. Jude lists both among the long-term effects, and says some
people need hormone replacement.

**The thirst problem often stays even when the tumor is gone.** A children's
hospital in Canada says it is often permanent, and that some children need the
medicine for it for life. It also says some children lose the feeling of being
thirsty. If that happens, drinking has to be planned and counted rather than
left to thirst.

**If one of your medicines replaces cortisol, the rule in the symptoms section
applies to you.** Keep your written plan where you can find it quickly, and tell
any new doctor that you take it.

**Puberty may need help.** The American Cancer Society says treatment can affect
sexual development. Ask your team what to watch for, and who will keep an eye
on it.

### Hearing, thinking, and school

**Some of the chemotherapy used for this tumor can affect hearing.** St. Jude
names carboplatin among the drugs used. The American Cancer Society says drugs
like that, and radiation to the brain, raise the chance of hearing problems. Ask whether and when your hearing will be checked.

**School may need to change.** The American Cancer Society says children and young
adults treated for brain tumors may need extra help at school. In the US, that
help is often set out in a 504 plan or an IEP, which St. Jude explains. Ask your
team about testing for thinking and learning, so the school has something to work
from.

### Hunger and weight

If the tumor or its treatment affected the part of the brain above the hormone
gland, hunger and weight can change. The American Cancer Society lists weight
gain among the hormone effects of treatment. The page on
[craniopharyngioma](/tumors/craniopharyngioma), a tumor in the same spot, says
more about it.

### Later on

The American Cancer Society says some side effects do not show up until years
later. It also says radiation to the brain raises the chance of a different
brain tumor later in life. That is part of why check-ups carry on for a long
time.

## Everyday life

**If thirst is part of this, water has to be close at hand.** At school, at
work, on trips and at sports, you need to be able to drink and get to a
bathroom whenever you need to. That is worth raising with the school early,
along with the rest of the plan.

**If you take cortisol replacement, wear medical alert jewelry.** St. Jude says
people who need it should wear medical alert jewelry or carry an ID card.

**Tiredness is common** during treatment and for a while after, and it is worth
telling your team about at each visit instead of pushing through in silence.
Sleepiness that is new, or getting worse, is different. It is one of the fluid
signs in the symptoms section. For most people that means calling your team the
same day. With a shunt or an ETV, it means getting help right away.

**Driving, for those old enough,** depends on the rules where you live and on
your own sight and health. Your team can say what applies to you. If seizures
are part of it, [living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) covers driving.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

**Checks go on for years.** St. Jude says follow-up includes lab tests and MRI
scans. If your tumor made markers, ask whether blood tests for them will be part of
your checks.
Boston Children's says children treated for this tumor should see a survivorship
clinic every year.

Have your own schedule written down, and ask what would bring a check forward.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers how each scan is compared with
the last. [Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is for the wait.

## If it comes back

**There are still options, and no single standard plan.** A review of these
tumors says there is no agreed standard for treating a tumor that comes back.
St. Jude says the options can include high-dose chemotherapy with a stem cell
rescue. That means very strong chemotherapy, followed by giving back
blood-making cells collected earlier. Boston Children's says a child whose tumor
comes back may be able to join a trial of newer treatments.

What fits depends on the first treatment and on what has come back. Ask what
each option is meant to do, and write the answers down as you hear them.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**For this tumor, the answer is no.** The American Cancer Society says that when a
child or teen has a brain tumor, nothing the child did and nothing the family did
could have prevented it. The Childhood Brain Tumor Foundation says these tumors are
found only very rarely in more than one family member.

**A few conditions people are born with are linked to a higher chance of it.**
These include Down syndrome and Klinefelter syndrome. They are present from
before birth. They are not something anyone did.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
**For many people with a germinoma, treatment is given with the aim of getting
rid of it for good.** Boston Children's says germinomas usually can be cured with
chemotherapy, radiation, or both. For the other kinds, treatment is stronger, and
St. Jude says they can be harder to treat.

**So for many families the harder question is how well, not only how long.**
That is why plans now try to use less radiation. St. Jude says the aim of its
current germinoma study is to reduce side effects while keeping survival the
same. What treatment leaves behind, in hormones, hearing and learning, is where a
lot of the work of the next few years goes.

**Numbers you find online describe groups, not you.** A middle figure means half
of a group did better and half did worse, and some did far better. And to know
what happened years after treatment, you have to look at people treated years
ago, so every such figure is older than it looks.

Ask your team when you want to. Not asking yet is a choice too, and it stays
open.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three things are specific to this tumor.

**Know the water rules in both directions.** Too little water and too much can
each be dangerous, and each has its own signs in the symptoms section. You may
be the one who notices first.

**Know the sick-day plan if they take cortisol replacement.** An ordinary stomach
bug can become serious. Keep the plan and the emergency shot where you can find
them in a hurry.

**Bring the teenager into the fertility conversation.** It is their future
family being talked about, and the American Cancer Society says they should be
part of it as soon as they are old enough to understand.

## What to ask your team

- Which kind is it: a germinoma, or one of the other kinds?
- What did the blood and spinal fluid markers show, and what do they mean here?
- Is a biopsy needed, or are the markers enough?
- Has the whole spine been checked?
- Before treatment starts, who can we talk to about saving eggs or sperm?
- What is the plan, and in what order: chemotherapy, radiation, an operation?
- How much radiation, and to which part of the brain?
- Which hormones are low now, and which ones will be checked later?
- If I need medicine for the thirst, what are my rules for drinking?
- If I need cortisol replacement, what is my sick-day plan?
- Should I carry an emergency shot and wear medical alert jewelry?
- If the tumor grows during treatment, what would you check first?
- Will my hearing be tested, and when?
- Who can help plan for school?
- How long will follow-up go on, and what will each check include?
- What number do we call at night or on a weekend, and who answers?
