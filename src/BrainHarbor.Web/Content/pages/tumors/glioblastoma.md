## The short version

**One thing this tumor does explains most of what you will be told about it.**
It grows faster than it can build a blood supply, so the middle of it starves
and dies. That dead area is why your scan report says necrosis, why the tumor
shows up as a bright ring with a dark center, why there is swelling around it,
and why it keeps pushing outward. **GBM** and **glioblastoma multiforme** are
older words for the same tumor. If your paperwork says *secondary
glioblastoma*, look for the **IDH** line on your report: it decides whether this
page is yours or whether [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma) is.

## What is a glioblastoma?

A glioblastoma is a tumor that starts in the brain's helper cells, the ones that
sit around and support nerve cells. It is one kind of
[glioma](/tumors/glioma), and it is the most common cancerous brain tumor that
starts in the brain in adults.

It is described as having **poorly defined boundaries**. That is not a detail
about the scan. It is the single most important fact about this tumor, and most
of this page follows from it.

### GBM, and the older name glioblastoma multiforme

You will see three words used for one thing.

- **Glioblastoma** is the current name.
- **Glioblastoma multiforme** is the older full name.
- **GBM** is the short version of the older name, and it is still what most
  people say, including many doctors.

They are the same diagnosis. Nothing about your tumor is different depending on
which one somebody uses.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes. A glioblastoma is cancer.** The word your team will use is **malignant**.

**It is always grade 4.** There is no grade 1, 2 or 3 glioblastoma. Grade 4 is
the top of the scale, and it describes how the cells look and behave rather than
how long anybody has. If you have been told "glioblastoma", you have been told
grade 4 at the same time, whether or not the number was said out loud.

Grade 4 is not a prediction about you. It is a description of the tumor.

### But my tumor did not look grade 4

This catches a lot of people out, and it is not a mistake.

Since 2021, a tumor can be called a glioblastoma on its **gene results alone**.
That holds even when the cells under the microscope look like a lower grade.

The rule is this. The tumor has no IDH change, and it carries at least one of
three findings: a **TERT** promoter change, extra copies of **EGFR**, or an
extra **chromosome 7** together with a missing **chromosome 10**. Any one of
those is enough. It is a glioblastoma, and it is grade 4.

So a report can say the cells looked lower grade and still land here. Nobody
changed their mind and nobody got it wrong. The gene results came in after the
first look, and they are what the rules go on now.

[Molecular markers](/tests/molecular-markers) explains each of those results.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

Most glioblastomas grow in the large upper part of the brain. That part handles
movement, speech, thinking and vision. So the first symptoms are often on one
side of the body, or in speech, or in behavior. Which ones come first depends
on where in that area the tumor sits.

[MECHANISM]

### The growth story, which explains four things at once

This is worth reading slowly, because it is the one paragraph that makes the
rest of the page make sense.

A glioblastoma grows quickly. It grows more quickly than it can build blood
vessels to feed itself, so oxygen runs low inside it. The cells in the middle,
furthest from a blood supply, starve and die. **That dead area is what the word
necrosis means on your report.**

Low oxygen switches on a chemical alarm inside the tumor cells that are still
alive. That alarm makes them release a signal telling the body to build new
blood vessels. New vessels do get built. But they are built in a hurry, and they
come out abnormal: immature, badly formed and leaky.

Those two facts explain almost everything else:

- **The ring on your scan.** Contrast dye leaks out of those abnormal vessels
  and lights up the living rim of the tumor. The dead middle stays dark. That is
  the ring.
- **The swelling.** Fluid and protein are thought to leak out of those same
  vessels into the brain around the tumor. That swelling is often what is actually causing your
  symptoms, which is why steroids are aimed at it rather than at the tumor.
- **Why it keeps growing.** New vessels feed more tumor, the new tumor outgrows
  them in turn, and the cycle starts again a little further out.
- **Why an operation cannot get all of it.** Tumor cells have already traveled
  past the edge that shows up on the scan. The picture shows where the tumor is
  dense, not where it ends.

None of that is a reason to expect less of an operation. What surgery does is
take pressure off, give the tissue the diagnosis is made from, and leave the
other treatments less to work against.

## What symptoms does it cause?

The commonest are:

- Weakness or numbness down one side.
- Trouble with speech or with finding words.
- Changes in vision.
- Headache, nausea and throwing up, from pressure inside the head.
- Changes in behavior, memory and thinking, which are especially common in
  older people.

**Seizures happen in up to half of people with this diagnosis**, and for many
people a seizure is what led to the scan.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this?

A scan first, then a piece of the tumor, then gene tests on that piece.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** typically shows the pattern the growth story
  predicts: a rim that lights up with contrast dye, a dark center where the
  tissue has died, and a wide area of swelling around it.
- **A tissue sample**, taken at [an operation](/treatments/craniotomy) or by a
  needle biopsy. A scan can strongly suggest this tumor. It cannot name it.
- **[Gene tests on that tissue](/tests/molecular-markers)**, which are what the
  2021 rules turn on and what can set the grade on their own.

[How tissue is taken](/tests/biopsy) goes through both ways of getting that
sample, and what happens if it does not give an answer.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

The results that matter most on this particular report:

- **IDH-wildtype.** No IDH change. Under the current rules a glioblastoma is
  always IDH-wildtype. If your report says IDH-**mutant**, see the rename below,
  because your diagnosis has a different name now.
- **TERT promoter mutation**, **EGFR amplification**, **chromosome 7 gain and
  chromosome 10 loss.** Any one of these, in an IDH-wildtype tumor, is enough
  for this diagnosis on its own.
- **Necrosis** and **microvascular proliferation.** The dead area and the
  crowd of new abnormal vessels. These are the classic features under the
  microscope.
- **[MGMT](/tests/molecular-markers#mgmt).** This one is different from the
  others, and people mix it up constantly. **MGMT is not part of the
  diagnosis.** It does not decide what your tumor is called. It is a separate
  test that helps predict how well one of the drugs is likely to work.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the document
itself, section by section.

### If your older paperwork says something different

Two names land on this page more than anywhere else.

- **Glioblastoma multiforme**, or **GBM**, is the same tumor under older wording.
  It does not change your diagnosis or your treatment.
- **Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant.** This is the one that matters, and it was
  retired in 2021. A tumor with an IDH change is **not** called a glioblastoma
  any more. It is now an
  **[astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4](/tumors/astrocytoma)**.
- **Secondary glioblastoma.** This is a different kind of old word, and it is
  worth being careful with. It described how a tumor **arose**, meaning it grew
  out of a lower-grade tumor over time. It did not say anything about IDH. So
  it does not tell you on its own which page is yours.

**So go by the IDH line, not by the old word.** If your report says IDH-mutant
and glioblastoma together, it was written under the old rules, and
[astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma) is the page for you. If it says IDH-wildtype,
this page is yours whatever the older paperwork called it.

That is not just filing. The names were separated because IDH-mutant tumors
behave differently, and the treatment conversation is not the same. It is worth
asking your team to confirm which one applies to you.

## How is it usually treated?

[TUMOR-BOARD]

There is a standard plan for a newly diagnosed glioblastoma, and most people are
offered a version of it.

- **[Surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) first**, taking out as much as can safely
  come out. It also gives the tissue the diagnosis is made from, and taking
  bulk away can relieve pressure.
- **[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy) with
  [temozolomide](/treatments/chemotherapy)** together, usually starting a few
  weeks after surgery. The standard course is thirty sessions over about six
  weeks, with the capsule taken daily alongside it.
- **Temozolomide on its own afterwards**, in monthly cycles.

### Why they cannot take it all out

This is the question people most want answered, and the honest answer is in the
growth story above. Tumor cells have already spread past the edge that shows on
the scan, into brain that has to stay. Surgery removes what can be seen and
safely reached. It is not a failure of the operation or the surgeon, and asking
for a bigger operation does not fix it.

What surgery does do is take pressure off, give the diagnosis, and give the
other treatments less to work against.

### MGMT, and what it changes

If the MGMT test comes back **methylated**, temozolomide is more likely to help
you. If it comes back **unmethylated**, it is less likely to. It is one of the
few results on this page that changes a treatment conversation rather than
describing the tumor.

It is a fair thing to ask about directly: what was my MGMT result, and what does
your team do differently because of it?

### Tumor treating fields, the device you wear

**Tumor treating fields** is the name to ask about, and the device is usually
called **Optune**. It delivers alternating electric fields through pads worn on
the scalp, and it is used alongside temozolomide after radiation
finishes. It is usually started around four to seven weeks after radiation ends. It is
for people whose tumor is in the upper part of the brain, and who are managing
reasonably well day to day.

The main side effect is skin irritation where the pads sit. It means wearing it
**at least eighteen hours a day** and shaving your head. Some people find that a
reasonable trade and some do not, and that is a legitimate thing to weigh rather
than a test of how hard you are trying.

### If you are older, or not well enough for the full course

A shorter course of radiation is the preferred plan for people over about
seventy, and for people who are less well. It is not a smaller version handed
out to save trouble. It gives much the same results with fewer side effects and
a better time of it. That is why it is the standard for that group.

### Bevacizumab, and one honest note about it

**Bevacizumab** is a drug used for the swelling around a glioblastoma. It can make scans
look better and cut down the need for steroids. But in the trials in newly
diagnosed glioblastoma it **did not help people live longer**.

It is useful for what it is useful for. The thing worth holding on to is the
difference between a treatment that improves a picture and one that changes an
outcome. That difference is easy to lose in a conversation.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

The library pages carry the detail: [what a craniotomy is
like](/treatments/craniotomy), [what radiation is
like](/treatments/radiation-therapy), and [what chemotherapy is
like](/treatments/chemotherapy), including the fever rule, which is the one
thing to know before you start.

Two things specific to this diagnosis:

**Steroids will probably be part of it, and they change how you feel.** They
work on the swelling described above rather than on the tumor itself. They
also affect sleep, appetite, mood and blood sugar. Coming off them is done
slowly and on a plan. If you feel unlike yourself on them, that is the drug. Say
so rather than putting up with it.

**The weeks after radiation are their own thing.** Tiredness often arrives after
treatment finishes rather than during it, and it can be heavier than people
expect. It is not a sign that something has gone wrong.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

**Driving rules depend on where you live** and on your seizure history. Your team
knows the rules where you are. Ask directly rather than guessing, and ask again
after any change in your medicine or your seizures.

- [Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with#driving) covers the practical
  side: triggers, sleep, medicine routines and telling people.
- [What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is worth reading before
  you need it, and worth showing to the people around you.

**Work and money.** Some people keep working and many change what they do or how
much of it. Treatment is close to a full-time commitment for the first couple of
months. Ask your team early who at the hospital helps with the practical side.
There is usually somebody, and people tend to find them late.

Tiredness and changes in concentration, word-finding and patience are common,
and they come from the tumor and its treatment rather than from effort.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans continue after treatment, and the wait between a scan and its result is
the hard part for most people.
[Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers what is happening in
that gap.

**A worse-looking scan does not always mean the tumor has grown.** After
radiation with temozolomide, a scan can look worse for a while and then settle
without any change of treatment. It has a name,
[pseudoprogression](/treatments/radiation-therapy), it usually shows up within
about the first three months after radiation finishes, and it can cause real
symptoms, so it is not only a picture problem. Telling it apart from real growth
sometimes needs a second scan rather than a decision on the day.

If your team says "let us scan again before we change anything", that is usually
this, and it is not them stalling.

## If it comes back, or changes

This is a tumor that is expected to come back, and saying so plainly is fairer
than letting it arrive as a surprise. Where and when it comes back varies, and
your own scans are what answer that rather than anything a page can say.

That is not the end of the options. There may be further surgery, radiation
again to a smaller area, a different chemotherapy such as **lomustine**,
**bevacizumab** for the swelling, tumor treating fields, or a clinical trial. What
fits depends on what was used before, how long the gap was, and where the tumor
is.

**Clinical trials are worth asking about early**, not only when other things have
been tried. Ask your team what trials you would be eligible for and what testing
would be needed to find out.

The thing worth doing now: ask your team what would make them want to see you
before your next appointment, and write the answer down.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**Will my children get this?** This is the other half of the same question and
it deserves a direct answer. The gene changes that define a glioblastoma, meaning
TERT, EGFR and the chromosome 7 and 10 pattern, are changes **in the tumor**,
not in you. They are not in the rest of your body and they are not passed on.

A small number of families do carry an inherited condition that raises the
chance of a brain tumor. It is uncommon. Your team can tell you whether anything
in your history points that way, and if it does they will offer genetic
counseling rather than leave you to work it out.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

They come from large groups of people treated years ago. That matters here for a
particular reason: the rules for naming this tumor changed in 2021, and they
moved a group of tumors out of this diagnosis and into a different one. So an
older figure may describe a group that included people who would not be given
your diagnosis today.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. Half
did better and half did worse. It is not a prediction about any one person, and
it is not a number of months anybody was handed.

The shape of the group matters as much as the middle. These figures are not
spread evenly. There is a long tail of people who do far better than the middle,
and those are real people rather than exceptions to be set aside. A median
cannot show you that tail.

What shapes your own situation most is your MGMT result, how much of the tumor
came out, how well you are managing day to day, your age, and how the tumor
behaves over the first months. Those are things your own team can see, and a
page written for everybody cannot.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Two things specific to this diagnosis.

**The changes you notice may not be the person.** Behavior, memory, patience
and word-finding can all be affected by the tumor, by the swelling around it and
by steroids. It can look like someone choosing to be difficult. It usually is
not.

**The first few months are the busiest.** Surgery, then six weeks of daily
radiation, then monthly cycles. Getting somebody to appointments every weekday
is the part people underestimate, and it is the thing worth asking for help with
early rather than when you are already worn out.

## What to ask your team

- Is my tumor IDH-wildtype? What was it that made it a glioblastoma?
- My cells did not look grade 4. What set the grade?
- What was my MGMT result, and what do you do differently because of it?
- Why could you not take all of it out, and what was left?
- Is the standard course right for me, or would a shorter one be better?
- Should I be using the device? What would make you recommend it or not?
- My older paperwork says something different. Which name applies to me now?
- If my next scan looks worse, how will you tell growth from pseudoprogression?
- What clinical trials would I be eligible for, and what testing does that need?
- What should I know about the steroids, and what is the plan for coming off?
- What are the rules about driving where I live?
- What would make you want to see me before my next appointment?
- Who do I call after hours, and what number is it?
