## The short version

!%oligodendrocyte%!%calcification%
**Both halves of this tumor's name are gene results, not opinions.** To be
called an oligodendroglioma, a tumor has to have two specific changes, and a
lab has to find both. That is why the wait for results matters here more than
almost anywhere else. The second of those changes is also what tells your team
this tumor is unusually likely to respond to chemotherapy. The name and the
treatment come out of the same biological fact. Seizures are the usual way it
announces itself, and there are only two grades of it.

## What is an oligodendroglioma?

Nerve cells carry signals. Around them sit helper cells, and one kind of helper
cell wraps nerve fibers in a fatty coat that lets signals travel faster. That
cell is called an **oligodendrocyte**. An oligodendroglioma is a tumor made of
cells that look like those.

It is one kind of [glioma](/tumors/glioma). It belongs to the group called the
**adult-type diffuse gliomas**, which has three names in it. The other two are
[astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma) and [glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma).

Grade 2 tumors of this kind sit under the
[low-grade glioma](/tumors/low-grade-glioma) heading, and grade 3 ones under
[high-grade glioma](/tumors/high-grade-glioma). Those are groupings rather than
diagnoses, and you may meet either word before you meet your real one.

### The two results that make the name

A tumor is only an oligodendroglioma if the lab finds **both** of these in the
tumor cells:

- **An [IDH gene change](/tests/molecular-markers#idh).** This is the first
  fork in the road for this whole family of tumors.
- **[1p/19q co-deletion](/tests/molecular-markers#p-1p-19q).** Chromosomes come in
  pairs, and each one has a short arm and a long arm. Here the short arm of
  chromosome 1 and the long arm of chromosome 19 are both missing, together.

**One of the two is not enough.** A tumor with an IDH change but no 1p/19q
co-deletion is an astrocytoma, and it is treated differently. This is worth
knowing because it explains a wait that can feel like nothing is happening.

### The microscope can be overruled, and often is

Under a microscope these cells have a look pathologists recognize. The cells can
appear to sit in little round spaces, and the small blood vessels branch in a
pattern with a name of its own.

None of that settles it. A tumor that looks like a textbook oligodendroglioma
under the microscope is **not** one if 1p/19q is intact. The gene results
decide, and the appearance does not. If your team seemed sure at first and then
became careful about the name, this is usually why.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes. An oligodendroglioma is cancer.** It is a malignant tumor, and it is a
diffuse glioma, which means its cells spread into the normal brain around it
rather than sitting in a lump with a clean edge.

That has one consequence worth saying plainly. An operation cannot get all of
it, however well it goes. Some of it is already mixed in with brain that has to
stay. With today's treatments, a grade 2 tumor of this kind is not curable.

That is true at grade 3 as well. It is a hard sentence and it is not the whole
picture. Not curable is not the same as not treatable. This is a tumor people
are treated for over years, and the treatments do real work.

### There is no grade 1 and no grade 4

Most brain tumors are graded from 1 to 4. **This one only comes in grade 2 and
grade 3.** There is no such thing as a grade 1 oligodendroglioma, and there is
no oligodendroglioma that corresponds to grade 4.

That last part matters for a specific reader. On the
[astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma) page there is a gene result called CDKN2A/B
which, when both copies are missing, sets an astrocytoma to grade 4 on its own.
In an oligodendroglioma the same result can point to a grade 3 tumor that is
moving faster. **It does not create a grade 4**, because there is no grade 4
here to create.

### What the grade describes

The grade describes how the cells are behaving, not how long anyone has.

- **Grade 2.** The blood vessels branch in the usual pattern for this tumor, few
  cells are dividing, and the marker that counts dividing cells is low.
- **Grade 3.** New blood vessels are being built, the cells are packed more
  tightly, they look more abnormal, and many more of them are dividing.

Grade 3 tumors tend to arrive faster and with more obvious problems than grade 2
ones. Both grades are treated, and with the same kinds of treatment. What
changes is usually the order and how soon they start.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

This tumor has a favorite address. It turns up most often in the **frontal
lobe**, and it tends to sit in and just under the cortex, which is the folded
outer layer of the brain where the electrical activity happens.

[MECHANISM]

That location explains most of what this tumor does. It is sitting in the part
of the brain that generates seizures, which is why seizures are so common here.
When it is in the frontal lobe it can also change thinking and personality
before it changes anything a scan would call dramatic.

One thing it usually does **not** do early is cause much swelling. Heavy
swelling and bleeding around the tumor are uncommon with this one, so the
symptoms that come from pressure inside the head are less prominent early than
they are with a faster tumor.

## What symptoms does it cause?

**Seizures are the hallmark.** They are the commonest first sign, and for many
people a seizure is the event that led to the scan that found the tumor. If that is your story, you are in the ordinary pattern for this
diagnosis rather than an unusual one.

Other things people notice:

- Headache that keeps going or gets worse.
- Weakness, numbness, or trouble with speech.
- Loss of part of the field of vision.
- Changes in thinking, concentration or personality, especially with a frontal
  lobe tumor.
- Feeling sick and throwing up, if pressure inside the head has risen.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this?

A scan first, then a piece of the tumor, then gene tests on that piece.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** shows where it is and something about how it is
  behaving.
- **A CT scan often shows calcification.** Calcification is calcium that has
  built up inside the tumor. It shows up on a CT scan as bright specks or
  streaks, and it is common enough in this tumor to be a real clue. It is not
  something you did, it is not a stone, and it does not need removing on its
  own. When it forms a certain pattern, a radiologist will often suspect this
  tumor before anyone has looked at tissue.
- **A tissue sample**, taken at [an operation](/treatments/craniotomy) or by a
  needle biopsy. A scan can suggest this tumor. It cannot name it.
- **[Gene tests on that tissue](/tests/molecular-markers).** These are what turn
  a suspicion into the diagnosis, and they are the part that takes the longest.
  [Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) explains why the answer
  arrives in layers.

[How tissue is taken](/tests/biopsy) goes through both ways of getting that
sample.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

The results that matter most on this particular report:

- **1p/19q co-deleted.** Present, and required. This is half the diagnosis.
- **IDH-mutant.** Present, and required. This is the other half.
- **ATRX retained.** On an astrocytoma report this protein is usually lost. Here
  it is usually kept, so "retained" is a point in favor of this diagnosis rather
  than a problem.
- **TERT promoter mutation.** Present in most of these tumors. On its own it
  means little, and it specifically does not mean glioblastoma. It has to be
  read next to the IDH result.
- **CDKN2A/B.** If both copies are missing, this can point to a grade 3 tumor
  that is moving faster. See the section above for what it does not mean.
- **[Ki-67](/tests/molecular-markers#ki-67).** A count of how many cells are
  dividing. It helps set the grade.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the document
itself, section by section.

### If your older paperwork says something different

Two names were retired in 2021, and both of them land here more than anywhere
else. If you are holding a report with either one on it, nothing about your
tumor changed. The rules for writing it down changed.

- **Anaplastic oligodendroglioma.** This is now written as an oligodendroglioma,
  IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, **grade 3**. The word *anaplastic* has gone
  and the grade carries what it used to say. It does not mean something was
  missed.
- **Oligoastrocytoma**, sometimes written as a *mixed glioma*. This one did not
  get renamed. It was **eliminated**, and the reason is the whole subject of
  this page. That name was used when a tumor looked like it had both cell types
  in it and nobody could settle which it was. The 1p/19q test settles it. A
  tumor with the co-deletion is an oligodendroglioma, and one without it is an
  [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma). So the name did not disappear because it
  was wrong. It disappeared because there is now a test that answers the
  question it was invented to dodge.

If your report says oligoastrocytoma and nothing else, that older name is the
strongest reason on this page to ask whether your tissue can be tested now. It
is the question in the section above, and this is who it was written for.

### If your report does not mention 1p/19q

Some reports do not have this result. Older ones often do not. Over six years,
reported testing for it rose from under half of cases to about three in five.
Whether it was done also varied by where people were treated and by who they
were. So a gap here is not always a mistake, and it is a normal thing to ask
about.

It is a fair question, and there is a direct way to ask it: **ask whether the
tissue already taken can be tested now.** Your team will know whether it can.
Getting the answer can change both the name of your diagnosis and the treatment
you are offered, which is why it is worth asking rather than waiting for it to
come up.

## How is it usually treated?

[TUMOR-BOARD]

- **[Surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) first, where it is possible.** Taking out
  as much as can safely come out is the usual first step. Even when the whole
  tumor cannot come out, removing part of it can help with seizures.
  If the tumor is close to the parts that handle speech or movement, you may be
  awake for part of it. [Awake brain surgery](/treatments/awake-craniotomy)
  explains why.
- **[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy)** to the area the tumor was in,
  usually after surgery.
- **[PCV chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy)**, which is three drugs given
  together. It usually comes after the radiation is finished, not at the same
  time.

### Why PCV, and why on this tumor

This is the question people most want answered and most often get a vague answer
to.

The 1p/19q co-deletion is not only the thing that names this tumor. It is also
what tells your team the tumor is unusually likely to respond to chemotherapy.
One chromosome loss is doing both jobs. So **the label and the treatment are two
consequences of the same biological fact**, and that is the real reason everyone
waits for that test rather than getting on with something.

That is why chemotherapy is part of the plan at either grade, rather than
something held back. PCV is the one most often chosen.

### PCV or temozolomide? This one is genuinely open

You may meet somebody with a brain tumor who takes a single capsule at home and
wonder why you are being offered three drugs.

Both are real options and the choice is not settled.

- **Temozolomide** is one drug, taken by mouth, and it is easier to tolerate.
- **PCV** is three drugs, it is harder work, and recent evidence suggests it may
  do better for people whose tumors are 1p/19q co-deleted.

A trial called CODEL was designed to compare the two directly. Until it
reports, this is a real conversation to have with your team rather than a
question with a known answer. It is reasonable to ask which one they are recommending for you
and what is behind that.

There is one more thing worth knowing: temozolomide can sometimes drive changes
in a tumor that make it harder to treat if it comes back. That is one of the
things weighed in the choice.

### Vorasidenib

For a **grade 2** tumor with an IDH change, where some tumor is left after
surgery or it has come back, there is a newer drug called
[vorasidenib](/tests/molecular-markers#idh). It is taken by mouth and it acts on
the IDH change itself. It was approved in 2024 for people aged twelve and over.

It applies to a specific situation, not to everybody with this diagnosis. If it
has not been mentioned, it is a fair thing to ask about, and a fair thing to ask
what the answer is based on.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

The library pages cover the detail: [what a craniotomy is
like](/treatments/craniotomy), [what radiation is
like](/treatments/radiation-therapy), and [what chemotherapy
is like](/treatments/chemotherapy), including the fever rule, which is the one
thing to know before you start.

Two things specific to this tumor:

**Seizure control is part of the treatment, not a side issue.** For this
diagnosis, how well your seizures are controlled is one of the things treatment
is judged on. If your seizures are not well controlled, that is a reason to go
back to your team, not something to put up with between appointments.

**If your tumor is grade 2, the timing of radiation is a real choice.** Having
it early rather than later has not been shown to change how long people live. It
does tend to give a longer stretch before the tumor grows again. It also tends
to give better seizure control after surgery. Some teams choose to wait and some
do not. Ask out loud rather than assuming the timing was fixed.
[Watching a tumor, step by step](/treatments/watch-and-wait) covers what the waiting involves.

**For a grade 3 tumor this is not the same conversation.** There, treatment
after surgery is the standard plan rather than something to time, and the
question to ask is what the plan is rather than whether to wait.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

Seizures are the hallmark of this diagnosis, and they are often part of daily
life for years rather than a one-off event.

- [Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with#driving) covers the practical side:
  triggers, sleep, medicine routines and telling people.
- [What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is worth reading before
  you need it, and worth showing to the people you live and work with.
- [Your seizure medicine](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) has a page of its
  own. It covers what it can do to mood, and why the dose is not yours to
  change.
- **Driving rules depend on where you live** and on your seizure history. Your
  team knows the rules where you are. Ask directly rather than guessing, and ask
  again after any change in your medicine or your seizures.

**Work and money.** Many people keep working with this diagnosis, and many
change what they do or how much of it. If you cannot drive, getting to work is
often the harder problem rather than the work itself. Ask your team early who at
the hospital helps with the practical side. There is usually somebody, and
people tend to find them late.

Tiredness is common and is usually not the same as being sleepy. Frontal lobe
tumors and their treatment can also affect concentration, word-finding and
patience, and those are the changes people around you may notice before you do.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

This is a diagnosis followed with scans over a long time, so the waiting is
something you will do repeatedly rather than once.

The wait between a scan and its result is the hard part for most people, and the
worry usually peaks there rather than in the scanner.
[Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers what is actually
happening during the wait and why it takes as long as it does.

A stable scan is the ordinary outcome for many people for a long time. A change
on a scan is not always growth: it can be an effect of treatment, and telling
the two apart sometimes takes a second scan rather than a decision on the day.
[Pseudoprogression](/tests/follow-up-scans#looks-worse) is the word for that,
and that page also goes through what each scan is compared with and what the
words on the report mean.

## If it comes back, or changes

This tumor can come back, and it can come back a long time later. It can also
change over time into something that grows faster.

Neither of those is the end of the options. There is usually more than one thing
left to try: further surgery, radiation to a smaller area, a different
chemotherapy, or a clinical trial. What is right depends on what was used
before, how long the gap was, and where the tumor is.

The thing worth doing now, before any of that: ask your team what would make
them want to see you before your next appointment, and write the answer down.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

They come from large groups of people treated years ago. That matters here for
a particular reason. The rules for naming this tumor changed in 2021, and they
now rest on a test that was not always done. Older figures describe groups put
together by how tumors looked, not by the gene results that name this diagnosis
today. Some of those people would not be given your diagnosis now.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. Half
did better and half did worse. It is not a prediction about any one person, and
it is not a number of months anybody was handed.

The shape of the group matters as much as the middle. These figures are not
spread evenly. There is a long tail of people who do far better than the middle,
and those are real people rather than exceptions to be set aside. A median
cannot show you that tail.

Compared with the other diffuse gliomas in adults, this one is described as
slower-growing, and as more likely to respond to chemotherapy. That is a
comparison between diagnoses, not a promise about a person.

What shapes your own situation most is your grade, how much of the tumor came
out, how well your seizures are controlled, and how it behaves over the first
few years. Those are things your own team can see, and a page written for
everybody cannot.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

For a lot of people in this position, the seizures turn out to be the harder
part of daily life, rather than the tumor itself. They come without warning,
they happen in public, and they put you in the position of watching. Knowing what to do turns that from dread into something you can
handle. So does knowing which seizures need an ambulance and which do not.
[What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) draws that line.

The other thing worth knowing early: changes in personality, patience and
word-finding can come from a frontal lobe tumor itself. They are not the person
choosing to be different.

## What to ask your team

- Were both results found, the IDH change and the 1p/19q co-deletion?
- If 1p/19q is not on my report, can the tissue I already gave be tested now?
- What grade is it, and what set the grade?
- What were the ATRX, TERT and CDKN2A/B results?
- Is the plan PCV or temozolomide, and what is that based on for me?
- Should radiation happen now or later, and what changes if we wait?
- Am I someone vorasidenib could apply to? If not, what is that based on?
- My older paperwork says something different. Which name applies to me now?
- How well controlled do you think my seizures are, and what would better look
  like?
- What are the rules about driving where I live?
- What would make you want to see me before my next appointment?
- Who do I call after hours, and what number is it?
- Is there a clinical trial I should know about?
