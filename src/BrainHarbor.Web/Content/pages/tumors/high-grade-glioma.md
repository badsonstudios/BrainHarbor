## The short version

!%chemoradiation%!%pseudoprogression%!%transformation%
**"High-grade glioma" is a group, not a diagnosis.** It means grade 3 or grade
4. Several different tumors sit in that group, and they are not the same as each
other. Two of them can both be grade 4 and still need different treatment. So
the number on its own does not tell you what you have. The full name on your
pathology report does, and this page is about finding it and reading it.

## What is a high-grade glioma?

It is a phrase doctors use for a glioma that grows fast. It is not the name of a
tumor, and it will not appear on your report by itself.

[Glioma](/tumors/glioma) is the family. High grade describes one end of it. Your
actual diagnosis is a specific name with a grade and some gene results attached,
and it is on your pathology report. [Your pathology report, part by
part](/tests/pathology-report) shows you where to look.

### What sits under the label

In adults, these are the names you are most likely to meet:

- **[Astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma), IDH-mutant, grade 3 or grade 4.**
- **[Oligodendroglioma](/tumors/oligodendroglioma), IDH-mutant and
  1p/19q-codeleted, grade 3.**
- **[Glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma), IDH-wildtype, grade 4.** This is the
  most common one.

There are others. Some are named after a change in a gene called H3, such as
[diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma). Some belong to the
group usually seen in children, which can also turn up in adults.

**The [diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma) page is still
short at the moment.** The others are filled in. If you land there, this page and
[glioma](/tumors/glioma) are the fuller ones for now.

**So the first question to ask is a plain one:** what is the full name of my
tumor, not just its grade?

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

Yes. A high-grade glioma is cancer. The word your team will use is
**malignant**.

**The three named above grow into the brain around them** rather than pushing it
aside, so there is no edge to cut around. For those, with today's treatments,
they are not curable. That is a hard sentence and it is here on purpose, because
being told the grade without being told this is how people are caught out later.

**A smaller number of high-grade gliomas are not like that.** Some sit in a more
defined lump with a clearer border. Your team may call those circumscribed. It
is on your pathology report, and it changes what surgery can do, so it is worth
asking which kind yours is.

**Not curable is not the same as untreatable, and it is not a timeline.**
Treatment works, and for some people it works for a long time. What it means is
that this is managed over years rather than finished in one go, which is why the
scans do not stop and why your team keeps making appointments.

### What the grade actually describes

A grade describes how abnormal the tumor cells look under a microscope, and what
the gene tests say. Grade 3 and grade 4 both count as high grade. They are not
the same thing as each other, and being told "high grade" without being told
which one leaves you with a blurry picture.

Two things about grade catch people out.

**Grade does not mean the same thing across different tumors.** Since 2021,
grading is done inside each tumor type. So the same number on two different
diagnoses does not describe the same thing. If you and somebody else have
different names on your reports, comparing your grades tells you much less than
it looks like it does.

**This is the one that matters most when you read about other people.** A tumor with an IDH gene
change can be grade 4 and is still not called a glioblastoma. These tumors were
found to behave differently from the ones still called glioblastoma, so in 2021
they were moved out from under that name. Same number, different disease,
different plan. [The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers)
explains IDH itself.

**There is no grade 4 oligodendroglioma.** If your diagnosis is an
oligodendroglioma, grade 3 is the top of its scale. It is not one step below a
worse version of itself, because there is no worse version to be one step below.
It is easy to brace for a grade 4 that does not exist here, so it is worth
asking your team to confirm which tumor you have.

### Why does my report say grade 4 when the scan looked milder?

Because a gene result can now set the grade on its own, even when the cells look
lower grade.

An IDH-mutant astrocytoma is called grade 4 if both copies of a gene called
CDKN2A/B are missing, whatever the cells look like. A tumor without an IDH
change can be called glioblastoma, grade 4, on gene results alone.

It is not a mistake and nobody was hiding anything. The gene results took longer
to come back than the first impression did, and they carry more weight.
[The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes them one at a
time, and that page plus a conversation with your team are the two things worth
having.

### Did this start as something smaller?

Sometimes it did, and the name on your report is what tells you which.

**A glioma that began at grade 2 and changed over time keeps the name
astrocytoma** when it reaches grade 4. It does not become a glioblastoma. That
is why two people can both have a grade 4 tumor and have arrived there by
completely different routes.

**A glioblastoma is the other route.** It is IDH-wildtype, and one of the things
doctors check before settling on that diagnosis is whether there was a
lower-grade glioma there beforehand.

If you are wondering whether something was missed for years, that is a fair
question and not a rude one. Your team can answer it from your report and any
earlier scans you have had.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

These tumors can start anywhere in the brain or spinal cord, but most often they
are in the upper part of the brain. Your team may call that supratentorial. It
is the part that handles movement, speech, feeling and thinking, which is why
those are the things most often affected.

[MECHANISM]

### What is different about a fast-growing tumor

A slow tumor gives the brain time to adjust around it. A fast one does not. That
is a large part of why these tumors are found sooner and cause trouble sooner.

- **The swelling sits where the tumor cells already are.** With these tumors,
  the swollen brain around the tumor is brain the cells have spread into. It is
  a large part of why you feel how you feel, and it is treatable on its own.
- **Symptoms build over weeks, not years.** People often describe a few weeks of
  something being wrong rather than a long slow drift. If you look back and
  cannot find years of warning signs, that is not something you missed.
- **These tumors usually take up the dye on a scan.** Slower ones often do not.
  It is one of the things that made your scan look the way it did.
  [Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) goes through what the dye is doing.

None of these are things you can change by having noticed sooner. They are
things to tell your team about, because most of them can be treated.

## What symptoms does it cause?

The most common are headaches, seizures, feeling sick or being sick, weakness or
numbness on one side, trouble with speech, and changes in thinking, behavior or
personality. Which ones you get depends mostly on where the tumor sits, which
the section above walks through.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this?

Usually a scan first, then a piece of the tumor.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** shows where it is and something about how it is
  behaving.
- **A tissue sample**, taken either at [an
  operation](/treatments/craniotomy) or by a needle biopsy. A scan can suggest a
  high-grade glioma. It cannot give you the name.
- **Gene tests on that tissue.** These decide both the exact name and, often,
  the grade.

[How tissue is taken](/tests/biopsy) goes through both ways of getting that
sample.

The answer arrives in layers rather than all at once, and the name can change as
later results come back. [Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is
about that wait.

## What do the words on my report mean?

Your report should carry the tumor type, a grade and the gene results, brought
together into one answer. That combined answer is what your team treats.

[Your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the
document, and [the words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers)
explains each test.

### The word that used to mean grade 3

**Anaplastic.** That is the retired word this page's readers are most likely to
be holding, and it has a simple explanation.

Before 2021, doctors put the word anaplastic in front of a tumor's name to say
it was grade 3. So an anaplastic astrocytoma was a grade 3 astrocytoma, and an
anaplastic oligodendroglioma was a grade 3 oligodendroglioma. The word is no
longer used that way. The grade number does that job now.

- **Anaplastic astrocytoma** is now written as astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 3.
- **Anaplastic oligodendroglioma** is now oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and
  1p/19q-codeleted, grade 3.
- **Glioblastoma multiforme**, or GBM, is now just glioblastoma. Many people
  still say GBM in conversation.
- **Secondary glioblastoma**, or glioblastoma with an IDH change, is now called
  astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4. The page you want for that one is
  [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma).

You will still hear all of these said out loud, including by doctors. Nothing
about your tumor changed when the words did. If an old report and a new one do
not match, that is a good thing to take to your team and ask about.

## How is it usually treated?

Most people have some combination of surgery, radiation and chemotherapy. Which
combination, and in what order, depends on the exact diagnosis.

- **[Surgery](/treatments/craniotomy)**, to take out as much as can safely be
  taken and to get tissue for testing.
  If the tumor is close to the parts that handle speech or movement, you may be
  awake for part of it. [Awake brain surgery](/treatments/awake-craniotomy)
  explains why.
- **[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy)**, usually after surgery,
  and usually over several weeks.
- **[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy)**, as pills or into a vein.

Alongside those there is supportive treatment: medicine to bring swelling down,
medicine to control seizures if you have had one, and help with tiredness.

**Clinical trials are a normal part of this conversation**, at diagnosis and
again later. Asking about one is not a sign that the usual treatment has failed.

### Why is my friend on different chemotherapy?

This is a fair question and it has a real answer. The three main diagnoses are
treated differently on purpose, because they behave differently.

- **Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, grade 4.** Radiation and chemotherapy are given
  **at the same time**, and then the chemotherapy carries on afterwards. Your
  team may call the combination chemoradiation, or the Stupp protocol, after the
  doctor who designed it.
- **Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 3.** Radiation **first**, then the
  chemotherapy afterwards. Giving the drug during the radiation as well was
  tested for years and did not help, so it is usually left out.
- **Oligodendroglioma, grade 3.** Radiation first, then a **different**
  combination of drugs, called PCV.

So two people can both say "grade 3" or both say "grade 4" and be on genuinely
different plans, and neither of them is being short-changed. If you want to
understand yours, the question that gets you the answer is: which of these is my
diagnosis, and why this plan for it?

There is also a device worn on the head that treats the tumor with electrical
fields. It is called **tumor treating fields**, and it applies to some
glioblastomas. It is worth knowing that experts disagree about it: the European
guideline calls its role controversial and says it is not widely available
there. So ask your team what they think of it for you, rather than assuming
either way.

Two things worth knowing before you start.

**Finishing treatment is not the same as feeling finished.** Tiredness after
radiation often arrives late and lingers for weeks. Thinking and memory can feel
slower for a while. Neither means treatment failed.

**"Is this the tumor, or the medicine?"** is a question worth keeping in your
pocket. Steroids cause muscle weakness. Seizure medicines can affect mood and
temper. Both can look like the tumor winning when they are not.
[Steroids](/treatments/steroids) goes through the first one properly, and the
pages linked above cover the rest.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

**Seizures and driving.** The rules about driving depend entirely on where you
live, and in some places it is your doctor who has to report it rather than you.
[Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) covers the practical side, and
[the driving section](/seizures/living-with#driving) is the place to start
before confirming it with your own team.

**Work and money.** Some people keep working, sometimes with changes to hours or
duties. Some cannot. The financial side is one of the most stressful parts of
this and it is worth asking early what help exists, rather than when it is
urgent.

**Tiredness and thinking.** Tiredness here is not ordinary tiredness. People
around you may not see how heavy it is. Memory and focus are often hit too. Say
both out loud to your team rather than pushing through. There are things that
help, and nobody offers them if they do not know.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans continue after treatment, usually for as long as you are being followed.
They are how a change gets found while there are still choices about it.

The waiting has a name: **scanxiety**. Patients coined it. For most people the
hardest part is not the scan itself but the gap between the scan and the result.
Knowing that in advance tells you which days to plan something for.

### If your scan looks worse after radiation

**A scan that looks worse in the first few months after radiation does not
always mean the tumor has grown.** This has a name, and it is often not
explained in advance: **pseudoprogression**.

Here is what it is. Radiation and chemotherapy damage the tumor, and the body's
response to that damage shows up on a scan looking very much like growth. It is
a change caused by the treatment working, not by the tumor winning. It usually
shows up within about the first three months after treatment finishes.

**It can make you feel worse too, not just look worse.** That is the part people
are least prepared for. Swelling from the treatment effect can cause real
symptoms, and those can be treated.

**This is why your team may say "let's scan again" instead of changing the
plan.** There is a rule they follow. For the first twelve weeks after radiation,
they will not call a change growth unless a repeat scan a few weeks later shows
the same thing, or the new area sits outside where the radiation was aimed, or a
sample shows it. Waiting is often the only way to tell the two apart.

Those twelve weeks are hard, and the researchers who study this say so plainly.
One paper puts it like this: when a scan looks worse early, families can wait
with anxiety for the recommended twelve weeks to find out what the treatment
did. Nobody is being casual with you. **Ask what your team thinks they are
looking at, what the next scan would show, and what you should call about in the
meantime.** Those three answers make the gap easier to live in.

### Later on: radiation necrosis

Something similar can happen much later. Months, or even years, after radiation,
a patch of dead tissue can form where the treatment was aimed. The name for it
is **radiation necrosis**. It can cause swelling and pressure, and on a scan it
can look like the tumor coming back. Steroids often settle it, and sometimes an
operation is needed.

It happens rarely. It is worth knowing about, because "your scan has changed"
does not automatically mean one thing.

[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans) is the
fuller version of this section: what each scan is compared with, what the words
on the report mean, and what the research actually says about the waiting.

## If it comes back, or changes

For a high-grade glioma, the plan assumes the tumor will need attention again.
That is not pessimism, and it is not a secret being kept from you. It is why the
scans continue and why your team keeps looking.

If something does change, there are usually still options:

- More surgery.
- Radiation again, in some situations.
- A different drug.
- A clinical trial.

Which of these are open to you depends on your diagnosis, what you have already
had, and how well you are feeling day to day.

**A related thing, if you started out lower grade.** Some gliomas that began as
grade 2 change into a higher grade over time. That is called transformation, and
it can go to grade 3 or straight to grade 4. If that is how you arrived at this
page, [low-grade glioma](/tumors/low-grade-glioma) is where the earlier part of
your story is written down.

Being told there has been a change is a hard day. It is not the same as being
told there is nothing left to try.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before deciding whether you want them.

They come from large groups of people treated years ago. That matters more here
than in most illnesses, because the way these tumors are named and sorted
changed in 2021. Groups that used to be counted together are now known to be
different diseases. So a figure from an older group may not describe the group
you are actually in.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. Half
did better and half did worse. It is not a prediction about any one person, and
it is not a number of months anybody was handed.

The other thing worth knowing is the shape of the group. These figures are not
spread evenly around the middle. There is a long tail of people who do far
better than the middle, and those are real people rather than exceptions to be
set aside. A median cannot show you that tail, which is one of the reasons it is
a poor thing to hear on its own.

What shapes your own situation most is your exact diagnosis, your gene results,
how much of the tumor came out, and how it behaves over the first year. Those
are things your own team can see with your scans in front of them, and a page
written for everybody cannot.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

**What is different here is the pace.** Things can move faster than with a
slow-growing tumor, and treatment often starts soon after diagnosis. There may
be very little gap between being told and being booked in. If you feel behind
from the first week, that is the situation and not you.

**The scans in the first few months are the hard ones.** If a scan comes back
looking worse, it may be the treatment rather than the tumor, and the only way
to tell is another scan. That wait is hard on you as well as on them. It is fair
to ask the team directly what they think they are looking at.

**Watch for the changes only you will see.** A shorter temper. Words coming more
slowly. Sleeping much more. Say them at the appointment rather than deciding
they are too small to mention. Some of them turn out to be
[the steroids](/treatments/steroids) or
[the seizure medicine](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) rather than the
tumor, and those can be adjusted.

**If seizures are part of this**, learn what to do before you need to.
[What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is the one to know, and it
says which seizures need an ambulance and which do not.

**Look after your own health.** Keep your own appointments. Let people help with
the ordinary things, and say yes when they offer something specific.

## What to ask your team

- What is the full name of my tumor, not just the grade?
- Does my tumor have an IDH change?
- Why this treatment plan for my diagnosis, rather than another one?
- How much of the tumor came out, and how do you know?
- If my scan looks worse in the first few months, how will you tell treatment
  effect from the tumor growing?
- What would make you want to see me before my next appointment?
- Who do I call out of hours, and what number is it?
- What should I do about driving and work?
- Is there a clinical trial I should know about?
- Is any of what I am feeling caused by swelling or by my medicines rather than
  the tumor?
