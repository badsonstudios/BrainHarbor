## The short version

You have been told there is something on your scan. **That is not a diagnosis,
and nobody is keeping one from you.** A picture can suggest what a thing is.
Confirming it almost always means taking a piece of it and testing that, and
the name arrives in stages while those tests run.

**There is a path, and it has about seven steps.** Right now it probably feels
like nothing is happening. Usually something is, and you are allowed to ask
which step you are on. That one question turns a silence into a place on a map.

This page is for the days in between. It covers what happens next, why a scan
often cannot say on its own, and what you can ask for this week.

## What have you actually been told?

That a scan found something. That is all, and it is worth being precise about,
because it is less than it sounds.

A scan shows a shape. It shows where the shape is, how big it is, how much of
the dye it soaks up, and how the water moves inside it. **It does not show what
the cells are.** The word your team may use for the shape is a **lesion**, which
just means an area that does not look like the tissue around it. It is not a
diagnosis either.

Most brain tumors are found because they start to cause something. **There is no
screening test for them**, so almost nobody has their brain scanned without a
reason to. That cuts the other way too: the things that sent you for the scan
can be caused by plenty of other things.

**So there are three separate questions, and only the first one has been
answered.** Is there something there? What is it? What happens about it?

## What happens next {#the-seven-steps}

Here is the path most people take. Not everyone goes down all of it, and some
of it overlaps, but this is the shape.

1. **A scan finds something.** Often a scan done for a symptom, and sometimes a
   scan done for something else entirely. If yours was the fast scan in an
   emergency room, [your CT scan](/tests/ct-scan) explains what that one can and
   cannot say.
2. **A better picture.** Usually an MRI with contrast dye, if you have not had
   one yet. This is the step that narrows the list.
3. **A referral to a specialist team.** Your images and your notes go to people
   who look at brains all day. How that is arranged differs from place to place,
   so this is a fair thing to ask about by name.
4. **A decision about tissue.** Take a small sample, take the whole thing out,
   or take nothing and watch. All three are real answers.
5. **The first look.** If tissue is taken, somebody looks at a piece of it right
   away, while you are still in the operating room.
6. **The lab work.** The rest of the tests run over days and weeks, and they are
   what produce the full name.
7. **A plan.** Often after a meeting where your case is talked through by the
   whole group.

**The useful part of this list is not the list. It is that you can ask which
number you are on.** Your team can answer it in one sentence, and it turns
"nothing is happening" into "they are waiting on step 6".

Two honest notes about it. Some people stop at step 4 and never go further,
because watching is the right plan for what they have. And the steps do not
always run in that order, so what you get is your team's version of it, not
this one.

[Waiting for your results](/tests/waiting-for-results) picks up at step 5 and
goes through the rest of it properly, including how long each test takes and why
the name can change while you wait. **It counts its own steps from one**, so a
number there is not a number here.

## Why the scan cannot say on its own

A scan is a very good photograph. That is the whole of its power and the whole
of its limit.

Several very different things can make a very similar picture. One example is a
round spot with a bright ring around the edge. The list of things that produce
that picture includes a tumor that started in the brain, a tumor that spread
from a cancer somewhere else, **an infection**, **an area of inflammation**,
**an old stroke**, and **old bleeding that is breaking down**.

**Four of those are not a tumor at all.** That is not a comforting detail thrown
in. It is why your team will not name it yet.

**An infection can look like a tumor.** A pocket of infection in the brain is
called an abscess. On a scan it makes a ring like the one above, and brain
tumors sit on the list of things a team has to tell it apart from. There is a
particular kind of picture, called diffusion, that helps.

**Inflammation can look like a tumor too.** There is a kind that makes a large
patch which mimics a tumor closely enough that people have the operation and
the answer comes back as inflammation instead. The ring and the crowding on the
scan are what fool it.

So when a radiologist writes a list rather than a name, that is not hedging and
it is not bad luck with your doctor. It is the honest output of the machine.

**What closes the question is usually a piece of it.** A scan can often give a
good idea of the type. Confirming it means taking some tissue and testing it.
There is a narrow exception, for a thing sitting somewhere that would be too
risky to take a piece of, and
[is there a way to find out without one?](/tests/biopsy#is-there-a-way-to-find-out-without-one)
sets out when that applies. It is your team's call rather than something to ask
for, and it is a conversation rather than something that happens quietly.

## Where is this coming from?

Whatever it turns out to be, one rule explains most of what you have noticed.
Where a thing sits in the brain usually decides what you feel. **The next
section is a map** that lines the parts of the brain up against what people
notice, and it names the one exception.

**Two things before you read on, because the rest of this page was written for
somebody further along than you are.** It opens by saying that most people are
told what they have long before anyone explains what it is doing. **For you that
is the other way round**, and being here early is the one thing that is
different. And it says "your tumor" in several places, here and in the caregiver
section near the end. Nobody knows yet whether that is what you have, so **read
those words as "whatever this turns out to be."**

## When to call, and what for

Nobody has told you what this is, so nobody has told you what your warning signs
are either. Until they do, use this list.

[ESCALATION]

**One more, and it belongs to this page rather than to the list above.** An
infection is one of the things that can make the picture you have. **If you run
a fever while you are waiting, call your team today** rather than saving it for
the appointment. **Plenty of people who do have an infection in the brain never
run a fever at all**, so not having one settles nothing either way. That sits on

## Were you sent home with anything?

Two things often start before anybody has a name, and both of them catch people
out because they arrive without an explanation attached.

**A steroid.** It is given for the swelling rather than for the thing on the
scan, so feeling better on it is not news about what the thing is. It also
changes sleep, appetite, mood and blood sugar, and there is one rule that
matters more than the rest: **it is not a medicine to come off on your own.**
[Steroids](/treatments/steroids#why-taper) explains why, and what coming off it
looks like.

**A medicine to stop seizures**, if a seizure is what sent you for the scan.
[Anti-seizure medicines](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) goes through what
they do, and through the side effects that are easiest to mistake for the
illness.

And if a seizure is what happened, **driving is the thing that changes first**,
and it changes now rather than when somebody finally names the thing on your
scan. The rules are set where you live, so ask your team on the day rather than
guessing.
[Driving and seizures](/seizures/living-with#driving) has the rest of it.

## Will I need a piece of it taken?

Maybe, and it is the question step 4 exists to answer.

**The thing worth understanding now is what the decision turns on, because it is
not what most people assume.** It is not how badly anybody wants the answer.
It is where your thing is sitting and how well you are, and those decide between
three different plans: a **needle biopsy**, an operation that takes the whole
thing and gives the lab a sample on the way, or neither for now.

So the useful question is not "do I need a biopsy". It is **"which of the three
are you thinking about, and why that one?"**

[Having a biopsy](/tests/biopsy) sets the two operations side by side, goes
through the day itself, and covers what can go wrong.
[Watching a tumor, step by step](/treatments/watch-and-wait) is the third one,
which is a plan rather than a delay.
[Getting ready for surgery](/tests/getting-ready-for-surgery) covers the week
before an operation.

## How long until it has a name?

Longer than you want, and there is a reason for each part of it.

The quick look during the operation takes minutes. The answer from the
microscope takes days. The gene tests take weeks, and for a lot of brain tumors
the gene results are part of the name, so the full name waits for them.

**You may be told part of the answer before you are told all of it**, and that
is the thing most worth knowing in advance, because when it happens it feels
like being told two different things by the same team.

[Waiting for your results](/tests/waiting-for-results#how-long-does-it-take)
has the real durations, why some of it is counted in weeks, and what to do with
yourself in the meantime.

**The name is what unlocks the rest.** Once you have the name,
[the library](/tumors) has a page on that exact type: what it is, how it is
usually treated, what to expect. None of that can be written for your type
here, because none of those questions has an answer until the name arrives.

## Why "benign" is the wrong comfort word here {#benign}

You may be told a thing looks benign. It is worth knowing what that word does
and does not promise, because in the brain it promises less than it does
anywhere else in the body.

Outside the head, benign means a growth that stays where it is. That part is
still true here, and it is genuinely worth knowing.

**What it does not mean is harmless.** The skull cannot stretch. A growth that
never spreads anywhere still takes up room, and it can still damage the brain
next to it as it gets bigger. **Both kinds can be life-threatening**, because of
where they sit rather than because of what they are.

**Look at what the people who count these things write.** The registry that
keeps the numbers for the whole of the United States does not use the word
benign at all. It writes **malignant** and **non-malignant**. That second word
is the more honest one, because it says what a growth does not do without
promising anything about what it does.

So if you are told it looks benign, that is real information and it is worth
having. The follow-up that gets you the rest of it is: **where is it sitting,
and what would it do if we left it alone?**

## Why nobody has given you a stage {#no-stage}

Because there is not one to give.

Brain tumors are not staged the way most cancers are. There is no formal staging
system for them. If you have been waiting to be told a number from one to four
that means how far it has spread, it is not coming, and nothing is being kept
back.

What you get instead is a **type** and a **grade**. The type is what kind of
cells it is made of. The grade is a number for how the tumor is expected to
behave, worked out from how the cells look and from what the gene tests find. So
both of them usually wait for tissue.
[What the grade means](/tests/pathology-report#what-the-grade-means) walks
through it on a real report.

There is one situation where a stage does turn up, and it belongs to a different
cancer. That is the next section.

## Did it start in my brain, or somewhere else? {#primary-or-secondary}

This is the fork, and it is the one that changes the most.

Something that **started in the brain** is called a primary brain tumor.
Something that **started somewhere else in the body and traveled** is called a
secondary, or a metastasis. They are named differently, treated differently, and
looked after by different people.

Of the ones found in adults, more came from somewhere else than started in the
brain. So if nobody knows of a cancer in you yet, expect questions about the
rest of your body, and probably scans of it. **That is your team answering the
question, not assuming the worst.**

If it turns out to have come from somewhere else, there will be a stage, and it
will be the other cancer's stage rather than a brain one.
[Brain metastases](/tumors/brain-metastases) covers all of that, including what
happens when the search does not find a starting point.!%tumor board%

## Asking somebody else to look

Two different things get called a second opinion at this stage, and only one of
them is available to you yet.

**Before there is tissue, the thing to ask about is the plan.** Whether to
operate, what to take, and whether to watch instead are judgment calls, and
asking another team what they would do is an ordinary thing to do. Your team can
point you at somebody, and it is fair to ask them to. Some people go to a center
that does a lot of this, and asking whether there is one near you is a
reasonable question rather than a criticism of anybody.

**After there is tissue, the diagnosis itself can be looked at again**, and that
is a different thing from a second opinion on the plan.
[Can someone else look at my slides?](/tests/waiting-for-results#can-someone-else-look-at-my-slides)
goes through how it works, what it costs, and whether treatment has to wait.

The one worth asking for now is the first one, because it is about a decision
that has not been made yet.

## What you can do this week

Not much of this is under your control. These parts are.

- **Ask for copies of your scan and your report.** On a disc, or through the
  patient portal. You will be asked for them more than once, and having them
  saves a week later.
- **Write down the exact words you were told, and who said them.** You will be
  asked to repeat them, by other doctors and by everybody else, and a note beats
  reconstructing a conversation you were in shock during.
- **Take somebody with you to the next appointment**, so you are not the only
  person who has to hold on to it.
- **Ask who your first call is, and get the number.** Then ask what would make
  them want you to use it.
- **Do not try to read your own radiology report as a diagnosis.** It is a
  careful description of a picture. A name and a grade almost always wait for
  tissue, so the report cannot hold them yet, however closely you read it.

## Did I cause this?

You do not have a name yet, and this question does not wait for one. What
follows is about brain tumors, because that is the possibility this page is
filed under.

[CAUSES]

**"Should I have come in sooner?"** This is the version people ask at this stage,
and it deserves its own answer. There is no screening program that would have
caught this earlier, so there was no earlier to come in to. And the things that
finally sent you are things that are usually caused by something else entirely,
which is why neither you nor your doctor jumped to this.

None of that means it did not matter. It means the question you can act on is
the one further up the page: **which step am I on, and what is the next one?**
That one has an answer, and somebody can give it to you this week.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
There is no outlook yet, and that is not the page being careful. It is that
outlook is attached to a diagnosis, and you do not have one.

The temptation is to look up the worst thing on the list you were given. It
answers a question nobody has asked about you, and it can cost you the week.

If you are going to read numbers eventually, it is worth knowing what they are
before you meet them. They describe a **group** of people who all had the same
diagnosis, and they are gathered over years, so they describe people treated a
while ago.

Two words come up over and over, and they are worth having now. **Median** is
the middle of that group: half of the people did better than it, half did worse.
**Five-year survival** is the share of a group still there at that mark, worked
out from people diagnosed long enough ago for anybody to know.

What almost nothing says out loud is that these are wide spreads, and the spread
runs a long way in the good direction as well as the other one. A figure from
the middle of a group is a picture of that group. It is not a prediction about
one person, and it never was.

**And there is a problem with numbers that is specific to where you are
standing.** Your list may hold possibilities with completely different answers.
An average across them would be a number about nobody at all.

The question worth keeping is the same one, asked later and asked of a person:
what does this mean for me? Save it for the appointment where somebody can
actually answer it.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three things below belong to this stretch rather than to tumors in general.

**The job this week is writing it down.** Go to the appointment, write down what
is said and who said it, and come out with the answer to which step they are on.
Then the two of you are working from a note rather than from two different
memories of the same ten minutes.

**You will want to look it up, and there is one search to avoid.** Reading about
the most frightening thing on the list answers a question nobody has asked about
them yet, and it can take the week. If you are going to read, read about the
step they are actually on.

**You are allowed to call the team yourself, between appointments.** Everything
above is about the room. This is the other half of it: if something happens at
home, a seizure most of all, you are the only person who saw it, and nobody is
going to think to ask you about it at the next appointment. Write down what you
saw and what time it was, and use the number that day.

## What to ask your team

- Which step am I on now, and what is the next one?
- What are the possibilities you are working from, including the ones that are
  not cancer?
- Can the scan alone answer this, or will you need a piece of it?
- If you need a piece, is that a needle or an operation, and why that one?
- Has my scan been looked at by the specialist team yet, and when did that
  happen?
- Is my case going to a tumor board, and when?
- Who do I call before then, and what would make you want me to call today?
- What is the after-hours number?
- Can I have copies of my scan and my report?
- Do you think this started in my brain, or somewhere else, and how will you
  tell?
- If it turns out to be unusual, would you send it somewhere else for another
  look?
- Who would you suggest if I wanted another opinion on the plan?
