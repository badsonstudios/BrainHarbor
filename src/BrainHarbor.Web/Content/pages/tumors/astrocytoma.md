## The short version

!%astrocyte%!%circumscribed%
**Three different groups of tumors share the name astrocytoma**, and they are
not variations of one thing. One group includes a tumor surgery can cure.
Another cannot be cured, and is treated over years. Which one you have is set by
your grade and your gene results, both of which are on your pathology report.
This page helps you work out which one is yours. It also covers the name changes
that land here more than anywhere else, including the one that moved a whole
group of tumors out from under the name **glioblastoma**.

## What is an astrocytoma?

Your brain has nerve cells and helper cells packed around them. One kind of
helper cell is shaped like a star, and is called an **astrocyte**. An
astrocytoma is a tumor that starts in those cells, or in cells that look like
them. It is one kind of [glioma](/tumors/glioma).

### The three groups, and why the difference matters

- **Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant.** This is the group most of this page is about, and
  the one an adult is usually pointed to today. It carries a change in a gene
  called IDH, it grows out into the brain around it, and it is graded 2, 3 or 4.
- **The ones with a clear edge.** A separate group sits in a more defined lump
  with a border, rather than spreading out. Your team may call these
  **circumscribed**. **Pilocytic astrocytoma** is the one in this group you are
  most likely to see named. It is grade 1, and an operation that takes all of it
  out can be the end of it. **But this group is mixed.** Others in it are graded
  2 or 3, and for those an operation is the start rather than the finish. So it
  is the grade on your report, not the word circumscribed, that tells you what
  surgery can do.
- **The ones usually seen in children.** These have their own biology and their
  own names. Some are grade 1. Some are not, and a few are among the hardest
  tumors there are. The name alone does not tell you which.

**So the grade and the gene results tell you more than the word astrocytoma
does.** If you have been told only the name, the question that gets you further
is: **is mine IDH-mutant, what grade is it, and does it have a clear edge?**

### Is an astrocytoma the same thing as a glioblastoma?

They are related, and the honest answer has two halves.

Biologically, a glioblastoma is an astrocytic tumor. The American Cancer Society
puts it plainly: glioblastoma is the most common type of astrocytoma.

But since 2021 they are **separate diagnoses**, and the dividing line is the IDH
gene. A tumor with an IDH change is never called glioblastoma now, whatever its
grade. A tumor without one may be called
[glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma) even at grade 4 of the same-looking
disease. So if your report says astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4, you do not
have a glioblastoma. Research written about glioblastoma is not written about
you.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Some are and some are not, and your grade is what decides it.** Grade 1
astrocytomas with a clear edge are usually called benign. Grade 2 and above
IDH-mutant astrocytomas are cancer.

The word for the ones that are cancer is **malignant**. The word for the ones
that are not is **benign**. **Benign in the brain does not mean harmless.** A
benign tumor still takes up room inside a closed skull, and it can still press
on a part of the brain that matters.

**Grade 1 astrocytomas usually sit in a lump with an edge.** Pilocytic
astrocytoma is the usual example. If a surgeon can safely reach all of it, an
operation can genuinely be the end of it. For those, "they got it all" can mean
finished.

**Grade 2 and above IDH-mutant astrocytomas are different.** They grow out into
the brain around them, so there is no edge to cut around. They are malignant,
and with today's treatments they are not curable. That is a hard sentence and it
is here on purpose. Being told "low grade" and hearing "not serious" is the
commonest mistake people make about these tumors. It falls apart later, at the
worst possible time.

**Not curable is not the same as untreatable, and it is not a timeline.**
Treatment can work for a long time, and some of these tumors are watched rather
than treated for years. What it means is that the plan assumes the tumor will
need attention again, which is why the scans do not stop.

### What the grade actually describes

A grade describes how abnormal the cells look under a microscope, and what the
gene tests say. For an IDH-mutant astrocytoma there are three:

- **Grade 2.** Low cellularity, little or no sign of the cells dividing.
- **Grade 3.** More crowded, and clearly dividing.
- **Grade 4.** Dead tissue inside the tumor, or new abnormal blood vessels, **or**
  both copies of a gene called CDKN2A/B missing.

**That last one catches people out.** A tumor can be graded 4 on the gene result
alone, even when the cells did not look that way. It is not a mistake and nobody
was hiding anything. The gene results took longer to come back than the first
impression did, and they carry more weight.
[The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes CDKN2A/B and
the rest one at a time.

Grading is also done **inside each tumor type** since 2021, so a grade 4
astrocytoma and a grade 4 of some other tumor do not describe the same thing.
Comparing your grade with somebody else's, when the names differ, tells you less
than it looks like it does.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

An IDH-mutant astrocytoma can occur anywhere in the brain or spinal cord, but it
is usually **near or in the frontal lobes**, the front of the brain. That
matters more than a location usually does, and the next section is about why.

[MECHANISM]

### The change that came before the diagnosis

**Tumors at the front of the brain are the ones linked to changes in behavior
and personality.** Those changes can come on slowly enough that nobody connects
them to illness.

The National Brain Tumor Society puts it plainly: with tumors in the frontal
lobe, **changes in behavior and personality can be present months to years
before diagnosis**.
Subtle changes in speech, vision or sensation can be there beforehand too.

After a diagnosis, families often look back and see it. Months or years of
someone being short-tempered, withdrawn, flat, or just not themselves. Seeing
that can bring a lot of guilt with it, on all sides.

So it is worth saying directly:

- **The person was not choosing it.** What looked like character was in some
  part a tumor in the part of the brain that produces character.
- **Nobody missed something obvious.** These changes look exactly like stress,
  depression, burnout or a difficult patch in a relationship, because that is
  what they look like from the outside.
- **It is worth telling your team what you noticed and when.** It helps them,
  and it is often the first time anybody has said it out loud.

If this is still going on, say so at appointments. Some of it can be helped, and
some of what looks like the tumor turns out to be the steroids or the seizure
medicine instead.

## What symptoms does it cause?

Seizures are common at diagnosis. Alongside them: headaches, trouble finding
words, trouble understanding speech, changes in vision, and weakness or numbness
on one side. So are the changes in behavior and thinking described above.

### When to call for help right now

Some things should not wait for the next appointment.

**Call an ambulance for any of these:**

- A **first ever seizure**.
- A seizure that lasts more than five minutes.
- One seizure straight after another, with no waking up in between.
- Somebody who cannot be woken.
- Trouble breathing, or somebody who seems to be choking.

**Call your team the same day for any of these:**

- A headache much worse than usual, or one that wakes you from sleep.
- Being sick over and over.
- New weakness in the face, an arm or a leg.
- New trouble speaking, or understanding what people say.
- New confusion.
- Sleeping much more than being awake.
- A sudden change in your vision.

**If you are having chemotherapy, a fever is its own rule.** It means calling
straight away, at any hour, not waiting for the morning.
[The fever rule](/treatments/chemotherapy#fever-rule) explains why.

**Same day means today, not the next clinic appointment.** Ask your team now for
the number to call out of hours, and put it somewhere you will find it at two in
the morning rather than somewhere sensible.

[What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) has the step-by-step, and
[getting help now](/get-help-now) is the short version to keep on a phone.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

A scan first, then a piece of the tumor.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** shows where it is and something about how it is
  behaving.
- **A tissue sample**, taken at [an operation](/treatments/craniotomy) or by a
  needle biopsy. A scan can suggest an astrocytoma. It cannot name it.
- **Gene tests on that tissue.** For this tumor they decide the diagnosis, and
  often the grade as well.

The answer arrives in layers rather than all at once, and the name can change as
later results come back, which on this page is not a small thing.
[Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is about that wait.

## What do the words on my report mean?

Four results do most of the work on an astrocytoma report.

- **IDH.** The one that decides everything else. An IDH change makes this an
  astrocytoma rather than a glioblastoma.
- **1p/19q.** If these are **not** co-deleted and there is an IDH change, the
  tumor is an astrocytoma. If they are, it is an
  [oligodendroglioma](/tumors/oligodendroglioma) instead.
- **ATRX and TP53.** These usually back up the astrocytoma answer. Most of these
  tumors lose ATRX staining and show strong p53, and that pattern helps separate
  them from oligodendroglioma.
- **CDKN2A/B.** If both copies are gone, the grade is 4, however the cells look.

Each has its own entry on
[the words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers).
[Your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the
document itself.

### The renames that land on this page, and they run both ways

More retired names point here than to any other page, and **one direction is
reassuring and the other is not**. Both are below, because finding out the hard
one by accident is worse.

**If your old report said glioblastoma with an IDH change, you are in the right
place.** That tumor is now called **astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4**. Nothing
about it changed. It was moved out from under the glioblastoma name. These
tumors were found to behave differently from the ones still called
glioblastoma. You have not been given a different disease, and you have not been
downgraded or upgraded. It was renamed.

**You may also see the older phrase secondary glioblastoma.** It meant a
glioblastoma that had grown out of a lower-grade tumor, so it described how the
tumor arose rather than what its genes said. **Which page is yours comes down to
the IDH line on your report.** IDH-mutant means this page. IDH-wildtype means
[glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma). If your old paperwork uses that phrase and
you cannot find an IDH result anywhere, that is a good thing to ask about.

**And here is the direction nobody warns people about.** If your tumor is IDH-
**wildtype**, meaning it does not carry an IDH change, the 2021 rules may call
it a **glioblastoma**, grade 4, on gene results alone. That happens when the
tumor has certain changes: a gain of chromosome 7 with a loss of chromosome 10,
extra copies of a gene called EGFR, or a change in the switch that turns on a
gene called TERT. It happens **even when the cells did not look
grade 4** under the microscope.

So some people whose report once said "anaplastic astrocytoma" are now told they
have a glioblastoma. **That is a real change in what you are called, and it is
fair to be shaken by it.** It is not a new tumor and it is not something that
was missed. It is the same tissue, read against rules that now weigh the gene
results more heavily. If this is you,
[glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma) is the page you want, and the question to
ask your team is: **is mine IDH-mutant or IDH-wildtype, and which name applies
to me now?**

## How is it usually treated?

The plan depends on your grade, your gene results, where the tumor sits, and how
much of it can safely be removed.

- **[Surgery](/treatments/craniotomy)**, taking out as much as is safe and
  getting tissue for testing. For a grade 1 tumor with a clear edge, a complete
  operation can be the end of it.
- **[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy)**, usually after surgery,
  for people whose situation calls for treating now.
- **[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy)**, as pills or into a vein.
- **Watching, with scans**, which is a real plan for some grade 2 tumors rather
  than an absence of one. [Low-grade glioma](/tumors/low-grade-glioma) goes
  through what watch and wait involves.
- **Vorasidenib**, a newer medicine taken by mouth, for some people with a grade
  2 IDH-mutant tumor after surgery. It works on the IDH change itself.

Alongside these there is supportive treatment: medicine to bring swelling down,
medicine to control seizures if you have had one, and help with tiredness.

**Clinical trials are a normal part of this conversation**, at diagnosis and
again later.

### Why the grades are treated differently

These three are for the IDH-mutant group. A circumscribed tumor of the same
grade is treated differently, so ask which applies to you.

- **Grade 2, IDH-mutant.** Often surgery, then either watching or treatment,
  depending on what is left behind and how the tumor behaves. Vorasidenib may
  be an option.
- **Grade 3, IDH-mutant.** Radiation **first**, then chemotherapy afterwards.
  Giving the drug during the radiation as well was tested for years and did not
  help, so it is usually left out.
- **Grade 4, IDH-mutant.** Treated along the lines of any
  [high-grade glioma](/tumors/high-grade-glioma), but as its own diagnosis
  rather than as a glioblastoma.

If your plan looks different from someone else's with the same grade, the
question that gets you the answer is: which exact diagnosis is mine, and why
this plan for it?

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

The library pages go through the day-to-day of each one.
[What the operation involves](/treatments/craniotomy), and the weeks after it.
[What a course of radiation is like](/treatments/radiation-therapy) from the
inside. [What chemotherapy does to your blood counts](/treatments/chemotherapy).

Two things particular to this group.

**Finishing treatment is not the same as feeling finished.** Tiredness after
radiation often arrives late and lingers. Thinking and memory can feel slower
for a while. Neither means treatment failed.

**"Is this the tumor, or the medicine?"** matters more here than on most pages,
because the front of the brain is already involved. Steroids cause muscle
weakness and can change mood. Seizure medicines can affect temper and
concentration. If someone seems more irritable after treatment starts, that is
worth asking about rather than assuming the worst.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

**Seizures and driving.** The rules depend entirely on where you live, and in
some places it is your doctor who has to report it rather than you.
[Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) covers the practical side, and
[the driving section](/seizures/living-with#driving) is where to start before
confirming it with your own team.

**Work and money.** Many people keep working, sometimes with changes. Where the
front of the brain is involved, the difficulty is often not the tasks themselves
but planning, starting things, and holding on to attention. Those are worth
naming at work rather than pushing through, because adjustments exist for them.

**Relationships.** This is the one people do not expect. Changes in temper,
motivation and warmth are hard on the people closest to you, and they often
started before anyone had an explanation. Saying out loud that some of it was
the tumor can matter a great deal to both sides.

**Tiredness and thinking.** Both are common and both are easy to blame on the
wrong thing. Say them out loud to your team. There are things that help, and
nobody offers them if they do not know.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans continue whether or not you are having treatment, and for some people they
*are* the plan.

The wait has a name: **scanxiety**. For most people the hardest part is not the
scan but the gap between the scan and the result. Ask who will give you the
result and roughly when, and ask one more thing: will it appear in your online
records before anybody calls you? For a lot of people it does.

If something changes between appointments, you do not have to wait. Bringing a
scan forward is an ordinary thing to ask for.

## If it comes back, or changes

**Some grade 2 astrocytomas change into a higher grade over time.** That is
called transformation, and it can go to grade 3 or straight to grade 4. It is a
known behavior of these tumors rather than a sign something went wrong, and it
is one of the things each scan is looking for. If it happens, the treatment
options are the ones on this page, in a different order.

For grade 3 and grade 4, the plan assumes the tumor will need attention again
from the start. That is why the scans continue.

If something does change, there are usually still options. More surgery.
Radiation, if it has not been used or can be used again. A different drug. A
clinical trial. Which of them are open to you depends on your diagnosis, what
you have had already, and how well you feel day to day.

Being told there has been a change is a hard day. It is not the same as being
told there is nothing left to try.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before deciding whether you want them.

They come from large groups of people treated years ago, and that matters more
here than on most pages. The 2021 rules moved a whole set of tumors out of the
glioblastoma group and into this one. So an older figure may describe a group
that included people who would be given a different diagnosis today, or exclude
people who would now be given yours.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. Half
did better and half did worse. It is not a prediction about any one person, and
it is not a number of months anybody was handed.

The other thing worth knowing is the shape of the group. These figures are not
spread evenly around the middle. There is a long tail of people who do far
better than the middle, and those are real people rather than exceptions to be
set aside. A median cannot show you that tail, which is one of the reasons it is
a poor thing to hear on its own.

What shapes your own situation most is your exact diagnosis, your grade, your
gene results, how much of the tumor came out, and how it behaves over the first
few years. Those are things your own team can see, and a page written for
everybody cannot.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

**This page has a section most tumor pages do not need, and it is for you.** If
the tumor is at the front of the brain, the changes you have been living with
may go back months or years. A shorter temper. Less interest in things. Less
warmth. Plans not made, or not kept. You may have spent that time thinking the
relationship was in trouble, or that you were failing at it.

**You were not.** Some of what you were dealing with was, in some part, a tumor
in the part of the brain that shapes personality. Knowing that afterwards is its
own kind of hard, and it is worth saying to somebody rather than carrying it
quietly.

**What helps, practically:**

- **Write down what you noticed and when**, and take it to appointments. You saw
  things a clinic never will, and it genuinely helps the team.
- **Ask which changes are the tumor, which are the medicines, and which are
  treatable.** Steroids and seizure medicines both change mood and temper, and
  those can often be adjusted.
- **Ask the team what to expect after surgery near the front of the brain.**
  Some changes settle over weeks. Knowing which ones are expected stops every
  bad day looking like bad news.
- **Ask for help before you need it.** Care that involves personality change is
  harder to talk about than lifting and appointments. It is the kind people most
  often try to do alone.

**If seizures are part of this**, learn what to do before you need to.
[What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is the one to know.

Look after your own health, and keep your own appointments.

## What to ask your team

- Is mine IDH-mutant or IDH-wildtype?
- What grade is it, and what set the grade, the cells or a gene result?
- Does it have a clear edge, or does it spread into the brain around it?
- My older paperwork says something different. Which name applies to me now?
- What were the ATRX, TP53 and CDKN2A/B results?
- Are we watching it or treating it now, and why that?
- Am I someone vorasidenib could apply to? If not, what is that based on?
- Some of what changed before the diagnosis, was that the tumor?
- What should I do about driving and work?
- What would make you want to see me before my next appointment?
- Who do I call out of hours, and what number is it?
- Is there a clinical trial I should know about?
