## The short version

A brain metastasis is the cancer you already have, growing in a new place. It
is not a second, different cancer, and it is not a brain tumor that started in
your brain. **It keeps the name of where it started, and that name is what
decides your treatment.** Breast cancer that reaches the brain is still breast
cancer, and is treated with what works for breast cancer. It is the most common
kind of brain tumor there is. More people have one of these than have a tumor
that started in the brain.
**This is a serious thing to be told, and this page is not going to pretend
otherwise.** What it can do is set out what changes the plan: how many spots
there are, where they sit, and whether any of your medicines can reach the brain
at all. That last one is a question worth asking out loud.

## What is a brain metastasis?

It is cancer that started somewhere else and travelled.

Cells break away from the first cancer, move through the blood, and settle in
the brain. They keep behaving like the cancer they came from. So what grows in
the brain is made of breast cells, or lung cells, or melanoma cells, not of
brain cells.

You will hear several words for the same thing. **Metastasis** is one of them.
**Brain mets** is what most teams say out loud. **Secondary brain tumor** means
the same, and so does **secondary**. None of them is worse or better than the
others. They are just different rooms' habits.

Lung cancer, breast cancer and melanoma are the ones that reach the brain most
often. They are not the only ones. **If your cancer is not on that short list,
this page is still about you**, because almost any cancer can do this and the
plan is built the same way.

## Why it is still called by the other cancer's name

This is the part that confuses nearly everybody, and it is worth a minute.

A cancer is named for where it began. Not for where it has got to. Breast
cancer that spreads to the brain is called breast cancer that has spread, and
the treatment follows breast cancer. **If you are told "it is in your brain
now", that is a statement about the address, not about the disease changing
into something else.**

That is why your paperwork keeps naming the first cancer. It is not an error,
and nobody has forgotten to update it.

### Does this mean it will keep spreading?

Two different questions get mixed up here, so they are worth separating.

**Can a tumor that STARTED in the brain travel out to the rest of the body?**
That one is not about you. [Gliomas](/tumors/glioma) answers it, and explains
why tumors that start in the brain are given a grade instead of a stage.

**Can the cancer you have spread somewhere else again?** Yes. It already has
once, and that is what the word metastatic means. Nobody can tell you whether
or where it will happen again, which is why new symptoms get looked at rather
than waited out. That is a hard sentence and it is the true one.

Your report may say **stage 4**, or **advanced**, or **metastatic**. All three
are about the first cancer having travelled. They are not a countdown, and they
are not a grade.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

A metastasis can land almost anywhere in the brain, and there is often more
than one. What you notice depends on where they are, and there is a map further
down this page setting out what each part of the brain does.

**One thing about that map works differently here.** Landing in more than one
place means the symptoms can come from more than one place at once, which is why
a list of them can feel like it is describing several different people rather
than you.

**The size of a spot on your scan is not the whole story.** A small one in a
busy place can cause more trouble than a larger one somewhere quiet. And the
swelling around a spot can cause as much as the spot itself, which is why a
medicine that brings swelling down can change how somebody feels within a day.

### And why does that cause symptoms at all?

[MECHANISM]

## What symptoms does it cause?

There is no single list, because it depends on where they are. The ones that
turn up across many places are headache, feeling sick to your stomach, a
seizure, an arm or leg that feels weak or numb, poor balance, a change in what
you can see, and shifts in thinking or mood that the people around you often
notice first.

Some metastases are found on a scan before they have caused anything at all.
That is common, and it is a better place to start from.

**If you have cancer and your legs go weak, or you lose control of your bladder
or bowel, that is its own emergency.** Cancer can press on the spinal cord as
well as on the brain, and the signs are new weakness in the legs, a change in
how you walk, numbness around the saddle area, and any new trouble with your
bladder or bowel. **New weakness or new bladder trouble is a reason to be seen
quickly, not to wait for the next appointment.** Do not wait for it to get bad
first, because the early signs are often vague ones.
[Spinal cord tumors](/tumors/spinal-cord-tumor) has the rest of it.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this?

Nearly always an MRI, with contrast dye. On a scan, metastases look like round
spots with a clear edge, frequently more than one, with swelling around them.
The pattern is clear enough that a team can often say what they are looking at
before anything is taken out.

**Sometimes the brain scan is what finds the cancer in the first place.** Some
people arrive at this page having had no idea they had cancer at all. A scan
for headaches, or after a fall, shows something, and the search for where it
started begins from there. That is not rare, and usually it means nobody had a
reason to look at your brain before now.

If that is you, expect scans of the rest of your body, and blood tests, and
probably a sample of tissue. **In most people that search finds the starting
point, and finds it within a few months.** In a smaller number it does not, even
after a thorough look. That situation has a name, **cancer of unknown primary**,
and it is a known situation rather than a failure. Treatment does not wait for
the answer, and testing the tumor itself can still point to what will work.

[MRI scans](/tests/mri) describes the machine and the appointment.
[Biopsy](/tests/biopsy) covers how tissue is taken, and
[the pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) covers what comes back.

## What do the words on my report mean?

Paperwork here follows the rules of your first cancer, so it will not look
like a report for a tumor that started in the brain. These are the words that
come up most.

**Metastasis, metastases, mets.** One of them, several of them, and the word
your team will say out loud.

**Solitary or single.** One spot in the brain. **Multiple** means more than one.
Teams count them, because the count changes which treatments are on the table.

**Mass effect.** The tumor is pushing on something rather than just sitting
there. It describes the crowding, not the cancer.

**Vasogenic edema.** Swelling in the brain tissue around a spot. It is treatable
and it often improves faster than the tumor does.
[Steroids](/treatments/steroids) covers the medicine used for it and what it is
like to take.

**Leptomeningeal.** The cancer is in the thin coverings and the fluid around the
brain rather than in lumps. That is a different situation and it has its own
section below.

**Stage 4, advanced, metastatic.** All three mean the first cancer has
travelled. They say nothing about how fast anything is growing.

## Who is looking after me now?

Something changes here that is rarely spelled out.

**If you already have a cancer team, they are not replaced.** The people who
treat brain metastases are added to them. A neurosurgeon, a radiation oncologist
and a neuro-oncologist join the conversation, and the plan for your cancer as a
whole is still built around the cancer you started with.

**If this is all new, your team is being built this week**, and it is being built
from both ends at once: somebody for the cancer wherever it started, and
somebody for the brain. That is disorienting, and it is not a sign that nobody
is in charge.

What that means in practice is that you now have more than one team, and more
than one clinic, and that is exactly the situation where something gets dropped
between two sets of notes.

**So ask this, early: who is coordinating?** Ask for a name and a number, and
ask which team to call about what. It is an ordinary question and it saves a
great deal later.

## How is it usually treated?

There are four things on the table, and which of them applies turns on how many
spots there are, where they are, and what your first cancer responds to.

**Medicine for the swelling.** Often the first thing, and often the fastest to
help. It treats the swelling rather than the tumor, and it can lift symptoms
quickly. [Steroids](/treatments/steroids) covers the whole of that.

**Surgery.** Considered when there are only a few spots, when one of them can be
reached safely, and when it is causing trouble by itself. It is also how tissue
is taken when nobody yet knows where the cancer began.
[Brain surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) covers what an operation involves.

**Focused radiation.** Aimed at the spots rather than at the whole brain. It is
the usual choice when there are only a few of them. It can often be given in one
visit, or in a small number.

**Whole-brain radiation.** The whole brain is treated, not only the spots,
usually because there are too many to aim at one at a time. Healthy tissue gets
the dose as well, so this is a genuine trade-off and not a formality.
[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy#whole-brain-radiation) sets
out what it involves, **and it carries a question to ask by name about
protecting memory**. Read that before the planning appointment rather than
after it.

**Where the line falls between a few and many is your team's to draw.** It has
moved over the years, and it depends on the size of the spots and on how the
rest of your cancer is doing, so a number from a web page is not your number.

Ask about trials here, not at the end.
[Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) explains why an early question
is a better question.

## Is there a medicine that reaches the brain?

**This is the section with something in it to actually do. It ends in a
question, and the question has a real answer.**

Not every cancer medicine gets into the brain. The brain is guarded by a lining
that keeps many drugs out, and a drug that has been working well on the rest of
your cancer may be doing less inside your head. That is a property of the
medicine, not a sign that anything is being done badly.

The ones that do get in tend to be the newer drugs aimed at a specific change in
the cancer's genes. **Which of those is even possible for you depends on
whether your cancer has been tested for that change.** Testing is routine for
some cancers and not for others, and it is not always repeated once somebody has
been on treatment for a while.

So the question to ask, in those words, is: **"Has my cancer been tested for a
marker with a drug that reaches the brain?"**

And there is a second half. **The tumor in your brain can carry a different
change from the one you started with.** If tissue was
taken from your brain, it is fair to ask whether that tissue was tested too, and
not only the original sample.

[What the markers mean](/tests/molecular-markers) explains the testing itself.

**None of this means there is a pill for brain metastases.** It means there is a
question with a real answer, the answer differs from person to person, and
nobody will ask it for you.

## If the cancer is in the covering rather than in lumps {#leptomeningeal}

This is a different thing from the spots described above, and it is worth its own
section because it is handled differently and because it is easy to miss.

Your brain and spinal cord float in fluid, inside thin coverings. Cancer cells
can settle in those coverings and travel in that fluid instead of forming lumps.
Doctors call it **leptomeningeal disease**, or leptomeningeal metastasis. Breast
cancer, lung cancer and melanoma are the ones that do it most often.

**The clue is symptoms in several places at once.** Rather than one problem you
could draw on a map, there are a few: double vision, a drooping or weak face,
hearing trouble, a headache with vomiting, and confusion. The nerves that leave
the base of the skull are often involved, and they are why a person may have
eye, face and hearing trouble together.

Leg weakness and bladder trouble can also come from this, and those two are
handled differently. They are in the urgent list above rather than here.

Finding it takes two tests, and both are worth understanding before you have
them.

- **An MRI** of the brain and often the spine.
- **A spinal tap**, where a small amount of the fluid is taken from your lower
  back with a needle and the cells in it are looked at.

**A clear spinal tap is not an all-clear.** The
test misses this often enough that a single clear result settles nothing. Doing
it again finds cases the first one missed, and a scan can show it when the fluid
looks normal, and the fluid can show it when the scan looks normal. The two
tests are used together on purpose.

**So if your symptoms are still there and you have been told a tap was clear, it
is fair to ask whether it should be repeated.** That is not arguing with your
team. It is the same question they would ask each other, and the guidance they
work from says plainly that a negative result does not rule this out.

Treatment is different here too. It may involve radiation aimed at the places
causing trouble, and medicine given in a way that gets into the fluid itself,
and which of those applies varies a great deal. Ask what is being aimed at, and what it is expected to do, because the
goals are often about symptoms rather than about the scan.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

Focused radiation is usually short. Often one visit, sometimes a few, with a
mask made to hold your head still. Tiredness afterwards is common and it can
arrive days later rather than on the day.

Whole-brain radiation runs over more days, and the tiredness builds. Hair loss
is usual, and it does grow back. The tiredness can go on for weeks
after the last session, and it surprises people who were expecting the end of
treatment to feel like an end.
[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) goes through the mask, the
weeks, the hair and when it comes back.

If you have surgery, expect a deep tiredness that comes in waves, a sore scar,
patchy concentration, and odd sleep for a while.

**One more, and it catches people out.** You are probably still having
treatment for your first cancer at the same time. Two sets of side effects
overlap, and it is genuinely hard to tell which is causing what. Say so at the
appointment rather than working it out alone. It is useful information, and it
changes what your team is able to offer you.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

Nobody is ready for how tired this is. It is not the same as needing an early
night, and it is rarely down to one cause you can point at. Plan around it
rather than through it.

A seizure is possible with metastases in some places.
Read [the steps to take when one happens](/seizures/what-to-do) now rather than
later, and [living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) picks up everything in
between.
[Anti-seizure medicines](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) goes through the
medicines, and whether you need one at all.

Whether you can drive is decided where you live, and a seizure changes the
answer. No web page can tell you yours, and this one will not try.
[Driving and seizures](/seizures/living-with#driving) points you at the right
authority.

Steroids change things while you are on them: sleep, appetite, mood and blood
sugar. That is covered on [steroids](/treatments/steroids), and it is worth
reading because the effects are easy to mistake for the illness.

Trouble with memory, focus and temper is real here, and from the outside it can
look like a person being awkward on purpose. Raise it with the team rather than
absorbing it at home. It is treatable more often than people expect.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

You will have brain scans as well as the scans you already have for your first
cancer. They are often on different clocks, run by different teams, and the
results do not always arrive together.

**Ask who is reading the brain scan and who tells you.** With two teams
involved, this is the most common place for a wait to get longer than it needed
to be.

The waiting is the hard part, not the scanner.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers how scans are compared over
time, and what to do with yourself in between.

One thing worth knowing in advance: after radiation, a treated spot can look
bigger or brighter on a scan without the cancer having grown. Teams expect this
and look at a series of scans rather than one. If a report worries you, ask
whether it is being compared with the last one.

**It is not only a change on the screen, though.** Whatever is causing the spot
to look bigger can crowd the brain beside it, and that can make you feel
different. Tell somebody at the time rather than saving it for the next scan,
because there is usually something to be done about it.
[When a scan looks worse](/tests/follow-up-scans#looks-worse) goes through
it.

## If it comes back, or changes

New spots can appear in the brain even while the rest of your cancer is quiet,
and the reverse happens too. That is the blood-brain lining again, not a sign
that treatment failed everywhere.

**So a change in the brain does not automatically change your whole plan.** It
may mean adding something aimed at the brain, and it may mean revisiting whether
your systemic medicine is one that gets in. Those are two different questions
and it is fair to ask which one is being answered.

If radiation has already been given to an area, that shapes what can be given
again. Surgery, more focused radiation, a change of drug and a trial are all
still possible options. Which of them is open depends on what has already been
done.

## Did I let this happen?

Almost everybody asks some version of this, usually in private, and it deserves
a plain answer rather than a soft one.

**Cancer spreading is something the cancer does. It is not something you did.**
Whether cells travel, and whether they settle and grow once they get somewhere,
depends on the biology of that cancer. It is not a measure of how quickly you
got to an appointment.

**"I should have gone to the doctor sooner."** This is the one that keeps people
awake. Even where an earlier diagnosis would have helped the first cancer, it
does not follow that it would have stopped this. Cells can leave a cancer long
before there is anything to find, and they can also settle years after the first
cancer was treated. Neither of those is a timetable you could have beaten.

**"I stopped a treatment", or "I took a break."** Treatment breaks happen for
real reasons, including side effects that were making life unlivable. A break is
not a cause, and your team made those decisions with you.

**"Somebody missed it."** Sometimes there is a real question to ask here, and
asking it is allowed. Often there is nothing to find: a scan of your chest does
not show your head, and metastases can arrive between two appointments. If you
want to know, ask directly: *was there anything on my earlier scans?* You are
entitled to the answer, and hearing it is usually easier than imagining it.

**"Could I have passed this on?"** Different question, and a real one. A few
cancers run in families, and if yours is one of them your relatives may be
offered testing. That belongs to the team who treat your first cancer rather
than to the brain team, so ask them. And no part of this is catching.

**"I did something to deserve it."** No. Stress does not cause cancer to spread,
diet does not, and neither does attitude. The idea that staying positive changes
the outcome puts the blame in the worst possible place, and it is not true.

Going round this at night is normal, and almost nobody says so. Put it to
somebody on your team in daylight. They have answered it before, and they would
much rather have the conversation than let you keep having it alone.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

Here they go out of date faster than almost anywhere else in cancer, because
what can be offered has changed more than once in the last decade. A figure
worked out from people treated before the newer medicines existed is describing
a world that no longer exists.

And the figures are usually about the first cancer, not about the brain. Two
people with the same number of spots in the brain can be in completely different
positions, because their cancers are different diseases.

What is worth taking from any of it is the shape rather than the value. How your
first cancer is behaving matters more than the count on your brain scan. Whether
there is a medicine that reaches the brain matters. Whether the spots can be
treated one at a time matters. Those three are visible to the people looking
after you, and invisible to any figure you will find written down.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three more, and they come with this situation rather than with tumors in general.

**You are dealing with two teams, and you will be the one holding both.**
Appointments, scans and phone numbers come from more than one place, and nobody
on either side sees the whole of it. Keeping one list, in one place, is worth
more here than anywhere else. Ask each team who their coordinator is.

**Three different things can be behind how they are behaving, and only one of
them is the tumor.** Flatness, a short temper, broken sleep and confusion come
just as easily from a steroid or from the treatment aimed at the first cancer.
They are put right in completely different ways. You are usually the only person
who knows what changed and when. Bring the dates to the appointment, and say it
with them in the room.

**People will hear "it has spread" and treat it as the end of the conversation.**
It is not. There are decisions still being made, and questions still worth
asking, and the person you are caring for is still in the middle of their own
life. You do not have to correct anybody. You just do not have to agree.

## What to ask your team

- Where did this cancer start, and does that change what I am given?
- How many spots are there, and where are they?
- Is surgery, focused radiation or whole-brain radiation being considered, and
  why that one?
- If whole-brain radiation is planned, can it be planned to protect my memory,
  and should I be on memantine?
- Has my cancer been tested for a marker with a drug that reaches the brain?
- If tissue was taken from my brain, was that tissue tested as well?
- Who is coordinating between my cancer team and my brain team?
- Which team do I call about what, and what are the two numbers?
- Am I on a steroid, and how will we get me off it again?
- Could any of this be in the coverings rather than in lumps, and how would we
  know?
- Is there a trial I should be asking about now rather than later?
- Who reads my brain scans, and how will I hear the result?
