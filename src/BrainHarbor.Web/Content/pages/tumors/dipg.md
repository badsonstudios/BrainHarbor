## The short version

**DIPG is a fast-growing tumor in the pons, part of the brain stem.** It is most
often found in children. Most tumors called DIPG are now called [diffuse midline
glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma), H3 K27-altered, and are grade 4. Surgery
cannot safely remove it. **Radiation is the main treatment.** Ask about clinical
trials at the start, without holding up radiation your team says is urgent, and
ask about a palliative care team too.

## What is DIPG?

DIPG stands for **diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma**. It is a tumor in the
**pons**, which is part of the brain stem.

**Diffuse** means it grows out into the healthy tissue around it, like fingers,
rather than sitting in one lump. That is why surgery cannot take it out.

It is most often found in children, and it is rare in adults.

Not every tumor in the brain stem is a DIPG. Some are low-grade tumors with a
different name, and a different plan.

### DIPG and diffuse midline glioma

DIPG is an older name that says **where** the tumor is. Most tumors called DIPG
are now called **diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered**, a name that says
**what** it is. A diffuse midline glioma can also grow in other places, such as
the thalamus or the spinal cord, and those are not DIPG.

[Diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma) covers the name, the
gene change and the grade.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes. DIPG is cancer.** The word your team will use is **malignant**.

**A DIPG with the H3 K27 change is grade 4**, which is the top of the scale.

With today's treatments, DIPG is not curable. That is a hard sentence, and it
is here on purpose, because hearing the grade without hearing this can leave
people shocked later.

**Not curable is not the same as untreatable, and it is not a timeline.**
Treatment can ease symptoms and slow the tumor for a time, and there are still
choices if it grows.

You may read that a DIPG can look low grade. That is about how the cells look.
For a DIPG with the H3 K27 change, it does not change the grade.
[Diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma) explains why.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**It grows in the pons**, in the brain stem. The pons carries the nerves for
vision, hearing, speech, swallowing and movement. It also helps control things
the body cannot do without, such as breathing and heartbeat.

So a tumor there shows up in the eyes, the face, swallowing, speech and balance.
As the pons swells, it can also stop fluid draining from around the brain. The
section below explains what that does.

[MECHANISM]

## What symptoms does it cause?

**Symptoms usually come on fast**, often over about a month.

The commonest are:

- Double vision, or eyes that do not move together.
- One side of the face drooping.
- Trouble with balance or walking.
- Drooling, or trouble swallowing.
- Slurred speech.
- Weakness in an arm and a leg, usually on one side.
- Headaches, especially in the morning, and throwing up.
- Changes in behavior, trouble at school, or being more irritable.

[ESCALATION]

## How do doctors find out it is this? {#finding-out}

**An MRI scan is the main test.** DIPG often has a picture on the scan that
doctors recognize, and it can be diagnosed from the scan and the symptoms alone.

**Whether to do a biopsy is a real choice, and centers differ.**

- In the past, a biopsy was not usually done. It is more common now.
- At experienced centers it can be done safely. There is still some risk,
  because of where the tumor is.
- A biopsy gives the gene result. Many clinical trials need it, and so does the
  newer drug below.
- If the risk of a biopsy is too high, some children go straight to radiation
  without one.

It is fair to ask your team how they weigh it for your child.
[How tissue is taken](/tests/biopsy) explains the needle biopsy itself, and
[the H3 K27 result](/tests/molecular-markers#h3-k27) explains the test.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

- **DIPG**, or **diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma.** A name that says where the
  tumor is.
- **Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered.** The current name, if the tumor was
  tested. The gene change is part of it.
- **[H3 K27M](/tests/molecular-markers#h3-k27).** An older way of writing it.
  Both may describe the same tumor.

If there was no biopsy, the diagnosis comes from the MRI report, and there is no
gene result on paper.

### If your older paperwork says something different

- **Brain stem glioma.** An older, broader name. It can include low-grade tumors
  that are not DIPG, so it is worth asking which one yours is.
- **Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27M-mutant.** The 2016 name for the same tumor.

## How is it usually treated?

[TUMOR-BOARD]

- **Surgery cannot safely remove DIPG**, because of where it is and how it
  spreads. Surgery may still be used to place a [shunt](/treatments/shunts) if
  fluid is blocked.
- **[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy) is the main treatment.** It is
  usually given on weekdays for about six to seven weeks. Some centers use a
  shorter course of about three weeks.
- **[Steroids](/treatments/steroids)** to bring down swelling and ease symptoms.

**One hard thing to know before radiation starts.** Radiation usually eases
symptoms for a time, and the tumor often shrinks for a while. It is not a cure.
Ask your team what radiation is meant to do for your child.

**In general, chemotherapy is not part of standard care for DIPG.**
[Diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma#chemotherapy) has the
honest note about it.

### Dordaviprone, and one thing to know for a tumor in the pons {#dordaviprone}

[Dordaviprone](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma#dordaviprone) is a capsule taken
once a week. It is approved for a diffuse midline glioma with the H3 K27M change
that has kept growing after other treatment.

It was given **accelerated approval**. That means it was approved early, based
on how many tumors shrank and for how long. **Trials are still checking whether
it helps people live longer.** Until they report, nobody can tell you that it
does.

**The studies the FDA used to judge whether it shrinks tumors left out DIPG.**
Most of the people in them were adults. The approval itself does not
exclude DIPG, and the American Cancer Society says it may be an option for a DIPG
that grows after radiation. So the evidence that it shrinks tumors comes from
other tumors, mostly in adults. Ask your team what they know about it for your
child's tumor, and whether your child has the gene result it needs.

### Clinical trials, and why to ask early

**Many children with DIPG get their care through a clinical trial.** There are
trials that start at diagnosis, after radiation, and after the tumor grows.
**Asking at the start keeps the most options open**, as long as it does not hold
up radiation your team says is urgent.
[Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) explains how they work.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

**Radiation for a young child.** A young child who cannot lie still may be given
medicine to sleep through each session. This is called anesthesia. At some
centers, parents can see and talk to their child on a screen from outside the
room. Some centers let a child who is close to managing without it practice lying
still in the empty room first. Ask what your center does.
[What radiation is like](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers the rest.

**Steroids change how your child looks.** Weight gain and a round, puffy face
come mainly from the medicine, not from the tumor.
[Steroids](/treatments/steroids) goes through the rest of what they do.

**The better stretch after radiation.** For many children the tumor shrinks for
a while after radiation. Symptoms ease, and they can go home to their usual
things. Some families hear this called the honeymoon period. **It is hard to
predict.** Some children are almost back to how they were, and some do not
improve at all. Neither says anything about how hard anybody tried.

If symptoms that had eased start to come back, call your team the same day, or
right away if there is a shunt. Anything on the ambulance list is still an
ambulance call.

**Palliative care is worth meeting early.** The word can sound like giving up.
It is not. The team is meant to be there from the start.

## Everyday life: school, seizures and tiredness

- **School.** Changes in behavior and schoolwork can come from the tumor. Ask the
  team who helps with school.
- [What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is worth reading before you
  need it.
- **Tiredness** is common during and after radiation. It can come from the
  treatment, and it can also come from the tumor. Sleeping much more than being
  awake is a same-day call, or a right-away call if there is a shunt.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans continue after radiation. [Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes
through what they are compared with, and
[waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the gap before the

## If it comes back, or changes

If the tumor grows again, there are still choices. Your team may talk about a
clinical trial, **dordaviprone**, radiation again, or care that focuses only on
comfort.

**Radiation a second time is something doctors disagree about.** St. Jude says it
can be done safely, but the tumor is harder to treat the second time and the
benefit is more limited. It is usually given over two to three weeks. A 2022
review of DIPG says it can help slow the tumor, but it has significant risks and
burdens, and is not usually recommended. Ask your team whether it is an option,
and what it would be for.

**No choice here is the wrong one.** Ask your team what each option is for, and
write the answers down.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**Will my other children get this?** This tumor is not passed down in families.
It is nobody's fault, and nothing done in pregnancy or early childhood has been
shown to cause it.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It helps to know what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

Those numbers describe children who were treated years ago. For a long time a
biopsy was not usually done, so many older studies counted tumors by how they
looked on a scan, before the gene test. An older figure may describe a mixed
group.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. It is
not a prediction about any one person.

The shape of the group matters as much as the middle. A few children live well
beyond it. St. Jude says that is usually linked to unusual features of the tumor,
or to a tumor that turned out not to be DIPG. It also says very young children, and
children who had symptoms for longer before diagnosis, may do a little better.
A median cannot show you any of that.

In the better stretch after radiation, families may use the time to be together,
or to do something special. Wish-granting organizations can often help with that.

**Care near the end of life** is something families are encouraged to talk about
early, with the palliative care team, while there is time to think. Hospice care
can happen at home, which is sometimes where a child would rather be, and it can
start while a child is still having treatment. Pediatric hospice keeps supporting
the family after a child dies, too. Ask your team who to talk to, and when.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Two things specific to this diagnosis.

**You are most likely a parent.** Everything above still applies to you, and so
does asking early for the palliative care team. That team is there for the whole
family, not only the child with the tumor.

**Watch swallowing.** New trouble swallowing, or new drooling, means calling your
team the same day. Food or drink can go down into the lungs. Choking, or trouble
breathing, is an ambulance call.

## What to ask your team

- Is this a DIPG, or a different tumor in the brain stem?
- Would a biopsy help, and what are the risks for my child?
- What is radiation meant to do, and how long will it take?
- Will my child need anesthesia for radiation?
- Are there clinical trials we should look at, and do any need to start before
  radiation?
- Is dordaviprone something you would consider for a tumor in the pons?
- When can we meet the palliative care team?
- What should we know about the steroids?
- What would make you want to see us before the next appointment?
- Who do we call after hours, and what number is it?
