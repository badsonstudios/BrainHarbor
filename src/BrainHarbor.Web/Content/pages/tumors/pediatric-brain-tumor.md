## The short version

**This page is for a parent.** The rest of this site is written to the person
with the tumor. Here, that person is your child, and you are the one reading.

**Brain tumors in children are not the same as brain tumors in adults.** The
types are different. The treatment is planned around a brain that is still
growing. And a lot of what you will deal with, like scans that need medicine to
sleep, school, and check-ups that run for years, does not come up for adults at
all.

**This is not one tumor.** It is a way of grouping many of them. Your child's
own tumor has a name, and the page for that name has the detail.

## What "pediatric brain tumor" means

It is not a diagnosis. It is a group, and the thing the tumors in it share is
the age of the person who has one.

Brain and spinal cord tumors are the second most common cancer in children,
after leukemia. There are many types, and they are treated very differently, so
the name on your child's report matters more than the group does.

[The tumor pages](/tumors) list the types this site covers. If you do not have a
name yet, [what happens between the scan and the
name](/tumors/all-brain-tumors) is the page for the days in between.

## Why children are not small adults

**The common types are different.** [Medulloblastoma](/tumors/medulloblastoma)
is far more common in children, and so is an
[ependymoma](/tumors/ependymoma) at the back of the brain. The ependymomas that
grow in the spine are more often found in young adults.
[Glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma) is more common in adults.
[Meningioma](/tumors/meningioma) is much less common in children than in adults.
Tumors that travel to the brain from a cancer elsewhere in the body are rare in
children.

**The same name can behave differently in a child**, and be treated
differently.

!%pediatric-type%!%adult-type%**A report may say "pediatric-type" or "adult-type".** Those words describe the
tumor's biology, not your child's age.
[Glioma](/tumors/glioma) explains what that means and why it is not a mistake on
the report.

**So a study done in adults may not apply to your child.** Check who was in a
study before you carry the result across. That is worth doing even when the
tumor name matches.

## Where it grows, and why that causes symptoms

**Two notes before the next part, because it was written for an adult patient
and you are a parent.** It says "you" and "your tumor", and here the tumor is
your child's. Read those words as "your child". It also opens by saying that
most people are told what they have before anyone explains what it is doing.
That is usually true for you too, and the person it is happening to is somebody
you are looking after.

## What symptoms look like in a child

**What a child notices depends on where the tumor sits, its size, how fast it
grows, and how old they are.** Many childhood brain tumors grow slowly. The symptoms
can be mild, can come and go, and may not follow a pattern. Early on they often
look like ordinary childhood illnesses, which is why they are missed.

In an older child, the things to notice are a headache that keeps coming back,
throwing up, trouble with balance or walking, changes in vision, seizures,
being much more tired, doing worse at school, or not seeming like themselves.

**Some of the signs below carry a different rule if the tumor started in your
child's spinal cord, or has spread there.** It comes just after the lists.

**The two lists that follow are written to the person who has the tumor.** Read
every "you" in them as your child. After them, this section goes back to speaking
to you.

[ESCALATION]

[SPINAL-CORD]

**That last rule only applies if the tumor is in your child's spinal cord, or has
reached the spine.** Some childhood tumors can reach it and some cannot. If you are
not sure whether it applies to your child, ask the team, and read the page for your
child's own tumor.

**In a baby or a toddler, the signs are different, and here is why.** A baby's
skull is not fixed shut yet. It can stretch as the brain is pushed, so the
pressure can build for longer before anything shows. When something does show,
it is often vague. Watch for a head that is growing too fast, a soft spot on
top that bulges, being sleepy or hard to settle, feeding badly, not gaining
weight, or losing a skill your child already had. **Any of those is a same-day
call, or a right-away call if your child has a shunt.**

**A young child who cannot talk yet does not say "my head hurts".** A worsening
headache may show up only as a child who is anxious, irritated or whiny. **That is
a same-day call, or a right-away call if your child has a shunt.** If your child
cannot be woken, that is an ambulance call.

## How doctors find out

The tests are the same ones adults have.
[An MRI scan](/tests/mri) is the main one, and
[a piece of the tumor](/tests/biopsy) is usually what names it.

**What is different is holding still.** A scan only works if your child stays
still, and small children cannot. So many children are given medicine for it.

- **Sedation** makes a child relaxed and sleepy.
- **General anesthesia** puts a child fully to sleep, like a deep sleep.
- **Many children need neither.** Deep breathing, music or a movie is often
  enough, and a child life specialist can practice it with your child first.

**You can usually stay with your child until they are asleep.** Ask.

**There is a reason teams use as little of this as they safely can.** Too much
general anesthesia could affect how a young brain develops, and that concern is
strongest under about age 3. It is a reason to ask what your center does, not a
reason to refuse a scan your child needs.

## What the words on the report mean

[CROSSWALK]

!%embryonal tumor%**One older word turns up a lot in children's tumors.** Some tumors are called
**embryonal tumors**, which means they grow from very early forms of nerve
cells. These used to be called **primitive neuroectodermal tumors**, or
**PNET**. Nobody uses that name for a diagnosis now. You may still meet it in
an older letter, and it is still used in some research papers, so seeing it does
not mean anybody has made a mistake.

**Two tumors with the same name can still be different**, because their gene
results differ. That is why a report can take weeks and why it looks technical.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the document, and
[what the grade means](/tests/pathology-report#what-the-grade-means) covers the
number.

Brain tumor names are genuinely confusing. Asking your team to say the full name
slowly, and writing it down, is a reasonable thing to do.

## How treatment is different for a child

The library pages carry what each treatment is:
[surgery](/treatments/craniotomy),
[radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy),
[proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy) and
[chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy). What follows is only what changes
because the patient is a child.

**Radiation is used carefully in the very young, and sometimes not at all.**
Teams try to avoid it in very young children and to limit it in others, because
of what it does to a developing brain. For children aged about 3 and under,
chemotherapy is often given first, so the brain has more time to grow.
Where exactly that line sits differs between teams and is changing, so ask your
team where they draw it and why.

**Radiation may mean daily anesthesia, for weeks.** A course can run every
weekday for several weeks, and a child who cannot lie still needs medicine to
sleep for each session. The radiation itself takes only a few minutes. The visit
takes much longer. Younger children usually need this, older children often do
not, and some older children still do. Practice varies a lot between centers, so
there is no standard to promise you.

Complications from anesthesia in this setting are uncommon.

**You can ask for things that reduce how much is needed.** Centers report
letting a parent into the treatment area, letting a child use a tablet, and
having a child life specialist there. Any of those is a fair thing to ask for by
name.

**[Proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy) comes up more often for
children**, because sparing healthy tissue matters more when tissue is still
developing. It is worth an honest note: it is clearer that this helps for tumors
with distinct edges than for tumors that grow into normal brain. Ask your team
what it would change for your child's tumor.

**[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy) is given on a cycle**, which is days
of treatment plus rest days. The rest is there on purpose, to let the body
recover.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

### After surgery low at the back of the head {#posterior-fossa-syndrome}

Many childhood brain tumors sit low at the back of the brain, so this is a
common operation in children. If your child's was somewhere else, this section
does not apply.

[POSTERIOR-FOSSA-SYNDROME]

### Giving chemotherapy at home {#chemotherapy-at-home}

If your child takes chemotherapy at home, ask to be shown how to handle it
before you leave.

Chemotherapy can be taken in through the skin or breathed in. Family members can
be exposed if it reaches food or the surfaces you use every day. Your team will
tell you what to do about gloves, spills and washing up, and what to do with
anything left over.

This is worth getting right rather than worrying about, and nobody minds
showing you twice.

## Everyday life: school, and the years after

**School is where late effects usually show up first**, and it is where families
most often go without help they are entitled to. Most children treated for a brain
tumor do not use the school support that exists, even though testing their
thinking and learning is considered standard care after a brain tumor.

**Start early, and do not wait for your child to struggle.** Ask the hospital
who helps with school. A large children's hospital will often have someone whose
job this is, and they can talk to the school for you.

**In the United States there are two routes**, and they are not the same thing.

- An **IEP** is a plan for special education services, under a federal law
  called IDEA. Children aged 3 through 21 come under one part of it. Babies and
  toddlers under 3 come under a different part, for early intervention.
- A **504 plan** is for accommodations, under a different law that forbids
  discrimination. It does not come with funding attached, and an IEP does.

These are US laws. If you are somewhere else, your country will have its own
route, and the hospital is still the place to ask.

**Things that are reasonable to ask a school for:** starting accommodations the
day your child returns rather than after a bad term, less homework at first,
extra time between classes, a second set of books to keep at home, and someone
checking whether your child is simply too tired to work in the evening.

**Ask again each year.** Teachers change, and the research on school support says
the next teacher often is not told any of this.

## Follow-up scans, and the check-ups that come with them

Scans continue for years, and they spread further apart with time.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers what each one is compared
against, and [waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the gap
before the answer.

**Follow-up is not only scans.** A visit usually includes a check of feeling,
strength, balance, walking and reflexes, blood tests including hormone levels,
and a hearing test.

**What gets checked depends on what your child was treated with**, not on what
the tumor was called. That is how the standard US guidelines are built.

- **Hearing.** Some chemotherapy medicines, including cisplatin and
  carboplatin, and radiation to the head can affect hearing. Finding it early is
  what lets a school do something about it.
- **Growth and hormones.** Radiation close to the glands under the brain can
  affect the hormones that control growth and puberty. This is watched for
  years, which is why hormone levels are on the blood-test list above.
- **Thinking and learning.** See the section below.
- **Later cancers.** There is a small added risk many years later, higher after
  radiation. It is a reason follow-up continues into adult life, not a reason to
  expect it.

**Ask for a survivorship care plan.** It is one document listing what your child
was treated with, what to watch for, and what should be checked and when.
Childhood cancer is rare, and a family doctor may see one or two such patients
in a whole career, so the document is often the only way that history travels
with your child. Ask for an updated copy at each follow-up visit, and keep the
records yourself. They matter most when your child is grown and seeing doctors
who know none of this.

**There is good material written for families** rather than for doctors. The
Children's Oncology Group publishes short guides called Health Links on
individual late effects.

## Thinking, learning and memory

This is the part parents most often hear as brain damage, and it usually is not
that.

**For most children, the problem is not that skills are lost.** It is that new
skills are picked up more slowly than before. Children keep learning and keep
developing. They may just do it at a slower rate than the children around them.

The parts most often affected are memory, attention, and being able to plan and
juggle more than one thing.

**It may not show up for months or years**, often when school gets harder rather
than when treatment ends. That delay is normal and it is not a sign that
anything new is wrong.

Risk is higher for children treated when they were very young, and one review
calls radiation to the brain a major contributor. Whether chemotherapy on its own causes
lasting effects is genuinely not settled, and honest sources say so.

**The thing that helps is testing, early.** It turns "he is not trying" into a
list of specific things a school can act on.

## Talking to your child

**You do not have to do this alone, and you do not have to do it perfectly.**
Your team can help with the conversation, or be in the room for it.

**Telling your child is usually better than not.** It is natural to want to
protect a child from this. But children notice. A child who is not told will
fill the gap with their imagination, and what they imagine is often worse than
the truth.

**Children often think it is their fault.** They need to hear plainly that it is
not, and they may need to hear it more than once.

**Say it in words that match their age.** A young child needs very little at
once. A teenager can handle the whole picture and will usually go looking for it
anyway, so it is better to point them somewhere trustworthy.

**Practical things that help:** practice what you plan to say, ask someone to be
with you, and tell your child early rather than late, because it builds trust
you will need later.

**Trust yourself here.** You know your child better than the team does.

## Brothers and sisters

**Tell them too, in words that fit their age.** Siblings are often scared because
they do not understand what is happening, and explaining lessens it.

**Some of them quietly think they caused it**, by something they said or thought
or did. Say out loud that nothing anyone thinks, says or does can make somebody
else ill.

**Keep their life going.** School, hobbies and friends should carry on. It is
not a betrayal of the child who is ill.

**Say the brave thing to them as well.** Siblings hear constantly how strong the
ill child is. They are being strong too, and nobody tells them.

**Ask how they are, and then listen.** Some siblings imagine hospital is fun,
because that is where the presents and the time with you are.

There are support groups specifically for siblings, run online.

## Who is on your child's team

Most children's hospitals have people whose whole job is this.

- **A child life specialist** explains what is happening in words your child
  understands, and can rehearse a scan or a procedure through play first. You can
  ask for them by name.
- **A psychologist** helps with how your child is coping, and with thinking and
  learning.
- **A social worker** helps with the parts nobody warns you about: money,
  travel, housing, work, and finding help near you.

## Palliative care, which is not what it sounds like

**Palliative care can happen at any stage of an illness, at any age.** It is not
only for the last part of one, and asking for it does not mean anyone has given
up.

It is a team whose job is symptoms, side effects and the strain on the family.
It sits alongside the treatment your child is already having.

**One thing here is specific to children.** A review of palliative care for
children with these tumors describes something it calls concurrent care, where
children can receive hospice support while still having treatment aimed at curing
the tumor. Ask your team whether that is how it works where you are.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**For childhood brain tumors specifically:** most children who get one have no
known risk factor at all. There is nothing you or your child could have done to
prevent it.

No link has been found with cell phones, power lines, chemicals, or anything
your child ate. And the lifestyle risks people associate with adult cancers,
like smoking, drinking, diet and weight, are not known to play a major part in
childhood cancers. If you have been going back over the pregnancy, or the months
before the diagnosis, looking for the thing you did wrong, there is nothing there
to find.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Outlook is usually talked about in numbers. It is worth knowing what kind of
numbers those are before you choose to look at them.

They come from groups of children treated years ago. Both the treatments and the
way tumors are sorted have changed since then, so an old figure can describe a
mix of children that nobody would put in one group now.

The word you will meet is **median**. It means the middle of a group. Line
everybody up, and the median is the one standing in the middle. It describes
that group. It is not a prediction about one child. It also hides the children at
the far end of it, the ones who do much better than the middle.

The honest position is that outlook depends far more on which tumor your child
has than on the fact that your child is a child. That is why it belongs on the
page for the tumor's own name, with your team, and not on a page about an age
group.

Your team knows your child in a way no page can. Ask them whenever you are
ready, and you are allowed to say not yet.
:::

**A note about that section, because this whole page is written to you.**
Everywhere else on this site, the caregiver section is the one part addressed to

Two things specific to being a parent.

finding their way around the hospital. Wanting that is not taking something away

**You are the one holding the whole picture.** Different people check hearing,
hormones, learning and the tumor itself, often in different clinics, sometimes
for years. Keep your own file. Nobody else has all of it.

## What to ask your team

- What is my child's tumor called, with the full name and any gene results?
- Was this treatment plan worked out for children, or from studies in adults?
- Will my child need medicine to sleep for scans, or for radiation?
- Can I stay with my child until they are asleep?
- What is being done to keep radiation away from the rest of the brain?
- Who at the hospital helps with school, and when should we start?
- Who checks hearing, hormones, growth and learning, and how often?
- Can we have a survivorship care plan, and an updated copy each visit?
- Should we meet the palliative care team, and what would they do for us?
- Is there a child life specialist we can see before the next procedure?
- What would make you want us to call before the next appointment?
- Who do we call after hours, and what is the number?
