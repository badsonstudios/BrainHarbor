## The short version

**This is a tumor that is not cancer, and it is full of tiny blood vessels.** It
grows slowly. The usual place is the lower back part of the brain. It also turns
up in the spinal cord.

**Often the trouble comes from a pocket of fluid beside it, not from the tumor
itself.** The symptoms section explains why that matters so much here.

**About 1 in 4 people with one have an inherited condition called von
Hippel-Lindau, or VHL.** Some studies put it closer to 1 in 3. It is worth
asking your team about a test for it, even if nobody in your family has it. The
answer changes what happens next.

**The main treatment is an operation to take the solid part out.** Some people
have a focused kind of radiation instead. Some small tumors that cause no trouble
are only watched.

**Bleeding from one of these is rare, but a sudden, very bad headache means
calling 911.** The other warning signs are in the symptoms section.

## What is a hemangioblastoma?

**It is a lump made of cells and a tangle of very small blood vessels.** Under a
microscope, it is packed with them. That is where the long name comes from.

**It often comes with a pocket of fluid.** On a scan, doctors often see a pocket
with a small solid lump in its wall. Two reviews of this tumor describe that as
the usual picture. The solid lump is the tumor. The pocket is fluid it makes.

**There are two groups of people who get it.** Most people have one tumor, found
as an adult, often in middle age. A smaller group has VHL. They tend to be
younger when the first one is found, and they can get more than one over the
years. This page says which group it means whenever that matters.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**No. It is not cancer.** Cancer Research UK and Cleveland Clinic both say so.
It stays where it started.

**Not cancer does not mean harmless.** Cleveland Clinic says it can still grow
big enough to press on the brain or spinal cord around it and cause serious
problems. That is why it is taken seriously, and why it is treated.

**It is classed as grade 1, the lowest grade.** Two reviews of this tumor say so.
Grade 1 describes a tumor that grows slowly. It is not a promise about any one
person.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**Most often in the cerebellum.** That is the lower back part of the brain, and
it runs balance and coordination. Cancer Research UK says most start there.

**Sometimes in the brainstem,** which joins the brain to the spinal cord and runs
things like swallowing and breathing.

**Sometimes in the spinal cord.** Here it presses on the nerves that run to the
arms and legs. In VHL, the VHL Alliance says the neck is the most common part of
the spine for one.

**People with VHL can also get one at the back of the eye.** That kind is looked
after by an eye doctor. It is one reason eye checks are part of VHL care.

**Why the pocket matters.** NORD, a rare disease group, says the fluid pocket
often causes more symptoms than the tumor does. Pacific Neuroscience Institute
says these tumors usually grow slowly, but the fluid part can get bigger more
quickly, so symptoms can start in a hurry. Low at the back of the brain, a
growing pocket can also block the fluid that drains around the brain, so
pressure builds up. The general part below explains how that works.

## What symptoms does it cause?

**Which ones you get follows the place it grows.**

**In the lower back part of the brain**, signs are usually headaches, feeling
sick or throwing up, feeling dizzy, and trouble with balance. You may walk
unsteadily, or feel clumsy. Any of these that is new is worth a call to your
team, and the next part says which ones cannot wait.

**In the brainstem**, symptoms can be more serious, because so many nerves pass
through that small space. The Brain Tumour Charity says signs from a tumor here
can need emergency care. It does not say which signs or where to go, so treat
anything new here as a call to your team today. And trouble breathing or
choking is an ambulance call however it started, fast or slow. The ambulance
list below, and the two rules after it, say what else cannot wait.

**In the spinal cord**, the signs are in the body rather than the head. Columbia
lists numbness or pins and needles in the arms or legs, clumsy hands, weakness,
trouble walking, and changes in how the bladder and bowel work. Macmillan and
Brain Tumour Research both add new back or neck pain to that list.

**Sometimes a blood test finds too many red blood cells.** This tumor can make a
hormone that tells the body to make more of them. It happens only sometimes.

### When to get help fast

**Something to know first.** Sixteen pages written about this tumor, or about
the brain and spine tumors of VHL, were read and used for this page. They come
from hospitals, brain tumor charities and rare disease groups. Not one of those
sixteen ties a warning sign to 911 or to an emergency room. Three further pages
would not open for us. Those three are unknown: they may say something we have
not seen. We also left out one page family whose rules we are not allowed to
reuse. So ask your own team which signs mean calling them, and which mean the
emergency room, and write the answer down. Nobody else can give you that list
for your own case.

**So no rule below is a rule about the tumor.** Each belongs to a warning sign,
and each names who gives it.

[ESCALATION]

**This tumor adds two rules of its own.**

**A sudden, very bad headache, unlike any you have had.** Bleeding from this
tumor is rare. A study of it says so. But when a blood vessel in the brain breaks
and bleeds, that is a kind of stroke. MedlinePlus lists a sudden, severe headache
with no known cause as a stroke sign, and says to call 911 right away. The Brain
Tumour Charity says a burst blood vessel needs medical help at once, without
saying where to go. **For this one headache, that beats the same-day rule for a
bad headache in the list above.**

**If your tumor is in the spinal cord.** New weakness, new numbness, unsteady
walking, or new back or neck pain means calling your team today.

**One group of cord signs is stronger than that, and it is the one to learn.**
Losing control of peeing or pooping, numbness in the parts of you that touch a
chair, or weakness that is worse each hour: for those three, Cleveland Clinic
says to get to an emergency room immediately, and it lists tumors as one cause.
Those three beat every other rule on this page.
[Spinal cord tumors](/tumors/spinal-cord-tumor) sets out why they are sorted
this way.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

**Usually from a scan.**

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** with contrast dye. The solid part of this tumor
  lights up brightly on the scan, and the fluid pocket does not. That look is a
  strong clue.
- **A scan of the whole spine as well,** if VHL is possible, because tumors can
  be in more than one place.
- **Sometimes an angiogram.** A thin tube is passed into a blood vessel, and dye
  shows how much blood the tumor carries. UCLA says it is often used. It helps
  plan the operation.
- **An eye check** to look at the back of the eye, because of the eye tumors
  that come with VHL.

### Ask about a VHL test

**Ask for a VHL gene test, even if nobody in your family has VHL.** Pacific
Neuroscience Institute says anyone found to have this tumor should be checked
for VHL. A review from the UK says testing is routine there for people found to
have one before age 60, and should be thought about for everyone.

**Why it matters.** About 1 in 5 people with VHL are the first in their family
to have it, MedlinePlus Genetics says. So a family with no history can still
have it. And if you do have it, the plan changes: regular checks of other parts
of the body, and a chance for your family to be tested too.

**A genetic counselor can help you through it.** Cleveland Clinic says one can
explain what the result means for you and for any children.

## What do the words on my report mean?

**Hemangioblastoma.** This is the name the World Health Organization uses.

**Capillary hemangioblastoma, Lindau tumor, and angioblastoma.** Older names for
the same tumor. A review of this tumor says they are no longer the recommended
names. Seeing one on an old report does not mean it was wrong.

**Cyst, and mural nodule.** The cyst is the fluid pocket. The mural nodule is the
small solid lump in its wall, which is the tumor itself.

**Stromal cells.** The tumor cells. The report may say they look clear or foamy.

**Reticular, or cellular.** Two patterns the lab sees under the microscope.

**Sporadic.** The tumor is not linked to VHL.

**VHL-associated.** The tumor comes from VHL.

**Clear cell kidney cancer, or renal cell carcinoma.** You may see this named on
the report as something the lab ruled out. Under a microscope, it can look a lot
like this tumor, so the lab checks. It is mentioned because the two can look
alike, not because you have it. If you are not sure which way your report
reads, ask.

**Grade 1.** The grade section above says what it means here.

[The pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) explains the rest of the report.

## How is it usually treated?

**For a tumor that causes symptoms, an operation is usually the main
treatment.** Cancer Research UK calls surgery the main treatment for this tumor.

### The operation

**The aim is to take out the solid part.** A review by surgeons who treat many of
these tumors says that is the goal. Once the solid part is out, the fluid pocket
usually collapses on its own.

**The blood vessels are the main risk.** Because the tumor is so full of them,
bleeding during the operation is the thing surgeons plan around. The team will
often study the blood vessels on scans first.

**Some people have the blood supply closed off first.** This is called
embolization. Weill Cornell says it is done through the same thin tube used for
an angiogram. The surgeons' review says it can have serious problems of its own,
so it is weighed case by case. Not everyone has it. Ask your team whether it is
part of your plan, and when it would happen.

[Craniotomy](/treatments/craniotomy) covers an operation on the brain, and what
the days after it are like.

### Focused radiation

**Some tumors are treated with one very focused dose of radiation.** This is
called [stereotactic radiosurgery](/treatments/stereotactic-radiosurgery). A
study of it names when it is used: for tumors that are hard to reach, for
several tumors, or for one that comes back.

**It works on the solid part, less on the pocket.** A large review found it did
less to shrink the fluid pockets. So if a pocket is what is causing your
symptoms, ask your team what this treatment would and would not do about it.

Experts do not all agree on when radiation should come first. Ask your team why
they suggest what they do.

### A pill, for people with VHL

**For adults with VHL, there is a pill called belzutifan.** The FDA approved it
in 2021. It is for brain and spinal cord tumors that need treatment but do not
need an operation right away. It works by blocking a signal these tumors use to
grow. [Targeted therapy](/treatments/targeted-therapy) explains how drugs like
this work.

**It is not approved for a tumor without VHL.** A 2026 report says whether it
helps those tumors is not known.

**It can harm an unborn baby.** The drug's label says it must not be taken in
pregnancy, and it can stop some hormone birth control from working. Ask what
kind of birth control to use.

**It can cause low red blood cells, and low oxygen.** The label says both can be
severe. Tell your team if you feel much more tired, short of breath, or dizzy.

### Watching instead of treating

**Some tumors are watched rather than treated.** This is most common with VHL.
The VHL Alliance says these tumors are usually not treated until they cause
symptoms, or are growing fast enough to cause harm soon. Cleveland Clinic says the
same can apply to anyone without symptoms. A very small one that is not growing
may just be watched, Cancer Research UK says.

**Watching is a plan, not doing nothing.** It means scans on a schedule, and
acting when something changes. [Watch and wait](/treatments/watch-and-wait)
explains how it works.

**With VHL, the goal is to treat each tumor at the right time.** Over a lifetime
there may be several. The VHL Alliance says to look at all three choices, surgery,
radiation and medicine, each time one needs treating.!%tumor board%

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

**Balance can be off after an operation low at the back of the brain.** That is
the part of the brain that runs balance. A physical therapist can help you work
on it, and the National Brain Tumor Society says exercises set for you are part
of getting your footing back. Ask whether you will be seen by one.

**New weakness or clumsiness that keeps getting worse is not the same thing.**
Call your team the same day for new weakness. If you have had surgery on the
spinal cord, the emergency room rule above holds afterwards as well.

**Make the home safer while you recover.** The National Brain Tumor Society
suggests things like grab bars and handrails.

**If fluid was backing up before the operation,** taking the tumor out may not be
the end of it. A guide written for doctors says blocked fluid here needs an
operation of its own, such as a thin tube to drain it, called a shunt. Ask your
team whether that applies to you. [Shunts and hydrocephalus](/treatments/shunts) is
the page about having one.

**If you take belzutifan,** you will have regular blood tests. That is how your
team watches for low red blood cells.

## Everyday life

**What everyday life looks like afterwards depends on where the tumor was, and
on how your recovery goes.** Some of what follows will not apply to you.

**Being worn out is common** for a while after treatment, and it is worth
raising at each visit instead of putting up with it. Being much sleepier than
usual is a different thing. That one can mean fluid backing up, so it is a
same-day call, and a right-away call for anyone with a shunt.

**Driving** turns on two things: the law where you live, and how well you see
and move. Ask your own team which of those affects you.

**With VHL, checkups become part of life.** It helps to keep your scan and test
dates in one place, and to bring the list to each visit.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

**Everyone has follow-up scans. How often depends on which group you are in.**

**One tumor, taken out completely.** Your team sets the schedule. Scans are more
often at first, and then spaced out.

**With VHL, checks go on for life.** GeneReviews, a guide written for doctors,
suggests scans of the brain and whole spine every 2 years from age 11, and eye
checks from early childhood. Your team may suggest scans more often if you have
tumors they are watching.

**If you are pregnant or planning to be,** tell your team. Studies disagree on
whether pregnancy makes these tumors grow. GeneReviews says closer checks before
and during pregnancy can be considered.

[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes through what the reports mean.
[Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is about the hard part, which
is usually the waiting.

## If it comes back

**For one tumor that was taken out completely, it does not often come back.** A
review written for doctors says the chance is low when all of it was removed. If
some was left behind, it can grow again, UPMC says, and scans are how that is
found early.

**With VHL, new tumors can appear as well.** People with VHL usually get more
tumors in the brain and spinal cord over the years, and The Brain Tumour Charity
says tumors that come with VHL are also more likely to come back after surgery.
A new one is not a sign that you did anything wrong. It is what the checks are
for.

**There are still choices.** Another operation, focused radiation, or, with VHL,
belzutifan. Ask what each one is meant to do.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**For this tumor, one part of that answer matters more than usual.** The part
above says a small number of people have an inherited condition. For this tumor
it is not a small number: about 1 in 4 people with it have VHL. That is a change
in a gene. It is passed on in families, or it starts new in a person. Either
way, nothing you did caused it.

**For a tumor without VHL, it was not passed on.** MedlinePlus Genetics says the
gene change in that tumor happens during a person's life, only in the tumor
cells.

**If you have VHL, each of your children has a 1 in 2 chance of having it
too.** GeneReviews says so. The VHL Alliance says children at risk can have the
test as early as birth. A child who does not have the gene change does not need
the lifelong checks.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
**For many people with one tumor, the operation is the end of the treatment.**
When all of it can be taken out, a review for doctors says it does not often
come back. Checkups go on for a while after, and then less often.

**With VHL, it is a longer road.** New tumors can keep appearing over the years,
in the brain, the spine, and other parts of the body. The regular checks are
there to find each one early, while there are the most choices. How well that
goes depends a lot on keeping up with them.

**Figures you may find online describe large groups of people.** They cannot say
what will happen to you. Many of them also mix the two groups on this page
together, which makes them even less useful to any one reader.

Ask your team when you are ready. You can also choose not to ask yet.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three of them belong to this tumor in particular.

**Learn the two rules in the symptoms section before you need them.** A sudden,
very bad headache means 911. With a tumor in the cord, one group of changes
means an emergency room. Often it is the person nearby who spots these first.

**After an operation low at the back of the brain, falls are the big risk at
home.** Clear the floor, add night lights, and be close by for the first
showers.

**If it is VHL, it is a family matter.** You may be caring for someone and also
wondering about your own test, or your children's. That is a lot to carry. A
genetic counselor can help the whole family, not only the person with the tumor.

## What to ask your team

- Is it one tumor, or are there others?
- Should I have a VHL gene test? Who can help me understand the result?
- Is the fluid pocket causing my symptoms?
- Do you recommend an operation, focused radiation, or watching it? Why?
- Will the blood supply be closed off before surgery?
- Was all of the tumor taken out?
- Which symptoms mean calling you today, and which mean the emergency room?
- How often will I have scans, and of which parts of my body?
- Will I see a physical therapist for my balance?
- If I have VHL, should my children be tested, and when?
- If I have VHL, is belzutifan an option for me?
- What number do I call at night or on a weekend?
