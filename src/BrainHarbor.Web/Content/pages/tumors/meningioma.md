## The short version

A meningioma starts in the layers that wrap your brain, not in the brain
itself. That is a real difference, not a form of words. On many scans you can see a
strip of fluid between the tumor and the brain. That strip is what tells a
radiologist it sits outside. Most meningiomas are grade 1 and grow
slowly, and a lot of them turn up by chance on a scan taken for another reason.
**What matters most is not how big it is. It is where it sits and what it is
pressing on.** A small one against the nerve to your eye is a bigger problem
than a larger one on the top of your head. And if you take any hormone
medicine, that is worth raising early, because a few of them matter here.

## What is a meningioma?

**It is a tumor that grows from the coverings of the brain.**

Your brain and spinal cord are wrapped in three thin layers called the
meninges. They cushion the brain, carry its blood vessels, and hold the fluid
that flows around it. A meningioma grows from cells in the middle of those
three layers.

So it is next to your brain rather than in it. Doctors have a word for that:
**extra-axial**, meaning outside the brain substance. It is the most common tumor of
that kind.

**And a scan can often show it.** On a scan, a rim of the fluid that
normally flows around the brain can often be seen between the tumor and the
brain surface. Radiologists look for that strip on purpose, because a tumor
growing *inside* the brain cannot have it. If your report mentions it, that is
what it is telling you.

### The honest limit of that

This is the part most pages leave out. Leaving it out is how "it is not in your
brain" turns into "it is harmless".

A meningioma grows inward and **presses**. Pressing causes real symptoms and
real damage. Where it grows around a nerve or a large vein, it can be
genuinely difficult to remove. Some of them are graded 2 or 3 and grow faster.

So: starting outside the brain matters, and what it matters for is what the
tumor is doing to brain tissue. It does not settle everything else, and this
page tries to hold both.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Most meningiomas are not cancer, and the word "benign" does more harm than
good here.**

Grades run 1 to 3, and they describe how the cells look and behave under a
microscope. They are written as ordinary numbers now. A grade is not a
prediction about you.

- **Grade 1** is most of them. Slow-growing.
- **Grade 2**, sometimes written "atypical", is a smaller number. It comes back
  more often, which changes the plan rather than the diagnosis.
- **Grade 3** is a small number. It grows faster and is treated more firmly
  from the start.

We do not print what share falls into each grade. The counts that exist do not
agree with one another, and the number would only be used to guess at your own
situation.

**Two things can change a grade without anything looking different under the
microscope.** One is a change in something called the TERT promoter. The other is losing
both copies of a gene called CDKN2A or CDKN2B. Either one makes it grade 3,
whatever the cells look like.
[What the markers mean](/tests/molecular-markers) explains those words.

**Why "benign" is a poor word for this.** It means "not cancer", and people
hear "harmless". A grade 1 meningioma in a bad place can take your sight. A
grade 1 meningioma can come back years later. Both of those are true of
something that is not cancer. The useful questions are where it is and what it
is doing, not which side of that word it falls on.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**This is the idea the whole page is built on: location decides almost
everything, and size decides less than you think.**

A meningioma can grow anywhere the coverings go. What it presses on is what you
notice. So two people with the same size tumor can have completely different
lives.

- **On the surface, under the top of the skull.** Often nothing at all for a
  long time. When it does show, seizures, headache, weakness in an arm or a
  leg, or trouble with speech.
- **In the covering that runs between the two halves of the brain.** Weakness
  in a leg,
  changes in personality, flatness, headache, unsteadiness, trouble with
  memory.
- **Behind the eyes, on the wing of bone there.** Loss of vision, double
  vision, numbness in the face, or an eye that starts to bulge.
- **On the floor at the front of the skull, by the smelling nerves.** **Loss of
  smell** that often goes unnoticed for years, with subtle changes in
  personality, concentration or memory.
- **Near the pituitary and the crossing of the optic nerves.** Losing parts of
  your field of vision.
- **At the back, near the brainstem and the nerves to the face and ear.**
  Facial pain or numbness, facial weakness, hearing loss, a hoarse voice,
  trouble swallowing.
- **Inside the fluid spaces of the brain.** A build-up of fluid and pressure,
  with headache, trouble walking, and trouble with memory.
- **On the spinal cord.** Back pain that is typically worse at night, numbness,
  and weakness in the legs.

**The point, said plainly.** A small meningioma against the nerve to your eye
will take your sight. A larger one on the top of your head may cause nothing
for years. The measurement on your report is not the headline. The address is.

**"Skull base" is an umbrella word** for the ones growing on the floor of the
skull and the ridge behind the eyes. They are harder to take out completely,
and they have more complications when they are. That is a fact about the
operation, not about the grade.

## What symptoms does it cause?

It depends entirely on where it sits, so the list above is the real answer.
The ones that turn up across many locations are headache and seizures,
something going wrong with your eyesight, and a limb that feels weak or numb.
Then there are the changes in thinking, mood or personality, which the people
around you often spot before you do.

Many meningiomas cause no symptoms at all.

**If your meningioma is on your spinal cord, the list below is not yours.** The
signs that matter there are different, and they are the ones doctors call red
flags: new weakness in the legs, a change in how you walk, numbness around the
saddle area, and any new trouble with your bladder or bowel. **New weakness or
new bladder trouble is a reason to be seen quickly, not to wait for the next
appointment.** Do not wait for it to get bad first, because the early signs are
often vague ones. [Spinal cord tumors](/tumors/spinal-cord-tumor) is the page
for that half.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

**Usually with a scan, and often before anybody suspected anything.**

A meningioma has a look an MRI can often pick out. A lump with a clear edge,
attached to the covering. It lights up evenly with contrast dye. And that strip
of fluid sits between it and the brain. A CT can show thickened bone next to it, or flecks of
calcium inside it. [MRI scans](/tests/mri) covers what the scan itself is like.

**Many are found by accident.** Somebody is scanned after a car accident, or
for headaches, or for dizziness, and there it is. That is not bad luck
uncovering a hidden disaster. Meningiomas are common enough that a meaningful
number of people are walking around with one they will never know about, and
scanning a lot of people finds them.

**Here is the gap almost nothing tells you: if you have not had surgery, your
grade is an estimate.** Grade comes from looking at cells, and that needs
tissue. Before an operation, a team can say how it looks and how it behaves,
and that is genuinely informative, but it is not the same as a grade off a
report. If somebody has told you a grade and you have not had surgery, it is
fair to ask which of those two you are being given.
[Biopsy](/tests/biopsy) and [the pathology report](/tests/pathology-report)
cover the tissue side.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

Meningioma reports are unusually full of jargon, and some of it sounds much
worse than it is.

**Extra-axial.** Outside the brain substance. This is the reassuring one.

**CSF cleft.** The strip of fluid between the tumor and the brain described
above. It is evidence the tumor sits outside.

**Dural tail.** A thin tapering streak of the covering next to the tumor,
lighting up with dye. Common with meningioma, but it turns up with other things
too, so on its own it does not settle anything.

**Hyperostosis.** Thickening of the bone beside the tumor.

**Calcification.** Flecks of calcium in the tumor, seen best on a CT.

**Brain invasion.** This is the one that frightens people, and it does not mean
what it sounds like. It has a strict definition: tumor cells have to break
through the innermost covering layer and push into brain tissue in
finger-shaped tongues. **Indenting the brain is not invasion.** Nor is growing
along the outside of a blood vessel as it dips into the brain, because that
innermost layer stays intact. When it is present it moves the tumor to grade 2
on its own. And whether it actually predicts
anything is unsettled among the specialists who wrote the rule. Several studies
have found no consistent link with the tumor coming back. If those two words are on your
report, they mean more testing and a closer plan, not a verdict.

**Ki-67, or MIB-1.** A measure of how many cells are dividing. Higher goes with
a higher chance of it coming back.

**Progesterone receptor.** Whether the tumor's cells carry a docking point for
that hormone. Many meningiomas do. It connects to the medicine question below.

**Grade 1, 2 or 3, and Simpson I to V, which is a completely different thing.**
Your operative note may carry a **Simpson grade** in Roman numerals. It is not
a second opinion on your WHO grade. Simpson describes **how much came out**. I is
the whole tumor, plus the piece of covering it grew from, plus any abnormal
bone. V is a biopsy, or an operation to relieve pressure only. I, II and III
all count as a complete removal. IV means some was deliberately left behind. So a report can say "grade 1, Simpson II", and
each number is telling you something different.

**One more, if the Roman numerals worry you.** The section above explains that
Roman numeral grades belong to the old way of writing things down. Simpson
grades are the exception, and always have been: they are written in Roman
numerals on purpose. So Roman numerals beside the word Simpson are expected.
Your WHO grade is the one that should be an ordinary number.

**NF2-related schwannomatosis.** An inherited condition linked with
meningiomas. It used to be called neurofibromatosis type 2. The name was
changed in 2022, because the growths the old name described are not the ones
that actually occur in it. An older letter will say the old name. Around half
the people who have the condition have meningiomas. If this is in your family, ask your
team what that means for relatives.

## How is it usually treated?

There are three treatments and one honest gap, and which of them applies
depends on the tumor rather than on preference.

**Watching it.** For a small one causing no trouble, a plan of scans is the
recommended approach rather than a delay. What triggers a change is growth that
keeps going, symptoms starting, or the tumor getting close to something
important. A little growth does not automatically change the plan, so ask your
team how much change would make them act.
[Watching a tumor, step by step](/treatments/watch-and-wait) sets out what
living with that plan is like, and it has a section on meningioma specifically.

**Surgery.** The main treatment for one that is causing symptoms or growing.
The aim is to take out the tumor. Where it is safe, the surgeon also takes the
piece of covering it grew from, and any abnormal bone. That is what makes it
much less likely to grow back.
How completely that can be done depends almost entirely on where it is.
[Brain surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) covers the operation and the weeks
after.

**Radiation.** Used when a tumor cannot be removed, when some is left behind,
or after surgery for grade 2 and grade 3 tumors. It can be given as a focused
single treatment or a few, or as a longer course over weeks.
[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers what that involves.
Doses and schedules are your team's to set, and they differ between tumors and
between hospitals.

**Drug treatment: the honest answer is that there is not one yet.** No medicine
is approved specifically for meningioma. Several have been tried over the
years. If your tumor keeps coming back, that is a reason to ask your team what
is being studied, rather than a reason to assume a drug exists.
[Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) covers how to think about that.

## Am I on a medicine that should be looked at?

**This is the part of the page with something to actually do in it. Read to the
end of the section rather than the middle.**

Some hormone medicines are linked with meningioma. **Not all of them, and not
to the same degree**, which is exactly why this needs three answers rather than
one.

**Strongest, and the one with a clear instruction: high-dose progestogens.** A
medicine called cyproterone acetate is linked with meningiomas being more
common and growing faster, and the advice to doctors treats that as a real
risk rather than a maybe. The guidance
to doctors is to **stop it permanently if somebody is diagnosed with a
meningioma**. Two related medicines, chlormadinone acetate and nomegestrol
acetate, carry a similar but lower risk. There is some evidence the risk falls
again after cyproterone acetate is stopped.

Cyproterone acetate is prescribed for several reasons, and one of them is
**gender-affirming hormone therapy for trans women**. It is also used for heavy
hair growth and for acne. If you take it for any of those reasons, this
applies to you and it is worth raising at your next appointment. It is a
conversation about alternatives, and there are alternatives.

**Mixed, and with a real trade-off: hormone replacement therapy.** HRT around
the menopause **seems to** raise the risk, and the evidence is less settled
than for the medicines above. The advice is to avoid it where you can if you
have a meningioma or have had one removed. **But menopausal symptoms can be
worse to live with than the meningioma**, and that is a judgement to make with
a doctor rather than alone. Do not stop HRT on your own on the strength of a
web page.

**Other contraceptive pills: no apparent increase, with one exception.**
Ordinary contraceptive pills do not appear to raise the risk, **and here is the
exception that matters**: some combined pills contain cyproterone acetate,
usually prescribed for acne or excess hair. Those belong in the group above. So
the useful move is not to reason about your pill. It is to take the box to your
team and ask which kind it is.

**Fertility treatment.** There is not enough information to say either way.

Nothing here means stopping a medicine today by yourself. What it means is
putting the list of what you take in front of somebody this month.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

If you have surgery, the first weeks are mostly tiredness. A deep, unfamiliar
tiredness that comes in waves and takes longer than people expect to lift. The
scar aches. Concentration is patchy. Sleep is odd.

What you can expect to recover depends on what the tumor was pressing on and
for how long. Pressure that has been there a long time does not always undo
completely. A nerve that was already thinned may not come all the way back.
Ask about that **before** the operation. What counts as a good outcome for you
depends on the answer.

Was the tumor near the nerves for vision, hearing, smell or the muscles of the
face? Then ask three things. What is expected to improve. What is expected to
stay. And how long anybody will wait before calling it settled.

**And here is the thing nobody warns people about.** A grade 1 meningioma taken
out completely is often the end of it. It still does not feel like an ending.
You get scans for years. That is normal, and the section below says when they
can stop.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

**Tiredness** is the symptom people underestimate, both before and after
treatment. It is not laziness and it is not depression, though it can look like
both from the outside.

**Seizures** happen with some meningiomas, mostly the ones on the surface of
the brain. Read [what to do when someone has a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) before you
need it. [Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) is about the days when
nothing is happening.
[Anti-seizure medicines](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines) covers the tablets.

**Driving** depends on where you live and on whether you have had a seizure.
The answer is set by whoever gives out licenses where you are, so this page
does not print one.
[Driving and seizures](/seizures/living-with#driving) has the shape of it and
where to look yours up.

**Work.** Many people go back. What gets in the way is usually the tiredness
and the concentration rather than anything dramatic, and those are the things
adjustments help with most.

**Changes in personality and memory** are real, common with tumors near the
front of the brain, and easy to mistake for the person choosing to be
difficult. Saying it out loud at an appointment is worth more than managing it
privately.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans go on for a long time with this diagnosis. For once there is something
worth waiting for at the end of them.

**The wait for the result is harder than the scan**, which catches people out.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes through how one scan is set
against the last, and what to do with the waiting.

**If your meningioma was found by chance and is being watched**, the long-term
studies of tumors like that are reassuring. Growth tends to slow down after the
first couple of years, and after several more years it slows almost to nothing.
That is the growth slowing, not the tumor shrinking. The
counterweight is on [watching a tumor,
step by step](/treatments/watch-and-wait), which has the study where more
people ended up having treatment than not. And when a tumor
turns out to be one of the ones that limits itself, it usually keeps doing
that. Your own intervals are your team's to set.

**And if you had a grade 1 meningioma taken out completely, the scans can
eventually stop.** In the work that surveillance plans are built on, no tumor
of that kind came back after eleven clear years. Eleven years is a long time to
be watched, and it is not forever. That is worth knowing at the start of it
rather than at the end.

For a grade 2 or grade 3 tumor, or one where some was left behind, watching
carries on. Ask your own team what yours looks like and what would change it.

## If it comes back, or changes

**A grade 1 meningioma can come back, and that does not mean anybody got
anything wrong.**

This is the clearest thing to understand about this tumor. A meningioma grows
*from* the coverings. An operation takes out the lump, and where it is safe,
the piece of covering underneath it. Sometimes the tumor is wrapped around a nerve or a large vein. Leaving a little
behind is then the right decision rather than a mistake, because taking it
would cost you something you use. If any of the
cells it grows from are still there, it can slowly grow again from them.

That is why "not cancer" and "completely removed" are two separate statements,
and why scans carry on for years after a grade 1 tumor.

The shape of it, without numbers: **grade 1 comes back rarely, and after about
a decade it essentially stops. Grade 2 comes back often enough that radiation
after surgery is usually advised. Grade 3 comes back often and early**, which
is why it is treated firmly from the beginning.

If it does come back, the choices are the ones you have already met. Surgery
again. Radiation, where there is room to give it. Or a trial. Which of those is
open depends on what was done last time and where the tumor sits.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**Two things are worth naming for meningioma specifically, because they are
real.**

**Radiation to the head, long ago.** People who had radiotherapy to the head as
children, for cancer or for old skin conditions, have a higher chance of a
meningioma decades later. This is an established link. **It is not the same
thing as a dental x-ray or a CT scan.** The evidence comes from doses far
larger than anything used for imaging, and your head scan did not cause this.
If you did have childhood radiotherapy, say so, because it changes how closely
you are watched.

**The hormone medicines above.** This one is different from the others on this
page, because it is the only one where there is something to do rather than
something to absorb. It is still not a thing you did wrong. Those medicines
were prescribed to you.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth knowing what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

For meningioma there is an extra reason to be careful with them. Most
meningiomas are not cancer, and the figures that get quoted are usually about
whether the tumor comes back rather than about how long anybody lives. Those two things get mixed up
constantly, including by people who should know better. A number lifted from
one and read as the other is frightening for no reason.

The figures that do exist come from groups of people treated years ago, sorted
by grade and by how much of the tumor came out. They cannot tell you which
group you are in. That depends on where your tumor was, what could safely be
removed, and how it behaves over the first few years. Those are
things your own team can see.

What is worth taking from them is the shape rather than the value. A completely
removed grade 1 tumor rarely comes back, and is eventually not watched at all.
The higher grades need closer watching for longer. Your team can tell you which
of those describes you. That is a more useful conversation than a number.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three things that belong to this tumor in particular.

**The changes you are seeing may be the location.** A meningioma near the front of
the brain can flatten somebody's personality. It can blunt their drive, or take
their sense of smell. None of that looks like illness from the outside. It looks like them being different. Say what you have noticed, at the
appointment, in front of them. Nothing else you do will help the team more.

**"Not cancer" will be said to you, and it will not settle anything.** People
outside will hear "benign" and expect you to be relieved. You may be dealing
with somebody facing brain surgery, or years of scans, or a change in who they
are. Both things are true and you do not have to pick one to say out loud.

**Go with them to the appointment where the medicine list comes up.** The
hormone question above is easy to forget in a room where somebody has just
been told they have a brain tumor. It is one of the few things here with a
clear action attached.

## What to ask your team

- Is it on the covering of my brain, or in my brain?
- Where exactly is it, and what is it near?
- Why is the size not the main thing here?
- What grade is it, and did that come from tissue or from the scan?
- If I have not had surgery, how sure is the grade?
- Are we watching it or treating it, and why that?
- What would make you change the plan?
- If surgery is the plan, how much do you expect to be able to take out, and
  what decides that?
- What should I expect to get back afterwards, and what might not come back?
- Does my report say brain invasion, and what does that change?
- Am I on any hormone medicine that should be reviewed? Here is the box.
- If I had radiotherapy as a child, could that have caused this?
- Is this linked to NF2-related schwannomatosis, and should anyone in my family
  be checked?
- How long will I have scans, and is there a point where they stop?
- If it comes back, what then?
