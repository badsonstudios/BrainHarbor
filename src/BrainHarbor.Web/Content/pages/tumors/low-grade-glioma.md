## The short version

!%transformation%!%watch and wait%!%vorasidenib%!%circumscribed%!%supratentorial%
"Low-grade glioma" is not one diagnosis. It is a label covering a lot of
different tumors that happen to grow slowly. **The most important thing on this
page is that grade 1 and grade 2 are not the same thing**, and being told "low
grade" without being told which one leaves you with the wrong picture. Many of
these are treated by watching first rather than acting at once, and that is a
real plan. Your actual diagnosis, the one on your report, is what everything
else depends on.

## What is a low-grade glioma?

It is a phrase doctors use for a glioma that grows slowly. It is not the name
of a tumor.

That matters more than it sounds. The label covers a couple of dozen different
tumors. They do not all behave the same way. That is why the World Health
Organization advised against using the term even before the rules were rewritten
in 2021. You will still hear it constantly. That is why this page exists.

**So the first thing to do is find your real diagnosis.** It is on your
pathology report, and it will be a specific name with a grade attached, like
"astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 2". [Your pathology report, part by
part](/tests/pathology-report) shows you where to look. Once you have that
name, it is worth more to you than anything on this page.

In adults, two names sit under the label more than any others. One is
**astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 2**. The other is **oligodendroglioma,
IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, grade 2**. Children and young people have their
own set of names. **Pilocytic astrocytoma** is the one you are most likely to
hear there.

You may also come across "**lower**-grade glioma", with the -er. Some doctors
use that for grade 2 and grade 3 together. It is a different grouping again,
and it is worth asking which one somebody means.

[CROSSWALK]

**Two older names turn up on this group's paperwork more than any others.**

- **Diffuse astrocytoma** on its own is now usually written with the gene
  result attached, as astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, with a grade.
- **Oligoastrocytoma**, a mixed name, is no longer given as a diagnosis. Gene
  tests now sort those tumors into one group or the other.

Seeing a retired name on your own report does not mean it was wrong. It means it
was written under the old rules. If you are comparing an old report with a new
one, ask your team to walk you through what changed and what did not.

## Is it cancer? What does the grade mean?

This is the hardest section on this page and it is the reason the page is
written at all. It has two answers, and which one is yours depends entirely on
your grade.

**Most grade 1 gliomas are a different situation.** Doctors call them
circumscribed. They have an edge. They sit apart from the brain around them
rather than growing into it. That is why an operation that takes all of it out
can often cure them. Pilocytic astrocytoma is the usual example.

Grade and growth pattern are two separate things, though, and they do not always
line up. Some grade 1 tumors in children are diffuse. So ask about both.

**Grade 2 diffuse gliomas are not that.** They grow into the brain around them
rather than pushing it aside, which means there is no edge to cut around. They
are malignant, and with today's treatments they are not curable. That is a hard
sentence and it is here on purpose, because the alternative is worse.

**Here is why it is here.** A brain tumor doctor wrote about this. The article
describes the shock on people's faces at being told what a grade 2 glioma is.
Many had already been told something else, before they ever reached a cancer
specialist. They had been told it was curable, and maybe not even cancer. The
phrases they repeated back were "this is the good kind", "we got it all", and
"this is the best brain tumor to have".

If somebody has said something like that to you, they were not lying to you.
They were probably thinking of grade 1, or reading a scan that genuinely looks
undramatic. But you deserve to know which of the two you have, and you are
entitled to ask the question plainly: **is mine grade 1 or grade 2, and is it
circumscribed or diffuse?**

**None of this means what happens next is fixed.** Not curable is not the same
as not treatable, and it is not a timetable. What it means is that this is a
long relationship rather than a single event, and that changes what good care
looks like. There is a section further down about how that tends to go, behind a
choice, so you can read it when you want to and not before.

[What the grade on your report means](/tests/pathology-report#what-the-grade-means) goes
through the grading itself.

## Why does my report say grade 4 when the scan looked low grade?

Because since 2021, the grade does not come only from what the cells look like.

For some tumors, a gene result now sets the grade on its own. It does that even
when the cells look low grade under the microscope.

Here are the two that catch people out. A tumor that looked grade 2 down the
microscope is called **glioblastoma, grade 4** if it is IDH-wildtype and carries
certain gene changes. And an IDH-mutant astrocytoma is **grade 4** if it has a
CDKN2A/B deletion with **both copies** gone, even when it looks nothing like a
grade 4.

This is one of the most frightening things that happens to people here. It
usually arrives with no warning. The scan looked mild. Everyone sounded
cautiously reassuring. Then the report says grade 4.

**It is not a mistake, and nobody was hiding anything.** The gene results took
longer to come back than the first impression did. And they carry more
weight.

[The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes IDH, CDKN2A/B
and the rest one at a time. If this has happened to you, that page and a
conversation with your team are the two things worth having.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

In adults, grade 2 diffuse gliomas are usually in the upper part of the brain.
Your team may call that supratentorial. It is the part that handles movement,
speech, feeling and thinking.

**A slow tumor causes trouble differently from a fast one.** It grows a little
at a time, so the brain around it adapts as it goes. That is why a tumor can
look large on a scan while the person feels mostly fine. It is also why the
early signs get put down to stress, or tiredness, or getting older. Sometimes
for years, before anyone scans anything.

It is also why these tumors often show up less dramatically on a scan than a
faster-growing one. They usually do not light up when dye is used.
[Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) goes through what the dye is doing.

## What symptoms does it cause?

**Seizures are the big one.** A seizure is often the first sign of a low-grade
glioma. If a seizure is what brought you here, you are in very ordinary company.

Other things people notice, usually slowly: headaches, trouble finding words,
changes in how sharp or organized thinking feels, weakness or numbness on one
side, and changes in vision.

Some of these tumors are found by accident. Someone has a scan for a head
injury, or for a headache that turns out to be nothing to do with it, and there
it is.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

A scan first, then tissue. A scan can suggest a glioma and can suggest it is
slow-growing, but **only a piece of the tumor, looked at in a lab, can name it**.
For these tumors, the gene tests are what settle both the name and the grade.

## What do the words on my report mean?

The three that decide most of what happens next:

- **IDH.** Whether the tumor carries a change in the IDH gene. For an adult
  diffuse glioma, this is the first fork in the road. It is also what makes
  vorasidenib possible later.
- **1p/19q.** If this is co-deleted and there is an IDH change, the tumor is an
  oligodendroglioma. If it is not, an IDH-mutant tumor is an astrocytoma.
- **CDKN2A/B.** If **both copies** are gone in an IDH-mutant astrocytoma, the
  grade is 4. That holds however the cells look.

You may also see **ATRX**, **TP53**, **Ki-67** and **MGMT**. Each has its own
entry on [the words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers), which is
where they are explained properly rather than in passing.

## How is it usually treated?

There is no single answer here. Your plan depends on your grade, your gene
results, where the tumor sits, how much of it came out, and what you want.

- **Surgery, taking out as much as is safe.** This is usually the first step
  where it is possible. For a grade 1 circumscribed tumor, a complete operation
  can be the end of it. [Brain surgery: what happens, and what comes
  after](/treatments/craniotomy) goes through the operation.
  If the tumor is close to the parts that handle speech or movement, you may be
  awake for part of it. [Awake brain surgery](/treatments/awake-craniotomy)
  explains why.
- **Watching, with scans.** Your team may call this watch and wait, or active
  surveillance. It is a real option for some people, and there is a section on it
  below, because it is the part that causes most doubt.
- **Radiation**, usually after surgery, for people whose situation suggests
  treating now rather than waiting. [Radiation therapy: the mask, the daily
  visits, and what comes after](/treatments/radiation-therapy) goes through it.
- **Chemotherapy**, usually PCV after radiation in this setting.
  [Chemotherapy: the drugs, your blood counts, and the fever
  rule](/treatments/chemotherapy) takes the drugs one at a time.
- **Vorasidenib**, a newer medicine taken by mouth, for some people. Its own
  section below.
- **Seizure medicine**, which for many people here is not a side issue but one
  of the main goals of treatment.

### Why is my team suggesting we just watch it?

Because for some people, watching is the treatment that does least harm.

**Watching is not doing nothing.** It means scans at set times, and seeing your
team, so a change is caught while it is small. Here is the point of it.
Radiation and chemotherapy both have costs. Over years, radiation's cost falls
on thinking and memory. Starting radiation right after surgery can hold the tumor
back for longer and mean fewer seizures. Even so, it has not been shown to change
how long people live. That is why some teams wait, to put radiation's cost off
until it is needed.

Whether it suits you depends on things your team can weigh and a web page
cannot: your grade, your gene results, how much of the tumor is left, your
symptoms, and how you feel about it.

**One thing worth knowing, because it gets misused.** You may hear about an age
line around 40, splitting "low risk" from "high risk". That line came from a
clinical trial. It was written to decide who could join the trial. It was not
written to decide who needs treatment.

The researchers who use it say so plainly. There is no clinical reason for a
strict age cutoff, and other things should be weighed too. **So if your plan has
been explained to you as "you are over 40, so...", ask again.** Not because
anyone is wrong. Because the reasoning should be about you.

Waiting is hard, and being uneasy about it is not a sign you are choosing wrong.
Ask what specifically would change the plan, and when the next scan is, so you
know what you are waiting for. [Watching a tumor, step by step](/treatments/watch-and-wait) goes through the plan itself, and
the worry that can come with it.

### What is vorasidenib, and could it apply to me?

It is a medicine taken by mouth, for some people with a grade 2 IDH-mutant
glioma. It is the newest thing on this page by a long way.

The FDA approved it in August 2024. It is for adults and children aged 12 and
over. The tumor has to be a **grade 2 astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma with an
IDH change**, and there has to have been surgery first. A biopsy counts as
surgery here. It works on the IDH change itself. Chemotherapy works a different
way, by going after cells that are dividing.

**Two reasons to ask about it by name.** It is new enough that a lot of patient
information written before mid-2024 does not mention it at all. And it turns
entirely on your IDH result. That is exactly the kind of detail that can sit in
a report with nobody talking it through with you.

So ask two things. **Does my tumor have an IDH change? Am I someone vorasidenib
could apply to?** If the answer is no, ask what that is based on. It is a
specific question and it has a specific answer.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

The library pages go through the day-to-day of each one. What the operation
involves, and the weeks after it. What a course of radiation is like from the
inside. What chemotherapy does to your blood counts.

Two things that are particular to this group:

**Seizures often get better with treatment.** Treating the tumor is often what
brings the seizures under control. So here, seizure control is one of the goals
of treatment. It is not a side effect of it.

**"Is this the tumor, or the medicine?"** is a question worth keeping in your
pocket. The medicines used here have effects that look like the tumor getting
worse: levetiracetam is linked with irritability and low mood, and steroids
cause muscle weakness. [Steroids](/treatments/steroids) covers that medicine on
its own, and the surgery and radiation pages go through the rest.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

People with a low-grade glioma are often living an ordinary life around it,
sometimes for a long time. So the practical questions matter as much as the
medical ones.

**Seizures and driving.** Driving rules after a seizure depend entirely on where
you live, and this page will not guess at yours. Ask your team, and ask early,
because it is one of the changes people find hardest and one of the easiest to
plan around once you know. [Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) goes
through the rest of it: what is safe, what to change, and how much of the usual
advice turns out to be wrong.

**Work.** Many people keep working. Whether you can, and in what shape, depends
on your symptoms and your job rather than on your diagnosis.

**Tiredness and thinking.** Both are common. Both are easy to blame on the wrong
thing. Raise them rather than putting up with them. If your thinking feels
different, say so. Your team can test it. You do not have to prove it first.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Scans continue whether or not you are having treatment, and for a lot of people
here they *are* the treatment plan.

**Even after a complete-looking operation, scans continue.** For a diffuse
glioma, cells extend past the edge of what shows up on a scan, so "we got it
all" describes the picture rather than the tumor. That is not a reason to think
the operation failed. It is the reason for the scans.

The wait before a scan result is its own thing, and for many people it is worse
than the scan. Ask who will give you the result, and roughly when. Ask one more
thing: will it appear in your online records before anybody calls you? For a lot
of people it does.

[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes
through what your team compares each scan with, and what the words on the report
mean.

## If it comes back, or changes

Because a diffuse grade 2 glioma is not curable, the plan assumes it will need
attention again at some point. That may be many years away. It is the reason the
scans do not stop, and it is built into your follow-up from the start.

**Some of these tumors change into a higher grade over time.** That is what
people mean by transformation. It is a known behavior of these tumors, not a
sign that something has gone wrong. It is one of the things your team looks for
on each scan.

There are no numbers about it on this page, deliberately. What is worth knowing
is what it would change: the plan, not the fact that you have a plan. If it
happens, the treatment options are the ones on this page, in a different order.

**Tell your team if something changes between scans.** New or different
seizures. New weakness. A headache that is not like your usual one. Do not wait
for the next appointment. Bringing a scan forward is a normal thing to do.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors talk about outlook using numbers. Those numbers describe large groups of
people, treated years ago. Those people were diagnosed before the gene tests
that now define your tumor existed. They were diagnosed before vorasidenib. So a
number from one of those groups describes a slightly different illness from
yours.

What can be said honestly: this varies enormously between people, and the things
that shape it most are your grade, your gene results, how much of the tumor came
out, and how it behaves over the first few years. Those are things your own team
can see and a page cannot.

**If somebody quotes you a "median", here is what that word is doing.** A median
is the middle of a group. Half the group did better than it and half did worse.
It is not a countdown, and it is not a prediction about you.

The other thing about these groups is that they are lopsided. There is a long
tail of people who do much better than the middle, and that tail is real. Some
people live many years with a grade 2 glioma, working and driving and raising
children, with it watched or treated as it changes. A middle number tells you
nothing about which part of the group you are in.

For a grade 1 tumor that was taken out completely, the picture is different
again, and often much more hopeful.

If you want a number, ask your own team for one, and ask them what it is based
on. That conversation can be had more than once, and it is reasonable to decide
you would rather not have it today.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

What is different about this one is the timescale. This is not a few hard weeks
and then recovery. Here it is usually years of living alongside something, and
that asks something different of you.

**Seizures are the thing to be ready for.** If seizures are part of this, learn
what to do before you need to. [What to do when someone has a
seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is the one to know by heart, and it says which
seizures need an ambulance and which do not. Knowing that difference is what
stops every event becoming an emergency.

**Scan weeks are hard, and they come round again.** The days before a follow-up
scan and its result are, for a lot of people, worse than anything else in the
year. It helps to know that in advance and to plan the week rather than pretend
it is ordinary.

**Watching is hard to live with, for you as well.** If the plan
is to watch, there is nothing for you to do. Having nothing to do, while
something frightening sits inside someone you love, is its own kind of hard. It
is not a sign the plan is wrong.

**The changes you notice may be the ones nobody else does.** Tiredness. A
shorter temper. Words coming more slowly. Thinking that seems a step behind. You
will see those long before a clinic appointment does. Say them out loud at the
appointment. They can be tested, and some of them turn out to be the medicines
rather than the tumor.

**Plan for the long version.** Whatever is happening this month, this is not an
event to get through. It is something to live alongside. Look after your own
health. Keep your own appointments. Let people help with the ordinary things.

## What to ask your team

- Is mine grade 1 or grade 2, and is it circumscribed or diffuse?
- What is my exact diagnosis, in the words that are on the report?
- Does my tumor have an IDH mutation? What about 1p/19q and CDKN2A/B?
- Are we watching it or treating it now, and why that?
- What specifically would make us change the plan?
- When is my next scan, and who will go through it with me?
- Am I someone vorasidenib could apply to? If not, what is that based on?
- If surgery is on the table, what is the goal, and what could I lose?
- What should I do about driving and work?
- What should make me call you before the next appointment?
- Is there a clinical trial I should know about?
