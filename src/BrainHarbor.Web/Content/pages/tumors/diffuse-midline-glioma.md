## The short version

**A diffuse midline glioma is a fast-growing tumor in the middle of the brain or
in the spinal cord.** It is named after a change written **H3 K27**, and it is
always grade 4. When it sits in the pons, a part of the brain stem, it is often
called **DIPG**, and [DIPG](/tumors/dipg) is the page written for that.
Radiation is the main treatment. **Ask about clinical trials at the start**,
without holding up radiation your team says is urgent.

## What is a diffuse midline glioma?

!%H3 K27-altered%It is a tumor that starts in the brain's helper cells, so it is one kind of
[glioma](/tumors/glioma). It grows in the middle parts of the brain and spine:
the **brain stem**, the **thalamus** and the **spinal cord**.

**Diffuse** means it grows out into the healthy tissue around it, like fingers,
rather than sitting in one lump. That is the fact most of this page follows
from.

It is found most often in children. It also happens in adults, where it is less
common and most often in the thalamus.

### DIPG, and why it is not another word for this

DIPG stands for diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma. It is an older name for one of
these tumors when it sits in the **pons**. So most tumors called DIPG are
diffuse midline gliomas. But one in the thalamus or the spinal cord is not a
DIPG.

Some websites say this tumor "used to be called DIPG". That is only true of the
ones in the pons.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes. A diffuse midline glioma is cancer.** The word your team will use is
**malignant**.

**It is always grade 4.** Grade 4 is the top of the scale.

**This tumor grows into the healthy tissue around it**, so there is no edge to
cut around. With today's treatments, it is not curable. That is a hard sentence,
and it is here on purpose, because hearing the grade without hearing this can
leave people shocked later.

**Not curable is not the same as untreatable, and it is not a timeline.**
Treatment can ease symptoms and slow the tumor for a time, and there are still
choices if it grows.

### But the cells did not look grade 4

This surprises people, and it is not a mistake. **Since 2016, a tumor with this
name is grade 4**, even when the cells under the microscope look like a lower
grade.

You may read that one of these can look low grade. That is about how the cells
look. It does not change the grade of a tumor with this name.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

It grows in the middle of the brain or in the spinal cord, and **where it sits
decides what you notice**.

- **In the pons**, part of the brain stem. The pons carries the nerves for
  vision, hearing, speech, swallowing and movement.
- **In the thalamus**, deep in the middle of the brain. What you notice depends
  on the exact spot, so ask your team which of your symptoms it explains.
- **In the spinal cord.** Here it can cause weakness, numbness, and trouble
  with the bladder or bowel.

In the brain, it can also stop fluid draining away. The section below explains
what that does.

## What symptoms does it cause?

In the brain stem, the commonest are:

- Double vision, or eyes that do not move together.
- One side of the face drooping.
- Trouble with balance or walking.
- Drooling, or trouble swallowing.
- Slurred speech.
- Weakness in an arm and a leg on one side.

From pressure inside the head: headaches that are worse in the morning, nausea
and throwing up, and being very sleepy.

In the spinal cord: weakness that keeps getting worse, numbness, and trouble
controlling the bladder or bowel. For a tumor in the spinal cord, these have
their own rule, at the end of the section on when to call for help, below.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

A scan first, and then, more often now, a piece of the tumor.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri).** For a child with the typical picture in the
  pons, the scan can be enough to start treatment.
  [DIPG](/tumors/dipg#finding-out) explains that choice.
- **In adults, a scan alone cannot make this diagnosis**, because the tumor can
  look like other fast-growing brain tumors.
- **A tissue sample**, by a [needle biopsy](/tests/biopsy) or during an
  operation, tested for the [H3 K27 change](/tests/molecular-markers#h3-k27).

Biopsy is done more often than it used to be. Part of the reason is that many
clinical trials, and the newer drug below, need that gene result.

## What do the words on my report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

The results that matter most on this report:

- **Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered.** The full current name. The gene
  change is part of the name.
- **Grade 4.** Always, for this name.
- **[H3 K27M](/tests/molecular-markers#h3-k27).** An older way of writing it.
  Both may describe the same tumor, and that page explains why there are two.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the document
itself.

### If your older paperwork says something different

- **Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27M-mutant.** The 2016 name for the same tumor.
  It does not change your diagnosis.
- **DIPG**, or **brain stem glioma.** Older names that said where the tumor sat.
  Brain stem glioma can also include low-grade tumors that are not this one, so
  it is worth asking which yours is. If yours is in the pons,
  [DIPG](/tumors/dipg) is the page for you.

## How is it usually treated?

[TUMOR-BOARD]

- **Surgery, where it can be done safely.** In the pons it cannot be done safely,
  because the tumor grows into parts the body cannot do without. Elsewhere,
  taking some of it out may be possible.
- **[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy)** is the main treatment.
- **[Steroids](/treatments/steroids)** to bring down swelling.
- **[A shunt](/treatments/shunts)**, if the fluid around the brain is blocked.

### Chemotherapy, and one honest note about it {#chemotherapy}

The chemotherapy drugs used for other gliomas **have not been shown to help
this one.** Some teams still give it, in a trial or outside one, and there is no
standard plan. If it is offered, it is fair to ask what it is meant to do.

### Dordaviprone, the drug for this gene change {#dordaviprone}

**Dordaviprone** is a capsule taken by mouth once a week. Its brand name is
Modeyso. In 2025 the FDA approved it for adults and children aged 1 and over
with a diffuse midline glioma that has the H3 K27M change and has kept growing
after other treatment.

It was given **accelerated approval**. That means it was approved early, based
on how many tumors shrank and for how long. **Trials are still checking whether
it helps people live longer.** Until they report, nobody can tell you that it
does.

Most of the people in the studies behind the approval were adults. Those studies
also left out DIPG and tumors that started in the spinal cord. So for a child, or
for a tumor in one of those places, ask your team what they know about it.

It needs the gene result, so ask whether yours was tested.
[Targeted drugs](/treatments/targeted-therapy) covers what being on it involves.
If the tumor is in the pons, [DIPG](/tumors/dipg#dordaviprone) has more on it.

### Clinical trials, and why to ask early

Some trials start at diagnosis, some after radiation, and some later. **Asking
at the start keeps the most options open**, as long as it does not hold up
radiation your team says is urgent. Ask your team which
[clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) you or your child could join, and
what testing that needs.

- **Tiredness** is common during and after radiation. It can come from the

Ask early who at the hospital helps with school, work and money.

## If it comes back, or changes

If the tumor grows again, there are still choices. Your team may talk about a
clinical trial, **dordaviprone**, radiation again, or care that focuses only on
comfort.

**Radiation a second time is something doctors disagree about.** For DIPG, St.
Jude says it can be done safely, though the benefit is more limited. A 2022
review of DIPG says it can help slow the tumor, but it has significant risks and
burdens, and is not usually recommended. Ask your team whether it is an option
for you, and what it would be for.

**No choice here is the wrong one.** Ask your team what each option is for, and
write the answers down.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**Will my other children get this?** This tumor is not passed down in families.
It is nobody's fault, and nothing done in pregnancy or early childhood has been
shown to cause it.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It helps to know what those
numbers are before you decide whether you want them.

Those numbers describe people who were treated years ago. Many older studies
grouped tumors by where they sat, not by the gene change, so an older figure may
describe a mixed group. The tumor has also been studied less in adults than in
children.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up, the median is the person standing in the middle. It is
not a prediction about any one person.

The shape of the group matters as much as the middle. A few live well beyond it,
and a median cannot show you them. Some studies suggest the tumor can move more
slowly in adults than in children, though it varies widely. For a
tumor in the pons, [DIPG](/tumors/dipg) has what is known.

Your own team knows your situation, and a page written for everybody does
not. They can talk it through with you whenever you want to.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Two things specific to this diagnosis.

**Many people caring for someone with this tumor are parents.** Everything above
still applies, and so does asking early for the palliative care team. That team
is there for the whole family.

**If the tumor is in the brain stem, watch swallowing.** New trouble swallowing,
or new drooling, means calling your team the same day. Food or drink can go down
into the lungs. Choking, or trouble breathing, is an ambulance call.

## What to ask your team

- Where exactly is the tumor, and is it in the pons?
- Was it tested for the H3 K27 change? Was a biopsy done, and if not, why not?
- What is radiation meant to do, and how long will it take?
- Is chemotherapy being offered, and what would it be for?
- Is dordaviprone an option now, or later?
- Which clinical trials could we join, and do any of them need to start before
  radiation?
- When should we meet the palliative care team?
- What should we know about the steroids, and what is the plan for coming off?
- What would make you want to see us before the next appointment?
- Who do we call after hours, and what number is it?
