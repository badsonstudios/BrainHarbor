## The short version

**This page is for a parent.** ATRT is a rare, fast-growing cancer that starts in
the brain or the spine. **It usually happens in very young children**, most
often before age 3.

**The full name is atypical teratoid/rhabdoid tumor.** You may see it written
ATRT or AT/RT. They are the same tumor.

**Treatment usually has three parts**: an operation, then strong chemotherapy,
and then radiation for some children. **Your child's age changes the plan, especially
the radiation part of it.**

Some signs mean calling your team today. A few mean going in now. They are in
their own section below.

## What is ATRT?

**It is a cancer.** Your team may use the word malignant. It grows quickly, and
it can spread through the fluid that moves around the brain and the spine.

**Most children with this tumor are babies or toddlers.** Half are diagnosed at
about 1 year old or younger. About two in three are found before age 2. Older
children get it, and so do adults, but that is rare.

**About half of these tumors begin in the lower rear of the brain**, the
cerebellum or the brain stem. Others begin higher up. A small number begin in the
spinal cord itself.

It is an !%embryonal tumor%**embryonal tumor**. That is a group of tumors that
[medulloblastoma](/tumors/medulloblastoma) also belongs to. The rules that name
brain tumors put them in separate groups inside it.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Yes, it is cancer, and doctors grade it 4.** That is the highest step on the
scale.

**Grade describes how the cells look, not how long anyone will live.** Under a
microscope, a pathologist compares tumor cells with normal ones. Cells that look
close to normal are a low grade. Cells that look very different are a high grade.
Higher grades may grow and spread more quickly.

**Every tumor in this group is grade 4.** The rules that name brain tumors put
all embryonal tumors at grade 4 together, rather than giving this one its own
number. So the grade tells you the family your child's tumor belongs to. It does
not sort one child's tumor from another's.

**Grades are now written as ordinary numbers**, so grade 4 rather than grade IV.
If an older letter uses Roman numerals, it means the same thing.

What matters more than the number is where the tumor is, whether it has spread,
and what your team can do about it. Those are the next sections.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**Start with the fluid.** The brain is bathed in a watery fluid. It circles the
brain, runs down around the cord, and drains off. A growth in the wrong spot can
dam that route. Fluid then pools behind the blockage, and pressure inside the
head climbs. Doctors call that !%hydrocephalus%**hydrocephalus**.

**And here is the part that works differently in a baby.** In an adult the skull
is locked shut, so rising pressure has nowhere to go.
**In a baby the skull can still give.** The bony plates have not knitted together
yet, and there is a gap near the crown, under the skin, where they have yet to
meet. So the head itself can enlarge, and that gap can push outward.

That makes the early warning harder to read, in two ways.

- **It changes the warning.** The pressure can show up in the head itself, not
  only in the headaches an older child could describe.
- **It disguises things.** The early signs are the ordinary ones on the list
  below: unsettled, feeding poorly, sleeping more.

**This is why a head that is growing too fast is taken seriously.** It is not a
small finding.

**The tumor can travel in that fluid too.** Loose cells drift with the current
and can take hold somewhere else, higher up or lower down the cord. That is why
your team images the whole spine and samples the fluid, and it is part of why
treatment often has to reach the entire nervous system instead of one spot.

**That is a lot to take in, and none of it is yours to work out.** Your team does
the finding. What this section is for is making the words they use make sense
when they use them.

## What symptoms does it cause in a baby or young child?

**A toddler cannot tell you they have a headache.** So the things you notice are
behavior and body, not descriptions.

The signs to look out for:

- **A head that is growing faster than it should**, or a soft spot that looks
  full or pushed out.
- **Throwing up**, often in the morning, sometimes with a child who seems better
  afterwards.
- **Being much sleepier than usual**, or harder to wake.
- **Being very unsettled or irritable**, or not seeming like themselves.
- **Feeding badly**, or losing skills they already had.
- **Trouble with balance or walking**, or being unsteady and clumsy.
- **Unusual eye or face movements.**

Seizures can happen too.

**A head growing faster than it should is a reason to call, not a reason to
wait.** No source we found puts an hour on it, so this page does not invent one.
Only one of the hospital pages about this tumor gives any instruction at all, and
it is plainer than a tier: if something has changed and you are worried, do not
wait, call a doctor or get your child seen.

**Symptoms can build quickly**, so a short history is not a reason to wait and
see.

### The signs that mean going in now {#going-in-now}

**These rules come from the signs, not from the tumor.** That is worth saying
out loud. The organizations that write about ATRT describe the symptoms and do
not tell you when to go to an emergency room. The clear emergency advice that
does exist is written about particular warning signs, and that is what is below.

**A soft spot that is bulging while your baby is calm and sitting up means going
in now.** Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room. A soft spot often looks
full when a baby is crying, lying down or throwing up, and that is ordinary. The
test is what it does when your baby is settled and held upright. If it goes back
to soft and flat, that is the normal kind. **If it stays bulging when your baby
is calm and upright, that is the emergency.** It matters more if there is also a
fever, or your baby is very drowsy.

**Rising pressure inside the head is treated as an emergency in its own right.**
Cleveland Clinic says to go to the nearest hospital for it, and names a brain
tumor as one of the things that can cause it. **In a baby it names
drowsiness, a bulging soft spot, and throwing up.** Those signs are printed here
rather than left on the page they come from. A rule you cannot match to something
you can see is no use at two in the morning.

**If your child has a shunt, the rules change and get stronger.** A shunt is a
thin tube that drains the fluid. Three things are worth knowing by heart.

- **If you cannot wake your child, go to the nearest emergency room right away,
  or call 911.**
- **If you think the shunt is infected, tell your neurosurgeon immediately, or go
  to the emergency room.** Signs include a fever, or redness or swelling at the
  shunt site.
- **If you think the shunt has stopped working, get medical help immediately.**
  Being hard to wake or hard to keep awake needs urgent attention whatever your
  child's age.

[Shunts and hydrocephalus](/treatments/shunts#warning-signs) has the fuller list of signs that
a shunt is not working.

**When in doubt, make the call.** You know your child better than any page does,
and a parent who calls about nothing has still done the right thing.

patient, so every "you" in it means them. Parts of it were written with adults in
mind and will not fit a baby at all.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri) of the brain, plus a separate one down the cord.**
  That second picture is taken because this tumor can travel in the fluid. Some
  children are given medicine to help them sleep through a scan. Many children
  manage without.
  [When your child has a brain tumor](/tumors/pediatric-brain-tumor) covers what
  that is like.
- **[An operation](/treatments/craniotomy), or a piece of the tumor.** The tissue
  that names the tumor comes from surgery.
  [Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) unpacks the document that
  comes back.
- **A test of the spinal fluid.** A small amount of fluid is taken, usually from
  the lower back, and the laboratory looks for tumor cells in it.
- **Gene tests on the tumor tissue.** These are what confirm the diagnosis. See
  below.
- **An ultrasound of the kidney.** There is a good reason for it. The same gene change can cause a tumor of the kidney, so the
  kidney may be checked too.

**The gene test is the one that settles it.** This tumor is defined by a missing
gene, not by how the cells look. The usual one is called SMARCB1, which you may
also see written INI1. Less often it is a gene called SMARCA4, also written BRG1.

**What the laboratory actually looks for is a missing protein.** The tumor cells
have lost it, while the normal cells around them still have it. That pattern is
what confirms ATRT.

[Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) deals with the delay itself,
which most families find harder than the tests.

## What do the words on my child's report mean?

[CROSSWALK]

### There is no official stage for this tumor

**This one catches people out.** You may see staging words on your child's
report, and there is no agreed staging system for ATRT. The National Cancer
Institute says so plainly. So the words describe what was found. They are not
steps on an official ladder.

**What your team sorts by is simpler than a stage.** The National Cancer
Institute groups this tumor as newly diagnosed or as one that has come back, and
says treatment turns on your child's age, how much of the tumor is left after
surgery, and whether it has spread to other parts of the brain and spine.

**One word is worth knowing, because it is where this tumor tends to go.**

- **Leptomeningeal** means cells were found in the spinal fluid and the thin
  coverings around the brain and cord. St. Jude describes that as where ATRT
  usually spreads.

**In some children it has already spread by the time it is found.** Published
figures put that at somewhere between about one in seven and one in three.
It usually spreads through the spinal fluid. That is why the spine scan and the
fluid test are done at the start rather than later.

If you see a letter and a number, such as M and a digit, ask your team what it
means for your child. That shorthand is borrowed from another tumor, and no
source applies it to this one.

### Older names, and names that are not this tumor

- **PNET**, or primitive neuroectodermal tumor. **This name is retired.** Before
  the gene test existed, this name covered many tumors now known to be
  different. The gene test is what separated them.
- **Rhabdoid tumor.** This is the family name. The same kind of tumor can grow in
  the kidney or elsewhere in the body. ATRT is the one in the brain and spine.
- **ETMR** is a different embryonal tumor, not this one, and it is defined by a
  different gene change.

## How is it usually treated?

**Treatment is intensive, and it usually has three parts.**

**An operation is usually the opening move.** The goal is to remove as much as
the surgeon safely can, and how much that turns out to be depends on where the
growth sits and what lies beside it. [What an operation
involves](/treatments/craniotomy) covers the rest.

**Blocked fluid may need to be relieved as well.** Your team may use a
short-term drain, or [a shunt](/treatments/shunts).

**[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy) comes next, and it is strong.** Two
things about it are specific to this tumor.

- **Medicine is often put straight into the spinal fluid.** Boston Children's
  Hospital calls this an important part of treatment, because it delivers the
  medicine directly to the spinal fluid, which is where this tumor travels. It is
  given either through a small reservoir placed under the scalp, or by an
  injection in the lower back.
- **Some children have very high doses, with their own stem cells given back
  afterwards.** The team collects some of your child's own blood-forming stem
  cells first, and they are frozen for later. The high doses damage the bone
  marrow, and giving the cells back lets it recover.

**[Radiation](/treatments/radiation-therapy) is the part that turns on your
child's age**, and this is the decision parents ask about most.

- **Under 3, radiation is often reduced, aimed at a smaller area, or left out
  altogether.** Radiation affects a brain that is still developing.
- **Over 3, it is generally part of standard treatment.**
- **You may hear about [proton therapy](/treatments/proton-therapy).** Ask what
  your team would expect it to spare, and whether they would use it for a child
  this young.

Ask your team where they draw that age line, and why.

**Ask about a clinical trial early, because here it is the usual route rather
than a last resort.** One US children's hospital says most children with this
diagnosis are treated as part of a brain tumor trial. There is also a registry
just for this tumor, which collects information to help research on a cancer too
rare for any one hospital to study alone. [Clinical
trials](/treatments/clinical-trials) covers how to think about one.

**A note on the three types you may read about.** Researchers sort ATRT into
three groups using a laboratory test on the tumor's DNA. Today that mostly helps
research and trials. Specialists say plainly that what those groups mean for
treatment is still unsettled.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

### After surgery low at the back of the brain {#posterior-fossa-syndrome}

**About half of these tumors grow in the lower rear of the brain**, the
cerebellum or the brain stem. **This part is for you if your child was operated
on there.** That is not the same as where the tumor sits, because what can be
removed, and how, depends on the exact spot. Doctors call what can follow that
operation **posterior fossa syndrome**.

[POSTERIOR-FOSSA-SYNDROME]

**How often it follows an operation for this particular tumor is not published.**
The clearest hospital pages about it were written for different growths in that
same region. It is reported after this operation, whatever the growth was.
Before the operation, ask your surgeon what they have seen and what they will be
watching for.

### The everyday mechanics

**Your child may have a central line.** It is used for children who are seriously
ill and need treatment for a long time. It is a soft tube that
stays in, and it means fewer needle sticks for medicines, fluids and blood
samples. Keeping the site clean and dry becomes a daily job, and washing your
hands before and after any line care is not a formality.

**Blood counts fall after chemotherapy and then recover.** When they are low, a
common cold is not a small thing. **A fever during treatment means telling your
team right away, whatever the hour.** You will not always know whether the counts
are down. Your team will give you the reading that counts as a fever, and it is
theirs rather than one from a page. [The fever
rule](/treatments/chemotherapy#fever-rule) explains why teams differ.

**Eating can become a problem**, and a feeding tube is a common procedure in
children with cancer. It is not a defeat. It is how a small child gets through
a long stretch of treatment without losing ground, and good nutrition
matters for growth, energy and healing.

**Hearing is tested, and not just as a precaution.** Two of the medicines used
here, cisplatin and carboplatin, can damage hearing, and high sounds are affected
first. In a child who is still learning to talk, hearing affects speech and
development, and finding a problem early is what lets somebody act on it. Ask
when your child's hearing will be tested and how often.

## Everyday life: feeding, development, hearing and school

**This section is about a child who is still learning everything**, which is what
makes it different from the same section on any adult page.

**Development is the thing to watch, and to act on early.** Treatment at this age
can slow how fast new skills are picked up. Often the issue is not that your
child loses what they had. It is that new things come more slowly than they
would have.

**Early intervention is the most useful thing on this page.** Support exists for
very young children. St. Jude puts it plainly: **early intervention and paying
close attention to symptoms can help.** [When your child has a brain
tumor](/tumors/pediatric-brain-tumor) sets out the two school routes for the
years ahead, and says that children under 3 come under a different part of the
law behind one of them, for early intervention. Ask your team who makes that
referral.

**Hearing again, because it connects to speech.** If hearing is affected, finding
it early is what lets somebody do something about it while your child is learning
to talk.

**School comes sooner than it feels.** Ask the hospital who helps with school
before you need them.

**Nothing in this section is about driving, work or money**, which the same
section carries on the adult tumor pages. They will matter one day. They do not
matter this year, and pretending otherwise would waste your attention.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

**Follow-up carries on after treatment ends.** Your team sets the rhythm. No published
schedule exists for us to point you to, so ask yours for theirs and write it
down.

**Scans are not the whole of follow-up.** Hearing and hormone levels are checked
as well.

[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) explains what a new picture gets
measured against, and [waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers
the stretch before an answer arrives, which many families find worse than the
scan itself.

If something new appears, report it that day instead of storing it up for the
next visit.

## If it comes back, or changes

**There is no standard treatment for this tumor if it comes back**, and a major
US cancer center says exactly that. The same center says [a
trial](/treatments/clinical-trials) is how children reach the treatments that
are still being tested.

Ask your team what they would weigh: which treatments your child has already
had, where the growth has appeared, and how they are doing now.

**Ask what the aim of any new treatment would be**, and write the reply down as
it comes. The purpose is not always what it was the first time around, and asking
that outright is entirely fair.

## Did I cause this?

**Before the general answer below, one thing is specific to this tumor, and it
runs the other way.**

**For ATRT, an inherited gene change is more common than it is for most brain
tumors.** Roughly one child in four to one in three with a rhabdoid tumor was
born with a change in the gene involved. That is not most children, and it is not
rare either.

**This is why every family should be offered genetic counseling.** Cancer genetics
experts recommend that every child diagnosed with this tumor is referred, whatever
the family history. A genetic counselor explains what a test would and would not
tell you, and helps you decide whether anyone else in the family should be tested.

**If your child does carry that change, it has a name**: rhabdoid tumor
predisposition syndrome. It matters because the same change can cause tumors
elsewhere, most often the kidney. Some teams then recommend a brain MRI and an
ultrasound of the belly at diagnosis, and regularly afterwards. How often is not
set, so ask yours.

**None of that means anybody did anything.** A gene change you were born with is
not a choice anybody made. If it turns out a parent carries it too, that is
information for looking after your family, not a verdict on anyone.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Outlook usually gets discussed in figures. Before you decide whether to look at
any, it helps to know how such figures are built and what they can honestly tell
you.

**For this tumor, the honest answer is that the numbers are thin.** Cleveland
Clinic says plainly that because it is so rare, there is not enough information to
give a survival rate or a cure rate. Any figure you find elsewhere is built from
very few children, which is why none is published here.

One word will come up: **median**. Treat it as a halfway mark. Sort a whole group
of children by how long they lived, and the median sits at the halfway point of
that sorted list.

Three things follow from that, and they matter more than the mark itself.

- **It is a fact about a crowd**, so it cannot forecast one child.
- **Half of that crowd is above it, and the upper half has no ceiling.** The
  children who did far better than the mark are inside the figure, hidden by it.
- **It is built from children treated years ago**, under plans that have since
  changed.

What your team can see, and no page can, is your child: the scans, what came out
at surgery, and how your child has been since. Put the question to them. And
leaving it unasked for now is a real choice, for as long as you need.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Four things specific to this diagnosis.

**Almost everyone reading this is a parent of a very small child**, so the whole
of the section above is written to you rather than about somebody else. Asking
your team to go over the same ground twice is completely reasonable.

**Two to know by heart**, and the rest are in [the signs that mean going in
now](#going-in-now): a soft spot that stays bulging when your
baby is calm and upright, and a child with a shunt who cannot be woken. You may
well spot either one before anybody else does, and acting on it is not
overreacting.

**One of you may end up living at the hospital.** Where high-dose treatment with
stem cells is used, stays are long, a caregiver is expected to stay, and it is
better if more than one person is trained to do the care. Work out early who that
second person is.

**Brothers and sisters need telling too.** Some may believe they caused it,
so St. Jude tells parents to say plainly that nothing anyone can think, say or
do makes someone else get sick. It also suggests keeping school and hobbies
going, and saying out loud that they are being brave as well.

## What to ask your team

- What is my child's tumor called, and is there a gene result yet?
- Has the spine been scanned, and has the spinal fluid been tested?
- Was the whole growth removed, and what did the scan afterwards show?
- Will treatment include medicine into the spinal fluid, and how will it be given?
- Is high-dose treatment with my child's own stem cells part of the plan?
- Where do you draw the age line for radiation, and why?
- Is proton therapy an option for us?
- Is there a clinical trial or a registry we should be talking about?
- Should our family see a genetic counselor, and when?
- Does my child need a kidney ultrasound, and how often?
- When will hearing be tested, and who follows it up?
- Who refers us for early intervention, and can we start now?
- What number do you use for a fever, and who do we call after hours?
