## The short version

An acoustic neuroma is a growth on the nerve that runs between your inner ear
and your brain. It is not cancer, and it usually grows very slowly.

It sits in a narrow space where the cerebellum meets the brainstem. Because of
where it is, the first signs are almost always on one side: hearing that fades
in one ear, ringing in that ear, or trouble with balance.

You will see two names for it. Your report may say **vestibular schwannoma**,
which is the more exact one. Both names mean the same growth.

Most people do not need an operation right away. The three choices are
watching it, focused radiation, and surgery. **Your hearing is part of this
decision, not just the size on the scan.**

One sign here is worth knowing before you need it: hearing that drops suddenly
in one ear should be looked at fast. It is [in the symptoms
section](#symptoms), below the general list of when to call.

## What is an acoustic neuroma?

**It grows from the cells that wrap a nerve, not from the nerve itself.**

The eighth of the nerves that come straight off the brain carries two things
from your inner ear. One branch carries sound. The other carries balance.

Around every nerve are cells that wrap it, the way the skin of an onion wraps
what is inside. They support the nerve and insulate it. This growth starts when
too many of those wrapping cells are made in one spot.

**It sits in a tight corner.** It begins in the narrow bony channel the nerve
runs through, and it can widen out into the narrow corner where the brainstem
meets the cerebellum, low down at the back of your head. That corner is why
balance, and later the face, can be involved.

**It grows beside the brain, not inside it.** That is a real difference and not
a form of words. It also does not settle everything, because a growth in that
corner can still press on what is next to it.

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**No. It is not cancer, and it does not spread to other parts of the body.**

The word benign will turn up, in a letter or in conversation. **It tells you
what this growth will not do. It says nothing about your hearing**, and hearing
is what it usually costs people. So the word is true, and it is not an answer
to the question you are actually asking.

**Where a grade is given at all, this one sits at the bottom of the scale.**
Clinical references describe this growth as a grade 1 tumor, which is the
slowest-growing kind. Older paperwork writes that same grade as grade I, in
Roman numerals.

Two things about that grade are worth keeping apart, because your report may
show either style. **The convention changed in 2021**, when ordinary digits took
over from Roman numerals, which is why two letters about one growth can look
unalike. The grade itself comes from the clinical
references rather than from that 2021 document, which lists this growth without
printing a grade beside it at all.

**What matters more is which nerve it is sitting on, and how your hearing is
doing.** Both come further down.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

**Nearly everything on this page follows from one fact: it grows on the nerve
that carries hearing and balance.**

The growth usually starts on the balance branch. That is the first reason the
old name is a poor fit, and there is a second: it is made of the wrapping
cells, not of nerve cells. So **acoustic neuroma** names the wrong branch and
the wrong cell. Your team may still use it, because it is the name most people
know.

Trouble arrives in a few ways.

**It presses on the hearing branch.** Hearing fades on one side. Most often it
goes slowly, over years. Speech can start to sound muffled on that side,
especially on the phone or in a noisy room.

**It irritates the same nerve.** Ringing, buzzing or hissing in that one ear is
common, and for some people it is the first thing they notice.

**It presses on the balance branch.** This one often causes less than people
expect. Because the growth is so slow, the other side has time to take over,
and the body compensates. So unsteadiness here is more often a vague thing than
a spinning attack.

**If it grows, it can reach what is next door.** Numbness or tingling in the
face can come from the nerve that sits nearby. Weakness of the face is unusual
with this growth, and worth saying plainly, because it is what people fear when
they read about a tumor near the face.

**Before you read the next part.** What comes below describes growths sitting
inside the brain itself. Two of the things it lists are not how this one
behaves: it does not set off seizures the way a growth inside the brain does,
and it does not make the brain around it swell. If you ever did have a seizure,
the ambulance rule below applies to you as much as to anyone. Two others do
apply, but only if a growth gets large: pressure building up inside the head,
and fluid being blocked on its way out. In the map of brain areas after that,
the entry about the skull base, below and behind the ear, is the closest fit of
the three that are about this growth. One thing in it is uncommon here: facial
weakness, which this growth usually causes only once it is large. If it is new,
the same-day list further down is the one to go by. The cerebellum entry is this growth's
territory too, balance and walking. The brainstem entry describes what can
happen only once a growth is big enough to press on it.

## What symptoms does it cause? {#symptoms}

**Almost always on one side, and almost always the ear first.**

The three that bring most people to a doctor are **hearing loss in one ear**,
**ringing in that ear**, and **trouble with balance**.

The reason it is often found late is that none of those is alarming on its own.
Hearing that slips over years gets put down to age. Ringing gets ignored.
Balance that is slightly off gets blamed on tiredness. **One-sided is the part
that matters.** Hearing that is worse in one ear than the other is worth
mentioning, even when it seems small.

If it grows, it can add numbness or tingling on that side of the face.
Headache, being sick, and trouble with walking come later and only with larger
growths.

**One sign here has a rule of its own.** It comes after the lists below.

[ESCALATION]

**This growth has one rule the list above does not carry.** There is only one,
and it is worth reading today rather than at the moment you need it.

**If the hearing in one ear drops suddenly, get it looked at fast.** Sudden
means over a few days, or all at once, rather than over months. It is not the
same as the slow fade described above.

Ask to be seen quickly rather than waiting for an appointment. Your
own doctor, an urgent care clinic, or an ear, nose and throat specialist are all
places that can start this. The National Institute on Deafness and Other
Communication Disorders calls sudden hearing loss a medical emergency and says
to see a doctor immediately, and a national hearing-loss charity says the same
and names those three places to go.

**Most sudden hearing loss is not caused by a growth like this one.** The
specialist guideline for sudden hearing loss says a growth on this nerve is only
rarely behind it. The reason to move fast is that treatment for sudden hearing
loss works better the sooner it starts, whatever the cause turns out to be.

**If you already know you have one of these**, a sudden change is a reason to
contact your team rather than wait for your next scan.
[What to report between scans](/treatments/watch-and-wait#step-by-step) sets out
that rule, and it applies to any new change, not only to hearing.

**What is NOT on this page is a checklist for a growth that has got large.**
The general list above covers pressure signs for brain tumors as a whole, and
it applies to you as much as to anyone. What this page does not add is a
separate set of signs for this growth in particular. That is deliberate. The
reports describing pressure on the brainstem are written for surgeons deciding
on an operation, and none of them tells a patient what to do or when to call.
The patient organizations say the opposite of act now: for most people this
growth is not something that has to be treated urgently. So this page does not
invent a rule its own sources do not give. What your team watches for is size
and change, and that is what the scans are for.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

**A hearing test first, then a scan.**

**The hearing test is the one that raises the question.** It is not the check
you have for glasses or the one at a work physical. It measures each ear on its
own, across a range of pitches, and it looks for a difference between the two
sides. A one-sided pattern is what leads to the scan.

**An [MRI scan](/tests/mri) is what shows it**, and it is what tells this apart
from other reasons for one-sided hearing loss. The scan shows where the growth
sits, how big it is, and what it is touching.

**Balance testing is sometimes added**, because the balance branch can be
affected without you noticing much.

**Tissue is not usually taken to make the diagnosis.** The scan and the hearing
test are enough to say what this is. That is unlike most of the tumors on this
site, and it is why a report may describe the growth without a piece of it ever
having been looked at under a microscope.

## What do the words on my report mean?

**Two names for this growth can appear in your paperwork, and both of them are
correct.**

**Acoustic neuroma, and vestibular schwannoma.** These are the same thing. The
second is more exact, for the two reasons above: the growth usually starts on
the balance branch rather than the hearing one, and it is made of the cells that
wrap the nerve. Many doctors prefer the newer name. The older one is still used
more often in writing, so you will meet both.

**Grade 1, and grade I on older paperwork.** One grade, written two ways.
Ordinary digits replaced Roman numerals when the convention changed in 2021, and
a great many letters were never rewritten.

**Schwann cells.** The cells that wrap and insulate nerves. They are what this
growth is made of, and they are where the name schwannoma comes from.

**NF2-related schwannomatosis.** This is the name of an inherited condition, and
it is not a diagnosis of the growth you have. It is here because you may meet
the name while reading, and because the name itself changed recently. **Older
letters use neurofibromatosis type 2.** An international group retired that
older name in 2022, to make the diagnosis more exact. A reference for doctors
records that the newer name was proposed by that group, to fit what the
condition actually causes.

**What that condition means for you depends on two things: whether there is a
growth on both sides, and your age.** Growths on both nerves are the mark of
that condition, and they are uncommon. The large majority of these growths are
on one side only and are not inherited. The reference above puts the rest
plainly: someone under thirty with a growth on one side is watched more closely,
because there is a real chance of one appearing on the other side, while
someone over thirty with a growth on one side has a very low chance of having
the condition at all.

**If it is in your family, ask.** About half of the people who have that
condition inherited it from a parent. The other half are the first in their
family to have it. Your team can say whether anyone else should be checked.

[Your pathology report](/tests/pathology-report) goes through a report made from
tissue, though yours may not be one.

## How is it usually treated?

**There are three choices, and doing nothing for now is one of them.** A federal
health institute lists them as watching, radiation, and surgery.

**Watching.** For a small growth that is not causing much trouble, a plan of
scans and hearing tests is often the first choice rather than a delay. The
European guideline puts the clearest case for it where a growth was found by
chance and is causing no symptoms. Many of these never need treating at all.
[Watching a tumor with scans](/treatments/watch-and-wait) is the page for that
plan, and it carries [a passage on this growth in
particular](/treatments/watch-and-wait#if-it-grows).

**Focused radiation.** The aim is usually to control the growth rather than to
take it out. Doctors call this stereotactic radiosurgery. [The day itself, and
what it is like](/treatments/stereotactic-radiosurgery) is a page of its own.

**Surgery.** An operation to take the growth out. It is the usual choice when a
growth is large, and for the largest ones it is the only way to take pressure
off the brainstem. [What an operation involves, and the weeks
after](/treatments/craniotomy) has the general aftercare.

**How the choice gets made, honestly.** Size matters, and so does your hearing,
your balance, your age and what you want. **No one option is better at
everything.** One comparison found that dizziness and ringing tended to do
better after surgery, while the nerve to the face came out about the same
whichever route was taken. The European guideline reaches a different conclusion
about that nerve for smaller growths. **The reports disagree**, and both sets of
authors say why: nobody has ever run the kind of study that would settle it, so
all of it comes from watching what happened rather than from a trial.

**Two things are genuinely unsettled**, and it is fair to ask about both. There
is no agreed point at which watching should turn into treating. And for growths
in the middle range of sizes, there is no settled best answer.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

### What happens to hearing

**This is the part people most want a straight answer about, and the honest
answer has two halves.**

Hearing on that side tends to get worse over time whatever is done, including
when the growth is only being watched. It can get worse even in a year when the
growth has not grown at all. That is worth knowing, because "we are just
watching it" can sound like "nothing is changing".

The other half is that treatment does not protect hearing from that. A review
of the evidence found that surgery and focused radiation both appear to speed up
a decline that was already happening, **and the same sentence says more research
is needed, because the evidence is weak**. Every recommendation in that review
sits at its lowest level of confidence. Both halves belong together: it is not a
reason to refuse treatment, and it is not a promise that waiting keeps your
hearing.

**So ask what your own hearing is now, and ask what each choice is expected to
do to it.** That question, asked early, is worth more than any figure.

### Balance, the face, and the first weeks

**Balance often settles.** The other ear takes over the work, and most people
adjust, though it can take weeks and it is worse in the dark or on uneven
ground. Ask whether balance therapy would help you.

**The nerve to the face runs right beside this growth**, which is why it comes
up in every conversation about treatment. Weakness of the face is not a common
way for this growth to show up in the first place. After treatment it is a real
risk, and it is bigger with larger growths.

**Ringing does not always go.** It can stay after successful treatment, and for
some people it is the symptom that bothers them most afterwards. Say so if it
does, because it can sometimes be treated in its own right.

**Tiredness after an operation is heavier than people expect**, and it lifts
slowly.

## Everyday life

**Hearing on one side changes some ordinary things**, and none of them is
obvious until it happens. Working out which direction a sound came from gets
harder. So do noisy rooms, restaurants and group conversations.

Small things help more than they sound like they will. Sit with your better ear
toward the room. Ask people to move to that side. Tell the people you see
often, so they stop repeating themselves into the wrong ear.

**Ask about a hearing aid or another device**, including for the ear that has
lost the most. It is worth raising rather than waiting to be told.

If your balance is off, be careful on stairs and in the dark, when your eyes
cannot help you.

**Tiredness is real here**, and it is not only about sleep. Straining to hear
all day is work, and it is a common reason people feel wiped out by the evening.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Here, follow-up means scans and hearing tests side by side, carrying on for
years whether or not you have had treatment.

[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) explains how one scan gets read
against the last. [Waiting for results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the
stretch before anyone calls you, which many people find worse than the
appointment.

Get your own plan written down: when the next scan is, what would bring it
forward, and who to tell if something changes in between.

## If it comes back

**After an operation, some of these can grow again.** That can happen when a
piece was deliberately left behind to protect a nerve, which is a recognized
choice rather than a mistake, and one of the things to ask about beforehand.

After focused radiation, the aim was to control the growth rather than remove
it, so a scan that shows it unchanged is the result that was wanted.

If something does change, the choices are the same as before, and which of them
fits depends on what was done the first time.

## Did I cause this?

[CAUSES]

**For this growth there is one thing worth adding.** The old story that cell
phones cause growths on this nerve has been looked at specifically, and it is
not supported. Nothing you did with a phone brought this on.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
The numbers people meet for this growth are not about how long anybody lives.
They are mostly about hearing, and about whether a growth stayed the same size.
Those two get mixed up, and a number read as the wrong one is frightening for no
reason.

Such numbers as exist were gathered by following people treated a long while
back, not by running trials. The people who gathered them say so themselves, and
rate their own confidence in them at the lowest level they have. That is not a
detail to skip over. It means the figures describe what happened to a group, and
cannot tell you what will happen to your ear.

The things that truly decide how this goes are more specific than any figure,
and your team can see every one of them: which nerve is involved, where your
hearing stands today, whether anything is changing, and what you most want to
protect.

Ask whenever it suits you. Choosing to leave it is a decision as well, and
nothing stops you coming back to it.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

Three things belong to this one in particular.

**One-sided hearing loss is invisible, and it gets read as rudeness.** Not
answering, answering the wrong question, going quiet in a group: all of that
looks like inattention and is not. Sit on their better side, and say their name
before you start.

**Learn the one urgent rule with them.** If the hearing in one ear drops
suddenly, it should be looked at fast, by their own doctor, an urgent care
clinic or an ear specialist. You may be the one who notices they have stopped
using the phone on that side.

**Watching is not doing nothing, and it is tiring to live with.** A plan of
scans can look like being left alone with it, and the wait around each scan is
its own kind of hard.
[The worry that comes with being watched](/treatments/watch-and-wait#worry)
covers what has been studied about that, including for this growth.

## What to ask your team

- Which side is it on, and which nerve is it sitting on?
- What is my hearing like now on that side, compared with the other?
- Are we watching it, treating it, or deciding? Why that one?
- What would make you want to treat it rather than keep watching?
- What is each choice meant to achieve: stopping growth, or removing it?
- What is each choice likely to do to my hearing?
- What is the risk to the nerve that moves my face, and how does size change it?
- If the plan is watching, when is my next scan and my next hearing test?
- What should I tell you about between appointments?
- Could a hearing aid or another device help me now?
- Is balance therapy worth trying?
- Is there any reason to think this is the inherited kind?
