## The short version

!%adult-type%!%pediatric-type%!%integrated diagnosis%!%diffuse glioma%
**Glioma is a family name, not a diagnosis.** If your paperwork says only
"glioma", it has told you the neighborhood and not the address. Your pathology
report will have a more exact name on it. That name usually comes with a grade
and some gene results. It is what everything else depends on. This page helps
you work out which one is yours, then sends you to the right place. If your
report uses words you cannot find anywhere, the naming rules changed in 2021.
That is covered here too.

## What is a glioma?

Your brain has nerve cells, and it has helper cells packed around them. The
helper cells are called glial cells. A glioma is a tumor that starts in those
helper cells. Gliomas are the most common kind of tumor that begins in the brain
itself.

"Glioma" covers a lot of ground. Some grow very slowly. Some grow fast. They are
treated differently and they behave differently, which is why the family name on
its own cannot tell you much.

### The family, roughly

Think of it as a family tree with a few main branches.

- **The adult-type diffuse gliomas.** This group has just three names in it:
  [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma),
  [oligodendroglioma](/tumors/oligodendroglioma), and
  [glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma). Gene results are what tell them apart,
  not how they look alone.
- **Gliomas of the kind usually seen in children.** These are a separate group
  with their own biology. Some grow slowly, some do not.
- **Gliomas with a clear edge.** Many gliomas spread out into the brain around
  them. A smaller group sits in a more defined lump, and the word for those on a
  report is **circumscribed**. That difference matters a lot for surgery.
- **[Ependymoma](/tumors/ependymoma)**, which starts in the cells lining the
  fluid spaces of the brain and spine.

**One thing here catches almost everybody out.** You may see the words
"adult-type" or "pediatric-type" on a report. Those describe the tumor's
biology, not the age of the person who has it. Adults are sometimes told they
have a pediatric-type glioma. Children are sometimes told they have an
adult-type one. It is not a mistake on your report. Nobody has filed you in the
wrong place.

### Words that sound like diagnoses but are not

Some phrases describe a group rather than name a tumor:

- **[Low-grade glioma](/tumors/low-grade-glioma)** and
  **[high-grade glioma](/tumors/high-grade-glioma)** describe how fast a group of
  tumors tends to grow. Each covers several real diagnoses.
- **Diffuse glioma** means the tumor spreads out into the brain around it rather
  than sitting in one lump.

You can be told any of these and still not have been told your diagnosis. It is
completely fair to ask, "so what is the full name of mine?"

## Is it cancer? What does its grade mean?

**Some gliomas are cancer and some are not, and which one you have is the thing
that decides it.** That is not a dodge, and this section gives you enough to
work out where yours sits.

The word doctors use for the ones that are cancer is **malignant**. The word for
the ones that are not is **benign**. But **benign in the brain does not mean
harmless.** A benign tumor still takes up room inside a closed skull, and it can
still press on a part of the brain that matters. It is a statement about how the
cells behave, not a promise that it will not cause trouble.

**Grade 1 gliomas** sit in a defined lump with a border. Most of them can be
cured by surgery, if the surgeon can safely reach all of it. For those, "they
got it all" can genuinely mean finished.

**Grade 2 and above are different.** These grow out into the brain around them,
so there is no edge to cut around. They are malignant, and with today's
treatments they are not curable. That is a hard sentence and it is here on
purpose. Being told "low grade" and hearing "not serious" is the commonest
misunderstanding about these tumors. It falls apart later, at the worst possible
moment.

**Not curable is not the same as untreatable, and it is not a timeline.**
Treatment can work for a long time. Some of these tumors are watched rather than
treated for years. What it means is that the plan assumes the tumor will need
attention again, which is why the scans do not stop.

### What the grade actually describes

A grade describes how normal or abnormal the tumor cells look under a
microscope. In a lower grade tumor such as grade 1, the cells look close to
normal. In a higher grade tumor such as grade 4, they look more abnormal.

Two things about grade catch people out.

**Grade is not the same across different tumors.** Since 2021, grading is done
within each tumor type. A grade 4 of one tumor type does not mean the same thing
as a grade 4 of another. So comparing your grade with somebody else's, when you
have different diagnoses, does not tell you what it seems to.

**Grade is no longer only about how the cells look.** Gene results can now set
the grade. That means a tumor can be given a higher grade than its appearance
alone would suggest. [The words on your gene
results](/tests/molecular-markers) explains the individual tests.

### Why does my report have a grade but no stage?

Most cancers are given a stage, which describes how far they have spread through
the body. Brain tumors are given a grade instead.

The reason is biological. A glioma can spread within the brain, but it very
rarely leaves the central nervous system for the rest of the body. Cases have
been reported, but they are unusual enough to be written up when they happen.
So the thing staging is designed to measure mostly does not apply here.

## Where does it grow, and why does it cause these symptoms?

[MECHANISM]

### The one that is specific to gliomas

Most gliomas do something the list above does not cover: **they grow through
brain tissue instead of pushing it aside.** They do not sit in a neat ball with
an edge. Tumor cells spread out into the normal brain around them, and they
reach further than the abnormal area you can see on a scan. So a scan understates
how far the tumor really goes.

**This is the reason "they got it all" and "cured" are not the same sentence for
most gliomas.** It is not that your surgeon missed something. It is that the edge
that shows on the scan is not the true edge.

Not every glioma behaves this way. A smaller group of them sit in a more defined
lump with a clearer border, and for those, surgery can mean something closer to
finished. Which kind you have is on your pathology report, and it is one of the
most useful things to ask about.

**Seizures are one of the most common ways a glioma first shows up**, along with
headaches, feeling sick, and problems with movement, speech or sensation. And
for tumors at the front of the brain, changes in behavior and personality can be
present for months or even years before anybody thinks of a scan. That is why
some people look back and recognize something they could not name at the time.

**Some people have no symptoms at all.** A small share of lower-grade gliomas
are found by accident, on a scan done for something else entirely. If that was
you, the strangeness of being told you have a brain tumor while feeling
perfectly well is real. Say so to your team rather than assuming you have no
right to be shaken by it.

## What symptoms does it cause?

The most common ones are headaches, seizures, feeling sick or being sick, and
problems with movement, speech, sensation or thinking. Which of them you get
depends mostly on where the tumor is, which the section above walks through.

## How do doctors find out it is this?

Usually a scan comes first, then a tissue sample.

- **[An MRI scan](/tests/mri)** shows where the tumor is, how big it is, and
  something about how it is behaving.
- **A tissue sample.** A scan can suggest a glioma. It cannot give you the exact
  name. That needs a piece of the tumor, taken either at
  [an operation](/treatments/craniotomy) or by a needle biopsy.
- **Gene tests on that tissue.** These are what separate the three main adult
  diagnoses from each other.

[How tissue is taken](/tests/biopsy) is about that step: the needle, the
operation, and what happens if the sample does not give an answer.

The full answer arrives in layers, not all at once, and the name can change as
later results come back. [Waiting for
results](/tests/waiting-for-results) is about that wait, which is one of the
hardest parts of this whole process.

## What do the words on my report mean?

Your report should carry the tumor type, a grade, and the gene results, brought
together into one final answer. Doctors call that combined answer the
**integrated diagnosis**, and it is what your team treats.

[Your pathology report, part by part](/tests/pathology-report) walks through the
document itself, and [the words on your gene
results](/tests/molecular-markers) explains each test in turn.

### If your paperwork uses words you cannot find

[CROSSWALK]

### The glioma names that changed

These are the ones most likely to be on an older report about a glioma.

- **Anaplastic** anything, such as anaplastic astrocytoma. Doctors no longer use
  the word anaplastic when they name these tumors. The grade does that job now.
  You will still hear people say it.
- **Diffuse astrocytoma**, on its own. This is no longer a diagnosis by itself.
  It is now written with the gene result and a grade attached. See
  [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma).
- **Oligoastrocytoma**, sometimes called a mixed glioma. This one no longer
  exists at all. It used to be the name for a tumor that looked like a mix of
  two types. Gene tests can now sort those tumors into one group or the other,
  so the mixed name was dropped.
- **Secondary glioblastoma**, or glioblastoma with an IDH change. Now called
  astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, grade 4, so the page you want is
  [astrocytoma](/tumors/astrocytoma), not glioblastoma. These tumors were found
  to behave differently from the ones still called glioblastoma, so they were
  moved out from under that name.
- **Glioblastoma multiforme**, or GBM. Now just
  [glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma), IDH-wildtype. Many people still say GBM
  in conversation.
- **DIPG**, or diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma. That name described where the
  tumor sat. Most of these are now called
  [diffuse midline glioma](/tumors/diffuse-midline-glioma), H3 K27-altered.

**Two things follow from all this, and both surprise people.**

A tumor with an IDH change is never called glioblastoma now. If your 2016 report
said secondary glioblastoma and your new one says astrocytoma, you have not been
given a different disease. It was renamed.

It also runs the other way. A tumor can now be called grade 4 because of its
gene results, even when the cells did not look that way under the microscope. So
you can be told grade 4 when nobody saw the changes that used to be required for
it. That is the new rules working, not a mix-up.

## How is it usually treated?

Treatment is chosen from the exact type, the grade and the gene results
together. This is the practical reason all that naming matters: the
classification is what guides the treatment. Two people who both say they have a
glioma can be offered completely different plans, and neither is wrong.

The usual building blocks:

- **[Surgery](/treatments/craniotomy)**, to take out as much as can safely be
  taken and to get tissue for testing.
- **[Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy)**, often after surgery.
- **[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy)**, as pills or into a vein,
  depending on the drug.

Which of these apply, in what order, and whether any apply at all depends
entirely on which glioma you have. Some people are watched rather than treated
at first, and that is a plan rather than an absence of one.

Alongside all this there is supportive treatment. That means medicines to
prevent seizures, medicines to bring swelling down, and treatment for a fluid
blockage if you have one.

## What is treatment actually like, and what is normal afterwards?

The part people are least prepared for is that finishing treatment is not the
same as feeling finished. Tiredness after radiation often arrives late and
lingers. Thinking and memory can feel slower for a while. Both of these are
common and neither means treatment failed.

If you are about to start something and want to know what the day itself
involves, these are the pages that walk through it:
[surgery](/treatments/craniotomy),
[radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) and
[chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy). Each one covers what is normal
afterwards and when to call somebody.

## Everyday life: work, driving, seizures and tiredness

**Seizures and driving.** If you have had a seizure, or are at risk of one,
[living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) covers the practical side: water
and bathrooms, cooking, sleep, alcohol, and how to keep doing the things you
want to keep doing. The rules about driving vary a lot by where you live. In
some places it is your doctor who has to report it, not you. Start with
[the driving section](/seizures/living-with#driving), then confirm it with your
own team.

**Work and money.** Many people keep working, sometimes with adjustments. Some
need time away and then return. The financial side is one of the most stressful
parts and it is worth asking early what help exists.

**Tiredness and thinking.** Tiredness is one of the most common and most
underestimated effects, and it is not the same as ordinary tiredness. Alongside
it, many people notice their memory or concentration is not what it was. Both
are worth saying out loud to your team rather than pushing through, because
there are things that help and nobody offers them if they do not know.

## Follow-up scans, and what to do while you wait

Most people with a glioma stay on long-term scan follow-up, often for years. The
scans are how a change gets spotted early, while there are still choices about
what to do.

The waiting is its own problem, and it has a name: **scanxiety**. Patients coined
it, and it is worth knowing because it is the word that will find you other
people who get it.

For most people the worst part is not the scan. It is the gap between the scan
and the result. Knowing that in advance helps, because it tells you which days
to plan something for. Tell your team if that wait is hard. Sometimes the timing
can be changed, and sometimes saying it out loud is what helps.

If something changes between appointments, you do not have to wait for the next
one. Bringing a scan forward is an ordinary thing to ask for.

[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes
through the rest of it: what your team compares your scan with, what the words
on the report mean, and why a scan that looks worse is not always worse.

## If it comes back, or changes

For many gliomas, a change over time is expected rather than surprising, and
your team will have been watching for it from the start. That is what the scans
are for.

If something does change, there are usually still options:

- More surgery.
- Radiation, if it has not been used yet or can be used again.
- A different drug.
- A clinical trial.

Which of these are open to you depends on your diagnosis, what you have already
had, and how you are doing in yourself.

Being told there has been a change is a hard day. It is not the same as being
told there is nothing left to try.

## What might happen over time

:::outlook
Doctors sometimes describe outlook using numbers. It is worth understanding what
those numbers are before deciding whether you want them.

They come from large groups of people who were treated years ago. That matters
here more than in most illnesses, because the way these tumors are named and
sorted changed in 2021. Some older figures describe groups put together under
the old rules, which means they may not describe the group you are actually in.

The word you will run into is **median**. A median is the middle of a group. If
you lined everybody up by how long they lived, the median is the person standing
in the middle. It is not a prediction about any one person, and it is not a
number of months anybody was handed.

The other thing worth knowing is the shape of the group. These figures are not
spread evenly around the middle. There is a long tail of people who do far
better than the middle of the group, and they are real people, not exceptions to
be discounted. Some people live many years with a glioma, working, driving and
raising children. A median cannot show you that tail, which is one of the
reasons it is a poor thing to hear on its own.

None of this tells you what will happen to you. What your own team can say, with
your scans and your gene results in front of them, is worth much more than
anything written on a page for everybody.
:::

## For the person caring for someone with this

[CAREGIVER]

**Which glioma it is changes what this job looks like.** For a grade 1 that was
fully removed, the work may be a few weeks of recovery and then scans. For a
diffuse glioma it is a longer road, with stretches where nothing much happens
and stretches where a lot does. It is fair to ask the team which of those you
are looking at, and you are allowed to ask it without the person you care for in
the room.

**If seizures are part of this**, they are the thing most caregivers most want
to be ready for. [What to do during a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) is the
step-by-step, worth reading before you need it, and
[living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) covers the rest of the time.

## What to ask your team

- What is the full name of my tumor, including the grade and the gene results?
- Is that name on my pathology report, and can I have a copy?
- My older paperwork uses a different word. Which one applies now?
- Which parts of my treatment are decided by the gene results?
- Is any of what I am feeling caused by swelling rather than the tumor itself?
- What would make you want to see me before my next appointment?
- Who do I call out of hours, and what number is it?
- Is there a clinical trial I should know about?

## Where to get support

- [Getting help now](/get-help-now) if you need somebody today.
- [Living with seizures](/seizures/living-with) for the practical side of
  seizures.
- Your team's specialist nurse, who is usually the fastest way to get a question
  answered without waiting for a clinic appointment.
- Other people with the same diagnosis. Many people say this helped more than
