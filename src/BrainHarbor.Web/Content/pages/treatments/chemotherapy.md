## The short version

!%neutropenia%!%nadir%!%procarbazine%!%vincristine%!%lomustine%!%carmustine wafer%
Chemotherapy uses drugs to stop tumor cells growing. The ones used most for
brain tumors are on this page, and most of them are capsules you take at home
rather than something given through an IV. The drugs lower your blood counts, so you will have blood tests
all the way through. **The one thing to know before you start: a fever during
chemotherapy is a phone call straight away, not something to sleep on.**

## What is chemotherapy for a brain tumor?

It is treatment with drugs that kill tumor cells or stop them dividing.

The drugs travel in your blood, so they reach the whole body. That is why they
cause effects in places the tumor is not.

**Not every chemotherapy drug reaches a brain tumor.** The brain has a barrier
around it, and some drugs do not get past it in useful amounts. That is part of
why the drugs offered for a brain tumor are a particular short list rather than
the whole shelf. If you have read about a drug for a different cancer and
wondered why nobody has offered it to you, ask. There is usually a reason, and
it is a fair question.

Chemotherapy is given in **cycles**: a period of treatment, then a rest period
to let your body recover. How long each one lasts depends on the drug and on
your plan.

Most of these drugs are capsules you take at home. One of them is given into a
vein at the hospital. One is not a drug you take at all, but wafers the surgeon
places during an operation.

## Why am I having it?

Chemotherapy is used for brain tumors in three broad situations, and it is
worth knowing which is yours.

- **Alongside and after radiation**, for some tumors, as part of the first
  course of treatment. [Radiation therapy: the mask, the daily visits, and what
  comes after](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers the radiation half.
- **After surgery**, to deal with what an operation cannot reach.
  [Brain surgery: what happens, and what comes after](/treatments/craniotomy)
  goes through the operation itself.
- **If the tumor comes back**, where a different drug may be used from the one
  you had the first time.

**Which drug you are offered depends on what the tumor turned out to be**,
including the gene results. [The words on your gene
results](/tests/molecular-markers) explains those one at a time. This is one of
the places where the lab report genuinely changes the plan, rather than just
describing it.

## The drugs, one at a time

### Temozolomide {#temozolomide}

**What it is for.** The chemotherapy used most often for gliomas. For some of
them it is given with radiation, and then after it.

**How you take it.** Capsules, by mouth, at home. There is no IV and no
hospital stay for it.

**What it does to you.** The common effects are tiredness, feeling or being
sick, constipation, loss of appetite, and low blood counts. The tiredness has
been reported during treatment and for months afterwards. Periods can stop for
a while.

**The blood counts are the part that shapes your treatment.** A low platelet
count is the effect that most often limits the dose. Platelets help blood clot,
so a low count raises the risk of bleeding and bruising. If a blood test shows
it, your team may delay a cycle or lower the dose. Your liver is checked
regularly too.

Rarely it causes a more serious blood problem, or liver injury. Your team is
watching for both, which is what the blood tests are for.

### PCV {#pcv}

**What it is.** Not one drug but three, given together: **p**rocarbazine,
lomustine (also written **C**CNU) and **v**incristine. The initials are where
the name comes from.

**What it is for.** Some gliomas, and it is particularly associated with
oligodendroglioma.

**How you take it.** Procarbazine and lomustine are capsules you swallow.
Vincristine is different: it goes into a vein, either through a thin tube in
your arm for the day, or through a line that stays in place for the whole
course.

**What it does to you.** The main problems are low blood counts, being sick,
constipation, effects on the liver, and nerve damage in the hands and feet.
That last one comes mainly from the vincristine, and it shows up as numbness,
tingling or weakness. Tell your team early if you notice it, because it is one
of the things that changes what they do next.

**There is a food and drink rule with procarbazine, and it is the one thing on
this page you can get wrong at home tonight.** Alcohol can cause a reaction
with it: flushing in the face and neck, feeling hot, sweating, feeling sick,
itching and a headache. Some foods can do the same, mostly aged or fermented
ones. **Ask for the list before your first capsule**, and ask whether it covers
low-alcohol and alcohol-free drinks too, because it often does.

**Be honest about how you are coping with it.** One study at a national
neuro-oncology center in Ireland looked at a full year of PCV in people with
low-grade glioma. It found it was poorly tolerated. Blood-count
problems often caused delays and breaks. Struggling with PCV is not a personal
failure. Do not save it up for the next appointment. Changing it is normal and
it is part of the plan.

### Lomustine on its own {#lomustine}

**What it is for.** Sometimes used by itself, often when a tumor has come back.

**How you take it.** Capsules by mouth. **You take it only occasionally, not
every day, because it lowers your blood counts for weeks after each dose.**
That is the whole reason for the gap, and it is not a sign that your treatment
is being rationed or delayed.

**What makes it different from temozolomide.** The dip in blood counts arrives
**late**: around four to six weeks after a dose, rather than the two to three
weeks that is usual with temozolomide. It also tends to build up over repeated
doses. So the blood test that matters most is often the one a month or more
after you took the capsules, which is exactly when you feel finished with that
dose and least expect a problem.

If the counts do not recover, your team may space the doses further apart,
lower them, or change to something else. That is a normal decision, not a
setback.

### Carmustine wafers {#carmustine-wafers}

**What they are.** Small wafers that hold chemotherapy. The surgeon puts them
into the space where the tumor was, during the operation. They release the drug
into the tissue right around that space, and then dissolve.

**Who gets them.** Some people having surgery for a high-grade glioma. They are
not offered everywhere, and they are not offered to everyone having an
operation.

**What is honestly known about them, in both directions.** Specialists do not
agree about how much they help. A review that pooled the studies came out in
favor of them for a newly diagnosed high-grade glioma, as long as the right
people are chosen for them. European guidelines are more careful. They point
out that in the main trial the difference did not hold up once one group of
tumors was taken out. Both things are true at the same time. That is why you
may hear different views from different doctors.

They can also cause swelling or an infection where they sit, and that is part
of why not every surgeon offers them.

One more practical thing, and it is easy to miss. Having wafers can rule you
out of some clinical trials afterwards, because the drug they release muddies
the results. If wafers come up, ask about that before the operation, not
after. [Clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials#already-started) covers
the rest of that question, and what to ask before you start anything new.

## How long does it go on?

This varies more than almost anything else on this page, so ask for your own
plan in writing. The general shape:

- **Chemotherapy is given in cycles**, with a rest period built into each one
  so your body can recover.
- **Some of it runs alongside radiation** and finishes when the radiation
  course does.
- **Some of it continues afterwards**, in cycles, for months.
- **Lomustine is spaced out on purpose**, weeks apart, because of the late dip
  in blood counts.
- **Delays are part of it.** Cycles get pushed back when blood counts are low.
  That is expected and planned for.

**Ask how many cycles are planned, and what would change that.** It is a
question with a real answer. Get it written down. On a bad week it helps to be
able to look at it.

## Which tumors usually get which drug

This is a rough guide and not a prediction about you. Your team decides from
your own pathology and gene results.

- **Glioblastoma, newly diagnosed.** Temozolomide, with radiation and after it.
- **Glioblastoma that has come back.** Often lomustine. Carmustine wafers are
  sometimes considered if there is another operation.
- **Oligodendroglioma.** PCV is the combination most linked with it.
- **Other gliomas, grade 2 and 3.** Temozolomide or PCV, usually after
  radiation.
- **A tumor that spread to the brain from somewhere else.** The drugs are picked
  for the first cancer, not for the brain. Your doctor for that cancer leads.

If your tumor is not in this list, that does not mean chemotherapy is not for
you. It means the list is short and yours needs a conversation.

## Your blood counts, and the fever rule {#fever-rule}

This is the most important section on this page. If you read nothing else, read
this.

**Why the blood tests happen.** Chemotherapy affects the bone marrow, where
blood cells are made. Three kinds of cell get counted, and each one matters for
a different reason:

- **White cells** fight infection. Low white cells mean infections take hold
  more easily and get worse faster. You may see this called neutropenia on your
  results, or hear your team say your neutrophils are low.
- **Platelets** help blood clot. Low platelets mean bruising and bleeding.
- **Red cells** carry oxygen. Low red cells mean tiredness and breathlessness.

**The lowest point has a name.** Counts fall after chemotherapy and then come
back up. The lowest point is called the nadir, and it is usually about seven to
ten days after treatment. That is when the risk of a serious infection is
highest, and it is why the timing of a temperature matters.

**Here is the rule.** During chemotherapy, **a temperature over 100.4 °F
(38 °C) means calling your team straight away, at any hour.** Not in the
morning. Not after seeing how you feel. An infection with low white cells can
go from mild to dangerous quickly, and it is treatable when it is caught early.

Call straight away for any of these too:

- Chills, or shaking, or drenching sweats.
- New or worsening confusion.
- Dizziness, feeling faint, or falling.
- Chest pain, or trouble breathing even while resting.

**Before your first cycle, get three things.** Ask for them by name:

1. **The number to call out of hours**, written down, kept where you can see
   it. Not the clinic's daytime number.
2. **Their temperature rule**, in their words. Some teams use a slightly
   different number. Some also ask you to call if your temperature is unusually
   **low**, which can be a sign of infection too. Use theirs.
3. **A thermometer**, and the habit of using it rather than guessing.

Some teams give you a card to carry that tells any doctor you are on
chemotherapy. If yours does, keep it with you. It changes how quickly you are
seen.

**If you are not sure whether to call, call.** Nobody on that team minds a
phone call about a temperature that turns out to be nothing.

## What does a cycle feel like?

Cycles differ by drug, and yours will have its own shape. But there is a common
pattern worth knowing, because it repeats and because it catches people out the
first time.

The day you take it is usually unremarkable. Feeling sick, if it happens, tends
to come later that day or the next, which is why the anti-sickness medicine
matters more than it seems to on the morning of day one. Tiredness builds over
the days that follow rather than arriving at once.

Then, and this is the part that surprises people, **you feel worst when you
feel finished**. Blood counts reach their lowest point about a week to ten days
after the dose, when the treatment feels like it is behind you. That is the
stretch to be careful in, and it is why the fever rule matters most in a week
when nothing is obviously happening.

Then counts recover, you start to feel more like yourself, and the next cycle
begins. Many people find the second and third cycles more predictable than the
first, simply because they know the shape of it.

## Side effects, and what is done about them

Most of these are managed rather than endured, and several of them have a
medicine of their own. Bring them up early rather than saving them for the next
appointment.

**Tiredness** is common with all of these drugs, and it is not fixed by one
good night. It can carry on for months after treatment ends.

**Constipation** is one of the common problems with PCV. There is medicine for
it, and it is easier to stay ahead of than to fix.

**Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet** comes mainly from vincristine.
Report it as soon as you notice, because it is one of the effects your team
acts on rather than waits out.

**Hair loss.** How much depends on the drug. With PCV it can be all of it,
including eyebrows and eyelashes. It usually grows back after treatment
finishes.

**Effects on the liver** are watched for with blood tests rather than felt.

**Periods can stop** for a while during treatment.

**Fertility.** If you might want children in the future, ask about it
**before** treatment starts rather than partway through. Your team can tell you
whether the drug you are having affects that, and who to talk to about it.

## Feeling sick, and the medicine for it

Feeling or being sick is common. Doctors usually prescribe anti-sickness
medicine to go with chemotherapy.

The useful thing to ask is **when** to take it. Anti-sickness medicine is
generally easier to stay ahead of than to catch up with, so ask your team
whether yours is meant to be taken on a schedule, or before a dose, or only
when you feel sick. Ask what to do if it is not working, because there is more
than one kind and swapping is normal.

If you cannot keep fluids down, that is a call to your team the same day rather
than something to wait out.

## Why am I on an antibiotic?

Because of an infection you do not have and they are trying to keep it that
way. This surprises almost everyone, and nobody explains it.

Temozolomide given at the same time as radiation lowers one kind of white cell
for a long stretch. That leaves you open to an uncommon lung infection. A
healthy immune system holds it off without you ever knowing. So you are given an
antibiotic to prevent it, through that stretch and usually until those cells
come back.

It is not a sign that you have caught something. It is not a sign that anything
has gone wrong. It is a precaution taken in advance.

**Not every team gives it, and that is a real difference of opinion rather than
a mistake.** Guidelines have recommended it for years. A recent review of the
pooled studies found the infection is rarer than the old advice assumed, and
argued for choosing who gets it rather than giving it to everybody. So if your
team does not offer it, or stops it, that is a considered position and worth
asking about rather than worrying about.

Ask how long you will be on it and what would end it, because "until your
counts recover" means a blood test decides, not the calendar. And tell your
team about **a new rash** while you are taking it, straight away.

## What you need first

Before chemotherapy starts, expect:

- **Blood tests**, including a full blood count and checks on your liver and
  kidneys. These are the baseline everything later gets compared against.
- **Your pathology and gene results.** These decide whether chemotherapy is
  offered at all and which drug it is. [Waiting for your pathology
  results](/tests/waiting-for-results) covers the wait if you are still in it.
- **A scan** to compare later ones against. [Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) goes
  through that appointment.
- **A conversation about pregnancy and contraception** where that applies, and
  about fertility if you may want children later.

Worth sorting out before day one:

- The out-of-hours number, written down.
- A thermometer.
- Somebody who knows you are starting, and knows the fever rule too.

## For the person caring for someone on chemotherapy

[CAREGIVER]

Chemotherapy is mostly taken at home, which means most of what goes wrong goes
wrong at home, in front of you. That is different from surgery and different
from radiation.

**Learn the fever rule as well as they do.** A temperature over 100.4 °F during
chemotherapy is a call straight away, at any hour. You may be the one who
notices they are hot, or shivering, or not making sense. Do not wait for
morning, and do not wait to be sure. Have the out-of-hours number somewhere you
can find it in the dark.

**Get a thermometer, and use it.** A hand on a forehead is a guess. This is
the one bit of kit that changes what happens.

**Know when the risky window is.** Counts are usually at their lowest about a
week to ten days after a dose. That is when an infection does the most harm
fastest. If the drug is lomustine, the dip comes much later, a month or more
after the dose. By then everyone has relaxed.

**The pills are a job, and it is your job as much as theirs.** These are taken
at home, on a schedule, sometimes several drugs at once, and the schedule
changes. Write it down where you can both see it. Check it against the pharmacy
label each time it changes rather than trusting either of your memories.

**Watch for the things they will not mention.** Constipation, numbness or
tingling in the hands and feet, and not being able to keep food or fluids down
all get put up with rather than reported. All three are things the team acts
on. If you notice, say it at the appointment.

**Delays are normal, and they land harder emotionally than they should.** A
cycle pushed back for low counts feels like losing ground. It is not. It is the
plan working the way it is designed to.

**When to call an ambulance.** These cannot wait:

- Chest pain, or coughing up blood.
- They are not breathing properly, or are breathless at rest.
- You cannot wake them.

**When to call the team straight away, at any hour.** Not the next morning:

- A temperature over 100.4 °F, or chills, shaking or drenching sweats.
- An unusually low temperature.
- Confusion, or thinking that has changed.
- Dizziness, feeling faint, or falling.
- A new rash, especially if they are on the preventive antibiotic.

**When to call the team the same day.** Ask for their own version of this list
before the first cycle. Generally:

- Being unable to keep fluids down.
- Unusual bruising, or bleeding that does not stop, or blood in vomit or stool.
- Mouth sores that stop them eating or drinking.
- Any new symptom that worries you.

If you are not sure which list something belongs on, call. Treat it as the
first one.

## Why has my treatment been delayed or stopped?

Being told a cycle is delayed is unsettling, especially when everything so far
has been about starting quickly. The reason is usually mundane.

**Low blood counts are the commonest.** If the counts have not recovered
enough, giving the next dose on time would do more harm than waiting. Your team
may push the cycle back, lower the dose, or both. That is the monitoring
working, not failing.

Treatment also gets changed when side effects are too much. Nerve damage in
the hands and feet is a common reason to stop a drug or swap it. Stopping early
is how your team keeps it from getting worse, and it often improves once
treatment finishes.

**A delay is not the tumor winning. A change of drug is not a last resort.**
Ask what they are waiting for, what number they want to see, and when they will
check again. All three have answers. Having them makes the wait easier to sit
with.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result?

Two different things get reported back to you, on two different clocks.

**Blood counts** come back quickly, often the same day. They decide whether the
next cycle goes ahead. Ask whether somebody will call you if a result is a
problem, or whether no news means proceed as planned. Teams differ, and
assuming the wrong one is how people miss a change.

**Scans** are the slower answer, and they are how anyone judges whether the
treatment is doing what it is meant to. Ask when your next scan is and who will
go through it with you.

Ask one more thing: whether results appear in your online records before
anybody calls you. For many people they do, and reading a scan report alone on
a phone is a hard way to find out. If you would rather hear it from a person,
say so, and ask them to call you when it lands.

## What to ask your team

- Which drug am I having, and what is it meant to do for my tumor?
- Is it capsules at home, or something given at the hospital?
- How often will you check my blood, and who tells me the result?
- **What temperature means I call you, and what is the number out of hours?**
- What anti-sickness medicine will I have, and when should I take it?
- Am I going to be on an antibiotic, and for how long?
- How many cycles are we planning, and what would change that?
- What should I report straight away rather than saving for the next visit?
- What happens if my counts are too low on the day?
- Could this affect my fertility, and who do I talk to about it before we
  start?
- When is my next scan, and who will go through it with me?
