## The short version {#short-version}

This is a treatment you wear on your head, for glioblastoma. Sticky pads on your
shaved scalp are wired to a small machine in a bag, which sends weak electric
fields through them.
It is called tumor treating fields. It is only expected to work while it is
switched on, which is why you wear it at least eighteen hours a day.
Doctors do not all agree about how much it adds. So the choice is about two
things at once: what it might do, and what living with it asks of you.

## What is it? {#what-it-is}

!%tumor treating fields%It is a treatment that uses weak electric fields to get
in the way of tumor cells as they divide.

Four sets of sticky pads go on your scalp once your head has been shaved. Wires
run from the pads to a small machine that runs on batteries.

The fields are thought to affect tumor cells more than healthy brain cells. It
is not the kind of radiation used in radiation therapy. The American Cancer
Society puts it in the same family as radio waves.

The device you will hear named is **Optune Gio**, often just called **Optune**.

## Why would I be offered it? {#why}

It is approved for some people with glioblastoma, and it is used at two
different points. What it has been shown to do is different at each one.

- **After surgery and radiation, alongside chemotherapy.** Here it is added to
  temozolomide, not used instead of it. The American Cancer Society says it may
  help people live longer than temozolomide on its own.
- **If glioblastoma comes back.** Here it can be used instead of chemotherapy.
  The American Cancer Society says that at this point it has not been shown to
  help people live longer than chemotherapy, but its side effects tend to be
  much milder.

**Those are two different claims, so check which one applies to you.**
[Glioblastoma](/tumors/glioblastoma) covers who the device suits and when it
usually starts.

## Do doctors agree about it? {#do-doctors-agree}

No, and it is fair to know that before you decide.

The European guideline calls its role controversial and says it is not widely
available there. In the United States, the main cancer guidelines list it as a
standard option for newly diagnosed glioblastoma.

**Here is what the doubts are about.** Reviewers in England and Canada raised
concerns about the main trial. It was an open trial, so people knew whether they
had the device. The people in it were a chosen group, which makes it less clear
how well the results fit everyone else. The European guideline also questions
whether it is practical, and whether it is worth what it costs. It is very
expensive, and in the United Kingdom it is only available privately or in trials.

In one study in Chicago, all the doctors asked called it a good option, and some
said it may not suit every patient. Ask your own team where they stand on it for
you, and what makes them think so.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

1. **You talk it through with your team.** They check whether it suits you.
2. **You, and anyone helping you, are taught how to use it.** That means putting
   the pads on, taking them off, swapping batteries, and what to do when the
   machine beeps.
3. **Your head is shaved close.** Hair stops the pads from sticking, so all of it
   has to come off.
4. **The pads go on in planned places.** Where each one sits is worked out for
   your tumor.
5. **You switch it on and take it home.** From then on, you wear it at home and
   when you go out.
6. **Every few days the pads come off.** Your scalp is washed and checked, shaved
   again, and a new set goes on.

## How long does it take? {#how-long}

**At least eighteen hours a day, every day, for as long as you and your team keep
going with it.**

- **The hours are the treatment.** Unlike a pill, it is only expected to act while
  it is on. The trial that found a benefit asked people to wear it at least
  eighteen hours a day. How much it helps is thought to depend on the hours.
- **The pads are changed about twice a week.**
- **A change takes a while.** In one small study in Germany, a change took about
  half an hour for a typical person, and much longer for some.
- **Getting used to it takes time.** In that study it often took about two weeks,
  and for some people it took a few months. Life felt hardest in the first month,
  and for most it had settled by the end of the second.
- **How many months it goes on is decided with your doctor.** Ask what would make
  them suggest stopping.

## What does it feel like? {#what-it-feels-like}

Mostly like having something on your head, and a bag to carry.

**The pads can feel warm.** Some people feel warmth or tingling under them. The
machine checks how warm the pads get, and switches itself off with an alarm if
they get too warm. If you wear a hat, choose one that lets air through, so your
head does not get too hot.

**It beeps.** Alarms go off when something needs attention, at night as well as
in the day. In the German study, most people said the alarms bothered them a
fair amount or a lot.

**The cap and the bag have their own downsides.** In the Chicago study, people named the
heat on their head, the discomfort of the cap, and carrying the bag as real
downsides. They also said sleeping and showering were harder. Tell your team
which of these is hardest for you, because several can be made easier.

## Carrying it, sleeping, and washing {#everyday}

It comes with you when you go out, so the ordinary parts of the day need a plan.

**Carrying it.** The machine rides in a shoulder bag or a backpack. Each battery
lasts a few hours, so you carry spares and swap them. When you are sitting still,
you can plug it into the wall instead. One person in the German study got back
pain from carrying it on one shoulder, so ask whether a backpack would suit you.

**Sleeping.** You wear it while you sleep, too. If you are not sleeping well,
tell your team rather than putting up with it.

**Washing.** Pads that get wet or start to lift need changing sooner, so a shower
takes planning. On the day you change them, the shower helps: wetting the old
pads first lets them come off more gently.

**Work, hobbies, and closeness.** In the German study, most people said it got in
the way of work or hobbies, of washing and looking after themselves, and of sex
and closeness with a partner. Most said they would choose it again. These are
known problems, and you can raise any of them with your team.

## The shaved head, and other people {#the-shaved-head}

Your head stays shaved for as long as you wear it, and the pads and wires can be
seen.

**For some people that is the hardest part.** In the Chicago study, shaving
their head was the reason people who said no gave most often. Some did not want
to look sick. One person had not told people at work what was going on, and the
device would have told them.

**For others it matters less.** In the Chicago study, one person said shaving was
easy, because most of their hair had already gone. Another went back to work the
day after being fitted, and said it was awkward at first but started some good
conversations.

In the German study, about half said the shaved head bothered them little or not
at all, and most said the same about how the pads looked.

**Both reactions are ordinary.** One person in the Chicago study said others
might think it silly to mind about hair with cancer, and that it had still been
really hard. If the look of it is what is holding you back, say that to your team
plainly. It is a real reason, and it belongs in the conversation.

**You decide what to share.** It can help to have a short answer ready for
strangers, and to decide ahead of time who you want to tell more.

## Your scalp, and other side effects {#side-effects}

**Skin trouble under the pads is the most common side effect, and many people
get some.** It can be:

- redness and soreness,
- itching,
- a sweaty scalp that stops the pads sticking well,
- dry, flaky skin,
- and, less often, broken skin, open sores or an infection.

**What helps:**

- Shave with a clean electric razor, to avoid nicks.
- Wash your scalp with a mild shampoo that has no perfume in it.
- Move the pads a little at each change, in the way your team shows you.
- Keep the pads off scars, and off any screws under the skin.
- Ask your team before you put anything on your scalp. If they say yes,
  water-based lotions are better than greasy ones, which get in the way of the
  pads.

**Tell your team about skin changes as soon as you notice them**, not at your next
visit. A sore scalp is one of the main reasons people lose hours, and early care
can help you keep wearing it.

**Other side effects.** Headaches, trouble sleeping, mood changes and muscle
twitching. It can also slightly raise the chance of a seizure.
Read [What to do when someone has a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) now, and share
it with anyone who spends time with you.

**If you are also taking temozolomide, some of what you feel may be that drug.**
In the main trial, adding the device made little or no difference to serious
side effects. An upset stomach, tiredness and changes in your blood counts are
known effects of that drug. [Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy) covers
those. If something new starts, ask your team which one it is likely to be.

## What you need first {#what-you-need-first}

- **A talk about any metal or implants from surgery.** Your team plans where the
  pads go around them.
- **Someone to help change the pads.** If there is nobody, ask about a visiting
  nurse before you start.
- **Answers about cost.** Ask who pays for it and what you will owe, before you
  start rather than after.
- **A number for the machine.** Ask who you call when it keeps alarming, or a pad
  comes loose at night. That may not be the same number as your care team. For
  anything about your health, call your care team.

## For the person alongside them {#caregiver}

[CAREGIVER]

This treatment happens at home, every day, and a lot of it can land on you.

**You may be the one changing the pads.** It is a real job, and it does not stop.
If you cannot do every change, say so to the team.

**You can see the parts of the scalp the person you care for cannot.** Look at
the skin each time the pads come off. Redness, broken skin, pus, or a sore that
is not healing are worth telling the team about early.

**If the hours are slipping, talk about it together, and then with the team.**
Tell the team what is getting in the way, rather than letting it slide.

**Night alarms can wake the whole house.** Decide together who gets up, so it is
not always the same person.

## If the tumor grows while you are wearing it {#if-it-grows}

**Growth is not the only reason a scan can look worse.** Treatment can change how a scan
looks for a while.
[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers how
teams tell the difference.

**If it is growth, going on with the device is not automatic.** The European
guideline advises against staying on it once the tumor has clearly grown while
you are wearing it, because no benefit from doing that has been shown. Ask your
team what they suggest next, and whether a trial fits.
[Clinical trials: is this for me?](/treatments/clinical-trials) goes through that
question.

## Who decides whether to keep going? {#who-decides}

You do, with your team.

**Your team says what they suggest, and why.** You decide whether the hours, the
shaved head and the pads are a trade you want to make. People weigh that
differently, and you can raise it again later.

**Your scans are how your team keeps watch on the tumor.** Ask how and when you
will be told each result.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Is this right for me, and why do you think so?
- Would it be added to my other treatment, or used instead of it?
- What do you make of the European guideline calling it controversial?
- Who will teach us to change the pads, and who helps if I have nobody?
- Who do I call when the machine alarms at night?
- What should I do about a sore scalp, and what is safe to put on it?
- Do I have any screws or implants that change where the pads go?
- What will it cost me, and who pays for it?
- What happens if I cannot manage the hours?
- How will we decide whether to keep going?
