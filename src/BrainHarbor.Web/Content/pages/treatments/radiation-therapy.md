## The short version

!%radiation oncologist%!%simulation%!%radiation mask%!%linear accelerator%!%somnolence syndrome%!%whole-brain radiation%!%memantine%!%fractionation%!%stereotactic radiosurgery%
Radiation therapy uses beams of high energy to kill tumor cells. You lie on a
table, a machine moves around you, and you do not see or feel the beam. Most
people come in Monday through Friday for several weeks. Before any of that
starts there is a planning visit, where a mesh mask is molded to your face.
Most pages skip that visit, so this one goes through it in detail. Tiredness is
the most common side effect, and it does not stop on the last day.

## What is radiation therapy?

It is treatment that uses beams of high energy to kill tumor cells.

The beams come from a machine outside your body, so nothing is put inside you.
It works a lot like having an x-ray, except the amount of radiation is far
higher. The machine is called a linear accelerator. Staff often say "linac".

Radiation damages the DNA inside a cell, which is the set of instructions a
cell needs in order to divide.

The doctor in charge of this treatment is a radiation oncologist. You will
also meet radiation therapists, who are the people in the room with you every
day.

**You may hear several names for it. Most of them describe how the beam is
shaped, not a different treatment.**

- **3D conformal radiation.** Scans are used to map exactly where the tumor is.
  Several weak beams come in from different directions and meet at the tumor.
- **IMRT**, short for intensity modulated radiation therapy. The same idea, with
  the strength of each beam adjusted as well as its shape, to spare the most
  sensitive tissue nearby.
- **VMAT.** Similar to IMRT, with the machine moving around you in an arc. It
  can shorten each session.
- **Stereotactic radiosurgery.** For a small, well-defined target. It can be
  done in one visit or a few, instead of a course lasting weeks. Nothing is
  cut, in spite of the name.
- **Proton therapy.** A different kind of particle, given at a small number of
  centers.
- **Whole-brain radiation.** The whole brain rather than one spot. It has its
  own section further down.
- **Radiation to the brain and spinal cord together.** Used when a tumor has
  spread through the fluid around them.

Which one you are offered depends on what your tumor is, where it sits, and
what your center has. It is a fair question to ask, and the answer will be
about your tumor rather than about which machine is newest.

## Why am I having it?

There are three usual reasons, and it is worth knowing which one is yours.

- **After surgery,** to deal with tumor cells that surgery could not take out.
  Even after a very good operation, cells can be left behind that no scan sees.
  [Brain surgery: what happens, and what comes after](/treatments/craniotomy)
  goes through that in full.
- **As the main treatment,** when an operation is not a good idea. That can be
  because of where the tumor is, or because of your health more generally.
- **To ease symptoms,** by shrinking a tumor that is pressing on something.

Ask which of these your radiation is for. It changes what the plan is trying to
achieve, and it changes what you should expect to notice.

## What happens, step by step

### The first appointment

You meet the radiation oncologist. They go through your scans and your history,
what the treatment is meant to do, and what it may cost you in side effects.

Bring your questions written down, on paper or on your phone. There are a lot
of them at this appointment, and you will not remember them otherwise. Bring
somebody with you if you can.

### The planning visit, and the mask

This visit is called a simulation. **No radiation is given.** It is the
appointment where your treatment gets designed, and it is the one this page
spends the most time on.

Here is what happens, in order.

1. You change into a gown. A wig, hairpiece or head scarf comes off. Tell the
   therapist if you wear a hearing aid.
2. You lie on a hard, flat table. There is no cushion. If you think lying still
   will hurt, say so beforehand, because you can take pain medicine about an
   hour before.
3. A flat sheet of plastic mesh is warmed until it goes soft. It is laid over
   your face and gently stretched to the shape of your head. **It feels like a
   warm washcloth on your face.** You can see through it and breathe through it
   the whole time, and it does not hurt.
4. Over a few minutes the mesh cools, hardens and holds that shape. That is
   your mask. It is yours, and it stays at the center between visits.
5. You have a CT scan lying in the mask, in the exact position you will be
   treated in. You may have a dye through an IV line in your arm to make the
   pictures clearer.
6. Marks are drawn on the mask and photographs are taken, so that staff can put
   you back in the same position every day.

You will see red or green laser lines on the walls. Those are guides the
therapists line you up against. You will be asked to stay still once things
start, and you can ask for music.

**Why the mask exists at all.** Radiation is aimed at a target a few
millimeters across, in an area surrounded by parts of the brain nobody wants to
treat. The mask holds your head in exactly the same place every single day, so
that what was planned is what is delivered. It is not there to restrain you. It
is there so the beam goes where it is supposed to go.

How long the visit takes varies. One cancer center puts the mask-making part at
about half an hour; another says to plan on about two hours for the whole
appointment, and longer for some treatments. Ask what to expect where you are
being treated.

### The gap before treatment starts

Nothing happens to you for a few days, and this part worries people.

Behind the scenes a team is building your plan: the radiation oncologist, a
physicist, and a dosimetrist, who works out the angles and the amounts. At one
large center this takes one to ten working days.

Waiting is not a sign that anyone has stopped caring or that your case has been
forgotten. The plan is being made and checked. If the wait is bothering you,
call and ask where it is up to. That is a reasonable call to make.

### A treatment day

You check in and wait, the way you would at any appointment. Then:

- You lie on the treatment table in the same position as at your planning
  visit, and the mask goes on and is clipped to the frame.
- Pictures are taken first to check you are lined up correctly. The first day
  takes longer, because everything is being set up for the first time.
- Everyone leaves the room. You are on your own with the machine, but a camera
  and an intercom are on the whole time. Somebody can see you and hear you, and
  you can talk to them.
- The machine moves around you and the beam is delivered. **You do not see or
  feel it.**
- Once a week, someone from the team sits down with you to go through how you
  are doing.

## How long does it take?

Most of a treatment visit is set-up. The beam itself is over quickly.

- **Each visit:** usually somewhere between a quarter of an hour and an hour in
  the treatment room. The treatment itself takes only a few minutes.
- **The whole course:** most often Monday through Friday, over several weeks.
  One common plan for a brain tumor is thirty treatments, which works out at
  about six weeks of weekdays.
- **Other shapes of plan exist.** Some people have one single treatment. Some
  have a handful. A shorter course is sometimes the right plan for a particular
  person, and that is a decision your team makes with you.
- **The planning visit:** about two hours.
- **The gap after it:** one to ten working days at one large center.

Plan your day around the whole visit, not the few minutes of treatment. That
matters if you are working, sorting out childcare, or lining up a driver.

## Why every day, and why so many?

Because splitting it up is what protects the healthy brain around the tumor.

The total amount of radiation is divided into a lot of small daily amounts.
Each one is called a fraction, and dividing them up like this is called
fractionation. Healthy cells are good at repairing radiation damage, and tumor
cells are usually worse at it. The gap between one day and the next gives the
healthy tissue time to repair itself, while the damage builds up in the tumor.

A radiation oncologist put it plainly: give too much in one day and even normal
tissue is damaged, give too little and not enough tumor cells are damaged. The
daily schedule is the point between those two.

So the length of the course is not a measure of how bad your tumor is. Somebody
with thirty treatments is not thirty times worse off than somebody with one.
They are different plans for different situations, and both were chosen on
purpose.

## What does it feel like?

The treatment itself feels like nothing. That is the honest answer, and people
often do not believe it until the first day is over.

Radiation is painless. You do not see the beam, and you do not feel it going
in. There is no injection and nothing to swallow.

What you do notice is everything around it. One person treated for an
ependymoma said the last snap of the mask onto the frame sounded very loud.
Then came a "here we go" feeling.

**Some people get a smell or a taste that is not there.** One person smelled
chlorine, like a swimming pool. Others describe an electrical smell, or
flowers. It does not happen to everybody, and it is not a sign that something
has gone wrong. It is worth knowing about, because it is strange the first time
it happens.

You are alone in the room while the beam is on. It is a few minutes, you are
being watched and listened to the whole time, and you can speak up. Ask whether
you can have music or an audiobook playing. Many centers can, and it is worth
asking on the first day rather than putting up with the quiet for the whole
course.

## If the mask frightens you {#the-mask}

Say so. Say it early, and say it to the radiation therapists. At one cancer
center a therapist stays beside you through the whole mask-making appointment
for exactly this reason: to deal with concerns and fears as they come up. This
is a thing they expect to be asked about.

It is worth knowing what the mask is not. It is not solid, and it does not stop
you breathing. You can see through the mesh and breathe through it, and staff
will tell you so while they are making it. It goes on when you get on the table
and comes off when the beam is finished. Ask your therapists how long that is
where you are treated, because most of the time in the room is spent lining you
up rather than treating.

Things that are worth asking for, and that people do get:

- **Medicine for anxiety, taken before you come in.** Ask at the planning visit,
  not on the day. If it helps at that visit, you can take it before your
  treatments too, about an hour beforehand.
- **A run-through without any radiation.** Some centers do this as a routine
  step anyway. Ask whether yours does, so the first time in the mask is not
  also the first day of treatment.
- **Music or an audiobook** during the session.
- **Somebody talking to you over the intercom.** The therapists can hear you and
  answer you.

There is nothing to be ashamed of in finding this hard. So ask, and keep
asking. Tell the therapist how it is going, every day if you need to. They are
in the room with you more than anyone else on your team, and they are the ones
who can change how this goes for you.

## Side effects during treatment, and in the weeks after

Most of what turns up during radiation eases off in the weeks after it
finishes, often over about six to twelve weeks. One thing on this list works
the other way round and starts once the rest have settled. That is somnolence
syndrome, and it has its own section at the end of this one.

**Tiredness is the big one.** This is not ordinary tiredness and a good night
does not fix it. It is the most common problem people report during radiation
to the brain. It usually starts after a couple of weeks of treatment, builds as
the course goes on, and carries on for weeks to months after the last day. It
does get better over time.

Rest helps, and so does moving, and you need both. Plan short naps in the day
when you need them, and aim for a long night. Guidelines also suggest gentle
exercise: walking, and some light strength work. Pick the things that matter
most and do them when your energy is best. Ask your team what is sensible for
you, and start smaller than you think you should.

**Hair loss, where the beam goes in and out.** If your radiation is aimed at
one part of the brain, that is where the hair goes, not all over. If you are
having whole-brain radiation, expect to lose it from your whole head. It
usually starts a couple of weeks after treatment begins. It grows back around three to six months after
you finish, and it may come back a different color or texture. If you want a
wig, get it before your hair falls out, so it can be matched. Ask whether a
prescription would get it covered by your insurance.

**Skin and scalp changes.** The skin in the treated area can get pink, darker,
dry, itchy or flaky. Your forehead, ears and the back of your neck may be
affected as well as your scalp. It sometimes gets worse in the week after your
last treatment, which is unfair timing. Call your team if that happens. It
usually heals over about three to four weeks.

Ask your nurse to point out exactly which skin is in the treated area, then
look after it:

- Wash gently and pat dry. Use only the moisturizers your team suggests.
- No perfume, cologne, aftershave, powder or makeup on it.
- Nothing sticky, so no adhesive bandages or medical tape.
- Do not scratch it. Put moisturizer on instead and ask about the itching.
- Avoid shaving the area. If you must, use an electric razor and stop if the
  skin gets sore.
- No tight hats or turbans rubbing on it, and keep it out of the sun. A sunburn
  in the treated area can hold up your treatment, which is worth avoiding for
  something so preventable.

**Swelling in the brain.** Radiation can make the brain swell. Symptoms you had
before can come back or get worse, and new ones can turn up. Watch for a
headache that acetaminophen does not touch, seizures, feeling sick or throwing
up, changes in vision, being unsteady on your feet, confusion, or trouble
finding words. **Call your team right away if anything is new or worse.** A
first-ever seizure, or one that lasts more than five minutes, or one that runs
straight into another, is an ambulance rather than a phone call. There is
treatment for brain swelling, usually a steroid, and telling your team early is
what keeps it simple.

**The emotional side, which can take people by surprise.** One person treated for
a meningioma said the worst effect of all was on their feelings. A few weeks in
they were sad much of the time, easily hurt and easily overwhelmed. Say that
out loud to your team if it happens to you. There is help for it, and it is not
a weakness.

### Is this the tumor, or is it the medicine?

Ask this before you assume the worst. One medicine often given during radiation
causes things that look like the tumor getting worse.

**Dexamethasone** is the steroid used to settle swelling around a tumor. It
causes weakness in the big muscles of the legs, arms, shoulders and neck.
Somebody who suddenly cannot get out of a low chair may be having a drug
effect, not a tumor effect. It also brings a bigger appetite, weight gain,
trouble sleeping and mood swings.

Your team may change your dose more than once during your treatment. When they
decide it is safe, they will give you a schedule for coming off it in steps.
That is called tapering.

**Never stop a steroid suddenly or on your own.** It has to come down slowly,
on your team's instructions. But do tell them what you are seeing, because
doses can be changed and there is usually room to move.

### Somnolence syndrome {#somnolence-syndrome}

This one has its own section because of when it happens: not during treatment,
but weeks after it has finished, when you were expecting to be picking up.

Some weeks after radiation to the head ends, some people get a stretch of heavy
drowsiness. With it come trouble concentrating, feeling clumsy, thinking
slowly, and being worn out. It has a name: somnolence syndrome. It usually gets
better on its own over a few weeks, with no treatment at all.

**When it turns up.** A patient charity in the United Kingdom puts it at four
to six weeks after radiation to the head. A small study that followed adults
through radiation for a brain tumor found it earlier as well, in two stretches:
roughly the second and third weeks after treatment, and then around the fifth.
So the window is wider than one number suggests.

**How often it happens is not settled, and the sources disagree.** That charity
calls it a rare side effect. That study found it in most of the group it
followed. It does not happen to everybody, and nobody can tell you in advance
whether it will happen to you. What both agree on is that it lifts.

**Here is why it is worth warning you about.** In that same study, people put
these symptoms down to flu, or some other bug going around. The symptoms were
heavy and had no clear cause, and that on its own made people anxious. The
study's answer was simple. Telling people in advance that this can happen cuts
the fear it causes.

So: if this happens to you, tell your team rather than sitting with it. They
will want to check that it is what it looks like. And knowing in advance that
there is a name for it, and that it is expected to lift, is most of what makes
it bearable.

## Side effects that can come later

Some effects of radiation to the brain show up months or years afterward, and
they are a real part of the decision.

- **Thinking and memory.** Radiation to a large area of brain can cause memory
  problems, trouble concentrating, and changes in personality. How much of a
  risk this is depends on how much of the brain was treated and where.
- **Radiation necrosis.** Rarely, a lump of dead tissue forms where the tumor
  was treated, months or even years later. It can swell and press on healthy
  brain. It also looks like tumor on a scan, which is why sorting the two out
  can take more scans. It is often controlled with steroid medicine, and
  sometimes an operation is needed to take it out.
- **Hormones.** Radiation that reaches the pituitary gland, at the base of the
  brain, can change the hormones that control your thyroid, your growth, your
  sex hormones and how your body handles stress. Tiredness, weight change and
  low mood can all come from that. Hormone problems after radiation to the head
  are common enough to be worth asking about, and follow-up for them is not
  standardized. **Ask whether your hormone levels will be checked at your
  follow-up appointments.** It starts with blood tests, and some hormones need
  a second, longer test before anyone can be sure.
- **Eyesight.** Radiation near the eye can cause a cataract months or years
  later, which makes vision cloudy or dim. Cataracts can usually be fixed with
  a small operation.
- **Another tumor, years later.** Radiation can damage genes in normal cells, so
  there is a small risk of a second tumor in the area that was treated. If it
  happens at all it is usually many years afterward.

None of this is a reason to turn radiation down. Your team is weighing it
against what the tumor does if it is left alone, and for people who need
radiation that small later risk does not outweigh what the treatment is for.
Ask your radiation oncologist which of these apply to the area they are
treating, and how they plan to check for them. That question is the useful
thing to do with this list.

## Am I radioactive?

No. Not during your treatment, and not afterward.

In this kind of radiation the beam comes from a machine, passes through, and is
gone. Nothing radioactive stays in you.

You are safe to be around other people and around pets. You can hug your
grandchildren and sleep in your own bed. Nobody has to keep away from you.

## Whole-brain radiation {#whole-brain-radiation}

Sometimes radiation is given to the whole brain rather than to one spot. That
is usually because there is more than one tumor in the brain.

Treating the whole brain means treating healthy brain too. That is why it is a
harder choice than aiming at one spot: what it can do to thinking and memory.

**There is a way of doing it that protects memory better, and it is worth
asking about by name.** The hippocampus is the part of the brain most involved
in learning and memory. The beam can be planned to give it as little radiation
as possible. That is called hippocampal avoidance. It is usually given together
with a medicine called memantine, which also helps protect thinking.

A large trial tested it, in people whose cancer had spread to the brain from
somewhere else. One group had whole-brain radiation planned this way, plus
memantine. The other group had standard whole-brain radiation, plus memantine.
**The people whose memory area was spared were less likely to lose thinking
skills. The tumor was controlled just as well.** The gains showed up in day to
day tasks, and in learning and remembering. Those people also said they were
less tired and found it easier to speak. The people who ran the trial say this
is a standard of care to offer.

**There are no numbers on this page for that comparison, and that is
deliberate.** Losing some thinking skills was common in both groups. Sparing
the memory area reduced that; it did not prevent it. A percentage from one
group, without that fact next to it, makes the treatment sound better than it
is. The direction is real, and the direction is what you can act on.

It does not suit everybody, and it depends on where the tumors are. If
whole-brain radiation is being talked about, ask this: **"Can my radiation be
planned to avoid the memory part of my brain, and should I be on memantine?"**
Your radiation oncologist will know the question at once.

## What you need first, and what to bring

Before treatment starts, expect:

- An MRI with dye, often lined up with the planning CT scan.
  [What an MRI is like, and what to ask](/tests/mri) goes through that
  appointment.
- The planning CT scan, in your mask, at the visit described above.
- Your pathology and gene results, because they affect whether radiation is
  given and when. [The words on your gene
  results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes them one at a time.
- A pregnancy test where that applies. If you might want children in the
  future, ask about that **before** treatment starts. It is on the list of
  questions patient groups tell you to bring to this appointment.
- Some centers test how your memory and concentration are doing beforehand, so
  there is something to compare against later.

There are a few things to sort out before your mask is made, because they
change the fit:

- Get any dental work you need done first. It can change the shape of your face
  and jaw.
- Trim a full beard or mustache, and keep it that way through treatment.
- Do not make a big change to your hairstyle.

On the day, bring or wear:

- Comfortable clothes that are easy to get in and out of.
- No jewelry, and no powder, lotion or cream on the treatment area.
- Your list of medicines, including anything you buy yourself.
- Your questions, written down.
- Something to pass the time in the waiting room. You will be doing this a lot.

## For the person caring for someone through radiation

[CAREGIVER]

Radiation is not one big day. It is a lot of small days in a row, and the load
it puts on you is different from the load surgery puts on you.

**Getting there is the job.** Every weekday, for weeks, somebody has to make
the trip. One person treated for an astrocytoma said the hardest part of the
whole thing was finding different people to drive them to a hospital
forty-five minutes away every morning. Do not try to be the only driver. Make a list of
people who offered to help and give each of them a day of the week. Ask the
center whether they have transport help, and ask whether some appointments can
be moved to a time that works better for you.

**Plan for a long appointment, not a short treatment.** The beam takes minutes.
The visit can take an hour. If you are fitting work around this, fit it around
the visit.

**What the tiredness looks like, week by week.** Not much at first. It builds
from around the second or third week, and by the end it can be most of the day.
Then, and this is the part nobody expects, **it does not stop on the last
treatment.** It carries on for weeks and sometimes months afterward before it
lifts. Somebody who is flat six weeks after the course finished is not
relapsing. They are still recovering.

**Wanting to sleep in the daytime is normal.** The advice for this kind of
tiredness is short walks most days rather than more rest, which is hard to do
alone when you feel like that. Go with them. That is the single most useful
thing on this list.

**The skin rules need somebody keeping track of them.** They are in the
side-effects section above, and there are more of them than anyone remembers
from one conversation. Ask the nurse to show you exactly which skin is in the
treated area, and write the rules down. The one worth knowing by heart is the
sun: a sunburn there can hold up their treatment.

**The wig, if they want one, has to be bought early.** It is ordered before the
hair falls out, so the color can be matched, and that means acting weeks before
anyone feels ready to. It is a good job to take off their hands. Ask whether a
prescription would get insurance to cover it.

**The steroid is the medicine to watch, and it moves.** Unlike after surgery,
the dose can be changed more than once while treatment is going on, and then
reduced in steps at the end. Get the current schedule in writing every time it
changes, and keep it somewhere you both look. And if they get weak in the legs,
hungry, sleepless or short-tempered, ask the team whether it is the steroid
before assuming it is the tumor.

**When to call the team the same day.** Ask for their own version of this list
before treatment starts. Generally, call if:

- A headache does not go away with acetaminophen.
- There is a seizure. **But if it is their first ever, or it lasts more than
  five minutes, or one runs straight into another, or they do not come round,
  that is an ambulance and not a phone call.**
- They are being sick, or feel sick and cannot keep anything down.
- Their vision changes.
- They are unsteady on their feet, more than before.
- They are confused, or their thinking has changed.
- They are mixing up words or struggling to find them.
- Any symptom they had before treatment comes back or gets worse.
- The skin in the treated area breaks, or gets much more sore.

**The mask is worth asking about, even if they have not raised it.** Some people
find it very hard and do not want to say so. If they are quiet on the way in
and quiet on the way home, it is worth asking straight out. There are things
the team can do, and they are used to being asked.

**And a date to put in your head.** Around four to six weeks after the last
treatment, some people go through a stretch of heavy drowsiness. It has a name
and it is expected to pass. There is a section about it above. Read it before
you get there, so it does not arrive as a shock.

## Why am I having radiation again?

Being told about more radiation, months or years later, is a hard conversation.
It is also a more complicated one than the first time.

Radiation can sometimes be given to an area that has been treated before. It
usually needs a long gap first, and around a year or more is the sort of
interval guidelines describe. Whether it is a good idea depends on the size of
what has come back and how it is spread out.

**Doctors do not agree about when this helps.** European guidelines say the
reasons for doing it are still unsettled, and that the evidence is not there to
settle them. Nobody is keeping an answer from you. There is not a settled one
to keep.

That is not a reason to assume it is off the table for you. It is a reason to
ask directly: is a second course of radiation something you would consider for
me, what would it be trying to do, and what else is on the list? A recurrence
usually has more than one option attached to it, and radiation is one of
several.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result?

Radiation is not a test, so there is no result at the end of the course. What
there is instead is a scan, some weeks later, and a conversation about it.

While you are on treatment, someone from the team checks in with you at least
once a week. That is where side effects get dealt with, and it is the right
place to raise anything, even something small.

**Ask before your last treatment: when is my next scan, and who tells me about
it?** Also ask whether the report will appear in your online records before
anybody calls you. For many people it does, and reading it alone on a phone is
a hard way to find out. If you would rather hear it from a person, say so.

**The first scan after radiation can look worse and not be worse.** Treatment
itself causes changes that look like tumor growth on a scan. There is a word
for it, pseudoprogression. Sorting it out often means waiting and scanning
again. If your team says that is what they want to do, that is not them
stalling. It is how the question gets answered.

## What to ask your team

- What is my radiation meant to do: deal with cells left after surgery, treat
  the tumor as the main treatment, or ease symptoms?
- How many sessions, over how many weeks, and how far do I have to travel?
- Can I drive myself to treatment?
- What will you do about the mask if I find it hard?
- Will I lose my hair, where, and will it grow back?
- Am I likely to need a steroid, and who adjusts the dose?
- What should make me call you the same day?
- What are the late effects for the area you are treating, and how will you
  check for them?
- Will my hormone levels be checked afterward?
- If I am having whole-brain radiation, can it be planned to avoid the memory
  part of my brain, and should I be on memantine?
- Could this affect my ability to have children, and who do I talk to about it
  before we start?
- When is my first scan after treatment, and who will go through it with me?
