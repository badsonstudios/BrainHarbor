## The short version {#short-version}

!%awake craniotomy%!%brain mapping%!%craniotomy%Some brain operations are done with you
awake for part of the time. The surgeon uses a tiny electric current to test
the brain next to the tumor, while you talk or move. **During the test you may
lose a word, or the use of your hand, for a moment. It comes back. That is the
test working.** It shows the surgeon a part of your brain to leave alone. You
are asleep, or very sleepy, while your skull is opened and closed.

## What is an awake craniotomy? {#what-it-is}

It is brain surgery where you are woken up in the middle, so you can help.

A craniotomy is the operation to open the skull and take out a tumor. In an
awake craniotomy, you are asleep or very drowsy while the skull is opened. Then
the medicine is turned down and you wake up. While you are awake, the surgeon
finds out which parts of your brain do which jobs. That part is called brain
mapping. Then you are made very sleepy again, or put back to sleep, for the
end.

Your brain has no nerves that feel pain. That is what makes this possible.
Your scalp does feel pain, so it is numbed first.

A scan before the day can suggest where speech or movement sit in your brain,
and it helps plan the operation. Testing the brain itself, while you do the
job, is how the surgeon checks it in the moment.

## Why am I having one? {#why}

Because your tumor is close to a part of your brain that does something you
would notice losing. That might be speaking, understanding words, moving,
feeling or seeing.

Everyone's brain is laid out a little differently. The surgeon wants to take
out as much tumor as is safe. To do that, they need to know where the working
brain starts, in your brain rather than in a textbook. For speech, the way to
find that out is to test it while you use it. Movement can sometimes be checked
while you are asleep. Speech and feeling need you awake to answer.

It is not done for everyone, and it is not done for every tumor. One surgeon
who does these says it is a big deal for the patient, so it should only be done
when it is really needed. Gliomas are the tumors it is most often used for.

To have it, you need to be able to lie still for a long time and to talk with
the team. A lot of trouble speaking or understanding before the operation can
rule it out, though sometimes that can be treated first. So can serious confusion, or some breathing problems during sleep. That is
why your team asks so many questions about you first.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

### Before the day

1. **A talk with your surgeon.** This usually covers why you need to be awake,
   what the room is like, when you will be awake, and what you will be asked to
   do.
   Tell them about yourself too, because that part matters. See
   [what you need first](#before).
2. **Practice.** Often you meet a speech therapist or a brain doctor, and go
   through the tests you will do during the operation. It is usually the same
   pictures and words each time. That way, the team knows what your normal
   answers look like.
3. **Maybe a special scan.** Some hospitals do a functional MRI. You do simple
   tasks inside the scanner while it watches which parts of your brain get
   busy. It helps plan the operation. It does not replace the test in the room.

### On the day

4. **You are made very sleepy, or put to sleep.** Your scalp is numbed, and a
   frame is fixed to your head so it cannot move. You may be lying on your back
   or on your side. Then the surgeon opens the skull. The medicine and the
   numbing are there so that you do not feel it.
5. **You are woken up.** The medicine is turned down. Waking up in an operating
   room is strange. The team talks you through where you are before anything
   else happens. There may be sheets hung up around you.
6. **The test.** The surgeon touches one small spot on your brain with a tiny
   electric current, for a few seconds at a time. While they do, you do a task.
   The same spot may be tested more than once.
7. **The tumor comes out.** The team keeps checking your speech or movement
   while the surgeon works, so they notice a change as it starts. That is how
   they know where to stop. A change at this stage is not the test. It may not
   pass, and that is exactly why they stop.
8. **You are made very sleepy again, or put back to sleep,** while the skull is
   closed.

### Your job while you are awake

What you are asked to do depends on where the tumor is. For speech, you may be
asked to:

- Name things in pictures.
- Repeat words or sentences.
- Read words out loud.
- Count, or say the alphabet.
- Answer simple questions, like your name.

For movement, you may be asked to squeeze a ball or a hand, lift an arm or a
leg, or move your fingers or toes. For feeling and seeing, you may be asked
what you see, or to say when and where you feel a touch.

## How long does it take? {#how-long}

The whole operation takes hours. The awake part is not all of it, but it can be
a long stretch of lying still. One team that does these tries to keep the awake
part to about two hours or less, because people get tired and tired people make
more mistakes on the tests.

At each spot, the current is on for only a few seconds, usually less than
four.

How long you stay in hospital afterwards depends on the operation and on you.
[The brain surgery page](/treatments/craniotomy#how-long) goes through the stay
and the weeks after it.

## What does it feel like? {#what-it-feels-like}

### When a word or a hand stops working {#the-test-working}

This is the part to know before you go in, because it is frightening if
nobody warns you.

When the current touches a spot that does a job, that job stops for a moment.
You may know exactly what a picture shows and not be able to say the word. You
may start counting and find the numbers will not come. Your hand may not move
when you tell it to, or it may twitch on its own.

**Then the current stops, and the word or the movement comes back.** That is
the test working.
It has just shown the surgeon a spot to leave alone.

One person who had this done could not say anything after the letter A while
the surgeon tested one spot. The team left that small piece of tumor where it
was, to protect that person's memory.

A speech therapist on one of these teams puts it simply: the operation is the
best time to make a mistake. Finding the spots that cause mistakes is the whole
point. **So if you get something wrong, you have not failed anything.** You
have helped.

### The rest of it

- **No pain from your brain.** You may feel odd things, but they should not
  hurt. A test near the movement area can make a muscle tighten by itself. A
  test near the feeling area can make part of your body tingle.
- **If anything does hurt, say so right away.** One surgeon says most people
  do not want to bother the team during the operation. Tell them anyway. They
  want to know, and they can do something about it.
- **Lying still is often harder than anything that hurts.** One patient's hips
  ached from lying on one side. The team stopped, moved them, and gave them a
  pillow, several times.
- **Noise.** You may hear the tools and the team talking.
- **Feeling cold.** The team has warm blankets. Say if you are cold.

Afterwards, some people remember parts of it and some remember very little. In one
small study of people who had it done, some remembered nothing of it at all.
In the same study, more than half remembered the frame going on their head, a
couple of them found that sore, and two had pain in their teeth. None of them
said it was harder than they had expected.

## What is hard about it, and what can be done {#what-is-hard}

**Being scared of it.** One person who had it done said the hardest part was
the night before, imagining it, and being afraid of feeling pain. Touching
your brain itself should not hurt, because it has no nerves that feel pain. Go
through every question you have at the talk
beforehand, and ask to go through the tests more than once if that helps.

**Panic, or feeling closed in.** Say so at the talk before the day, and say so
on the day if it happens. The doctor in charge of your sleep medicine and your
breathing, the anesthesiologist, is there the whole time. They can change how
sleepy you are.

**The tube for your bladder.** Because you lie still for hours, you may have a
thin tube put in to drain your bladder. If that worries you, bring it up at the
talk. It may not be possible to avoid it, and it is still worth asking. One
team found another way for a patient who did not want one.

**Feeling that you got the answers wrong.** You did not. A wrong answer during
the test is information the surgeon needs. It is not a mark against you.

## What can go wrong {#what-can-go-wrong}

Everything on [what can go wrong in any brain
operation](/treatments/craniotomy#what-can-go-wrong) applies here too. Being
awake adds two things of its own, and there is one more worth saying here.

**A seizure during the test.** The current can set off a seizure. There is no
number for this on the page. The studies disagree so much about how often it
happens that any one figure would give you the wrong idea. It is more likely if
you have had seizures before, and for tumors in some parts of the brain. Often
it is brief and stops on its own. The team is ready for it. The first thing
they do is rinse the brain with ice-cold salty water, and there is medicine if
that is not enough. Most pass without doing any lasting harm, though an arm or
a leg can stay weak for a while afterwards. Rarely, one is serious enough to
affect breathing or the heart. A doctor who watches your breathing is in the
room the whole time. Ask what your own risk is.

**The awake part has to stop.** Sometimes it cannot go on. This is uncommon.
The usual reasons are a seizure, or that you can no longer talk with the team.
Then you are put back to sleep and the operation carries on. That is planned
for. It is also not nothing: in one large study, people whose awake part could not be finished had
less of their tumor taken out, and more trouble with speech afterwards. The
same study found most of those cases could have been avoided, by choosing
carefully who has it done and by care with the medicines given during it. That
is the real reason for all the questions before the day.

**Waking up with something new.** After the operation, some people have a new
problem, like trouble finding words or a weak arm. The test is there to protect
you from that. It cannot rule it out. Many new problems after brain surgery
get better over the following weeks. Some do not. If your tumor is near the
area that starts movement and speech, read [the part about SMA
syndrome](/treatments/craniotomy#sma-syndrome) before the day. Ask your team
what they expect for you.

## Is being awake the better way? {#is-it-better}

Not for everyone. It is a tool for one situation: a tumor close to brain you
need. The surgeon quoted above says that when the tumor is not in that kind of
place, being asleep is much easier on you.

Some studies suggest that people with tumors in those places may have fewer
new problems afterwards if they are awake. But most of those studies cannot
settle it. The people chosen to be awake may have been different from the
people who were not, so the comparison may not be fair. Whether it is better
has not been proved. Even so, the experts who pulled those studies together
think it should be strongly considered for high-grade gliomas near speech or
movement, at a hospital with the experience.

Whether you need it depends on exactly where your tumor is. If your tumor is
next to the parts for speech and being awake has not come up, it is fair to ask
why. A second opinion is normal.

**If you do not want to be awake,** say so. Being willing is part of how the
choice is made. Ask what they would do instead, and what that would change.

## What you need first {#before}

**Tell them what matters to you.** At the talk beforehand, tell the team what
you most need to keep, even if they do not ask. Maybe it is a job that depends
on your hands, or playing an instrument. It can shape which tests they use, and
where they stop. One patient was asked, during the operation, whether the
surgeon should go further, when going further risked feeling and movement in
part of the face. That person chose to stop.

**Do the practice properly.** Ask for as much practice as you want. If a
picture shows something you would never have known the name of anyway, say so.
One patient called a picture of a swan a bird, because swan was not a word
they knew.
Finding that out early meant nobody mistook it for a problem during the
operation.

**Ask how many of these your surgeon does.** It is a special kind of operation,
and some hospitals do far more of them than others. If there is time, a second
opinion is normal.

**Get ready as you would for any brain operation.** [Getting ready for
surgery](/tests/getting-ready-for-surgery) goes through the tests, the fasting
rules and your medicines. **Read it before you change any medicine, and above
all a blood thinner.**

## For the person going with them {#caregiver}

[CAREGIVER]

**Go to the talk and the practice if you can.** Questions tend to come up
later, at home, and it helps if you heard the answers too. You may also know
better than anyone what they most need to keep.

**On the day, expect a long wait.** The operation takes hours. A long wait does
not mean something has gone wrong.

**After the operation, the caregiving is much like it is after any brain
operation.** The wound, the medicines, the two lists of when to call, and how
long it goes on are all in [the caregiver part of the brain surgery
page](/treatments/craniotomy#caregiver). Read it before the day.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result? {#results}

This operation gives you three answers.

**How much came out** comes from a scan in the first days afterwards. [What the
words about how much came out mean](/treatments/craniotomy#how-much-came-out)
goes through them.

**What the tumor is** comes from the lab, and takes longer. [Waiting for your
pathology results](/tests/waiting-for-results) explains that wait.

**What the test found** is the third answer, and it is easy to forget to ask
for it. Ask your surgeon where the working brain was, and whether any tumor
was left because of it. If some was left to protect your speech or movement,
that was the test doing its job.

Ask before you leave who will go through all three with you, and when.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Why do I need to be awake, and why is asleep not enough for my tumor?
- What will you ask me to do, and can I practice it more than once?
- What will I feel, and what odd feelings should I expect?
- Who will be with me and talking to me while I am awake?
- What happens if I panic, or cannot do the tasks?
- Can I take anything to help me sleep the night before?
- Will I have a tube for my bladder, and when does it go in?
- Does anything about me, like earlier seizures, make a seizure during the
  operation more likely?
- If the awake part has to stop, what is the plan then?
- How many of these operations does my surgeon do?
- What should I tell you about my life, so you know what matters most to keep?
- Afterwards: what did the test find, and was any tumor left because of it?
