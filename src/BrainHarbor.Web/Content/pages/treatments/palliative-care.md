## The short version {#short-version}

Palliative care is an extra team that helps you feel better. It helps with pain
and other symptoms, with worry, and with the strain on your family. **You can
have it at any stage, at the same time as treatment for the tumor.** It is not
the same as hospice, and asking for it is not giving up. If your team has not
offered it, you can ask for it.

## What is palliative care? {#what-it-is}

It is care that aims to make life better while you live with a serious illness.
It does not treat the tumor itself. It treats the things that come with it.

The National Cancer Institute says anyone can have it, at any age and at any
stage. It can start as soon as you find out you have a tumor.

The people who give it are called a palliative care team. They work alongside
your cancer team. They do not replace them.

## Is it the same as hospice? {#not-hospice}

**No.** Hospice is one kind of palliative care. It is for when treatment aimed
at the tumor has stopped, and the goal is comfort. Palliative care is much
wider than that. You can keep having treatment aimed at the tumor the whole time
you have it.

Many people mix the two up. A National Cancer Institute survey found that many
adults who had heard of palliative care thought it was the same as hospice. The
institute says this mix-up may stop people from asking for it, or from saying
yes when it is offered.

You may see the mix-up on official papers too. Medicare's booklet about hospice
calls palliative care a part of hospice care. That booklet is only about
hospice. Outside hospice, palliative care can go alongside any treatment.

## Why ask for it early? {#why-early}

**Because people who wait until treatment stops may miss much of the help.** A
large group of cancer doctors, the American Society of Clinical Oncology, says
people with advanced cancer should be sent to a palliative care team early. It
says early means not waiting until treatment aimed at the cancer stops. Ask your
team whether that includes you. Anyone with a serious illness can ask for it.

A brain tumor adds a reason of its own. It can change thinking, memory and
personality, sometimes early on. Talking through what matters to you now helps make
sure your own wishes are known and written down.

Many people with brain tumors never get it. In one large US study of older
people with glioblastoma, most never saw a palliative care team at all. So you
may need to ask.

## What can it help with? {#what-it-helps}

- **Symptoms and side effects.** Pain, headaches, tiredness, feeling sick to
  your stomach, trouble sleeping, and not wanting to eat.
- **Feelings.** Worry, fear and low mood.
- **Faith and meaning,** if that matters to you.
- **Practical things.** Money worries, insurance, work, forms, and getting to
  appointments.
- **Planning ahead.** Talking about what matters most to you, and putting your
  wishes on paper. That paper is called an advance directive.
- **Your family.** The team helps the people who care for you too.

## Who is on the team? {#team}

The team has doctors and nurses with extra training in palliative care.
It can also include social workers, pharmacists, dietitians, therapists,
psychologists and chaplains.

**They work with your cancer team, not instead of it.** You keep your own
doctors, and they keep treating the tumor. The palliative care team helps
everyone work from the same plan.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

1. **Ask your cancer team to refer you.** If they have not brought it up, you
   can.
2. **Check your insurance.** Ask your plan which palliative care teams it pays
   for.
3. **The team asks what bothers you most.** They ask about symptoms, worries,
   and what matters most to you.
4. **They make a plan that fits what matters to you.** It may mean a change
   of medicine, help with forms or money worries, or someone else on the team
   joining in.
5. **They keep in touch with your cancer team,** so the two plans fit.
6. **It goes on alongside your treatment,** and it can carry on after treatment
   ends.

## Where does it happen? {#where}

In the hospital, at a clinic or cancer center, or at home. Some teams visit
people in nursing homes or assisted living. Ask where your visits would be.

## Does it mean giving up? {#giving-up}

**No.** You can keep having treatment aimed at the tumor. The National Brain
Tumor Society says help from a palliative care team does not mean there is no
more hope. It says accepting the help is not giving up. It is speaking up for
what you need.

## Does it help people live longer? {#live-longer}

**That is not known for brain tumors.** You may read that it does. That idea
comes mainly from research in other cancers, such as one study of people with
lung cancer. It has not been shown for brain tumors. What palliative care is for
is how you feel from day to day. The National Cancer Institute says that is what
it is meant to improve.

## What does it cost? {#cost}

**Most insurance pays for some palliative care, but what is covered differs.**
The National Cancer Institute says private insurance usually covers it, and
Medicare and Medicaid pay for some kinds of it. What Medicaid covers depends on
your state. Veterans may be able to get it through the VA.

If you have no insurance, or are not sure what yours covers, ask a social
worker or the hospital's financial counselor.

## Children and teens {#children}

**Palliative care is for children too, and for the whole family.** It can start
at any age. For a child, the team may include a child life specialist.

There is one rule that is different for children. In the US, a child under 21
who has Medicaid, and who qualifies for hospice, can have hospice and keep
having treatment aimed at the tumor at the same time. Some CHIP plans work the
same way. If your child has CHIP or other insurance, ask how its rules work.

## What is hard about it, and what can be done {#hard}

**The word can be frightening.** Many people think it means giving up. If it
frightens you, ask the team to tell you what they would actually do for you.

**Your team may not bring it up.** The American Society of Clinical Oncology
says some cancer doctors worry that offering it will sound like giving up. So
you may have to ask first. The American Cancer Society says you or your family
can ask for the help you need.

**There may not be a team near you.** The Center to Advance Palliative Care has
a [directory you can search](https://getpalliativecare.org/provider-directory/)
by address or zip code. It lists only the teams that have signed up, so ask
your own hospital too.

## Hospice, if and when it comes up {#hospice}

:::outlook
**Hospice is a kind of palliative care for when treatment aimed at the tumor
has stopped.** The goal is comfort, for the person and the family. It can be
given at home, in a nursing home, in a hospital, or in a hospice center.

**To get hospice through Medicare,** your doctors have to certify that the illness
is in its last stage. You sign a form choosing hospice instead of the other
treatment Medicare would pay for to treat that illness. Medicare says you accept
comfort care instead of care to cure it. It still pays for care for other
health problems, though you may owe your usual share of the cost.

**You can change your mind.** Medicare says you always have the right to stop
hospice at any time and go back to treatment. If you qualify, you can go back
to hospice later too.

**Medicare says you pay nothing for hospice** from a Medicare-approved provider,
apart from small charges for some medicines and for short stays that give the
family a rest. It does not pay for room and board.

**Most of the day-to-day care at home is given by family and friends.** The
hospice team visits, helps with symptoms, and shows the family what to do.

**You can talk to a hospice doctor once without signing up.** Medicare pays for
a one-time talk about your choices, even if you decide not to have hospice.

In one small US study, all of the families who had lost an adult relative to a
brain tumor said they wished they had accepted hospice sooner. The National
Institute on Aging says starting hospice sooner may give more time with that
help.

Your palliative care team can help you talk about hospice before you have to
decide anything. Ask them when it would be time to think about it.
:::

## If you are caring for someone {#caregiver}

**The team is for you too.** The National Cancer Institute says palliative care
specialists help families and friends cope. You can ask them for help with your
own stress and worry. You do not have to wait until you cannot manage.

The American Society of Clinical Oncology says the cancer team can send
caregivers to a palliative care team for support of their own. So you can ask
for that, by name.

Looking after yourself is part of looking after them. You can talk to a person
today through [get help now](/get-help-now).

## Who does it, and who do I call? {#who}

A palliative care team does it, at a hospital, a clinic, or at home. Your cancer
team still looks after the treatment for the tumor.

Ask both teams who to call about symptoms between visits, and who to call at
night. Write the numbers down where you can see them.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Can I see a palliative care team now, while I am having treatment?
- Would seeing them change my treatment in any way?
- Who is on the team, and where would I see them?
- Can they help my family too?
- Who do I call about symptoms between visits, and at night?
- Does my insurance pay for it?
- Can the team help me write down my wishes?
- When would it be time to talk about hospice?
