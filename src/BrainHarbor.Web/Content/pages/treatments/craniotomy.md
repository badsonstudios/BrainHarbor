## The short version

!%craniotomy%A surgeon opens a window in the skull, takes out as much tumor as
is safe, and puts the piece of bone back at the end. The operation is called a
craniotomy. Most people need intensive care at first, then a few more days on a
ward. The brain itself feels no pain. The part people are least ready for is
not the operation. It is the weeks after it, and that is where a lot of this
page goes.

## What is a craniotomy?

It is an operation to reach something inside your skull.
!%bone flap%!%craniectomy%!%5-ALA%!%SMA syndrome%!%laser ablation%!%levetiracetam%

The surgeon makes a cut in your scalp and folds the skin back. Then a piece of
skull bone over the tumor is taken out. That piece is called a bone flap. With
it out of the way, the surgeon can see the tumor and take out as much of it as
is safe.

At the end the bone goes back. It is held in place with small metal plates and
screws. The metal is usually titanium. It does not set off metal detectors, and
it is safe in an MRI scanner.

The name for this operation is a craniotomy.

Sometimes the bone is not put back straight away. That is called a craniectomy,
and it is done to leave room if the brain is likely to swell. The bone is
usually put back later, in a smaller operation.

## Why am I having one?

There is more than one reason to operate, and it is worth knowing which one is
yours.

- **To find out what it is.** Only a piece of the tumor itself, looked at in a
  lab, can give it a name. Your team may already have a good idea from the
  scans. They cannot be sure without tissue.
- **To take out as much as is safe.** Less tumor left behind can mean less for
  radiation or medicines to deal with afterwards.
- **To take the pressure down.** A growth inside a closed skull pushes on
  everything around it. That is what causes a lot of headaches, nausea,
  vomiting and blurred vision. Taking out even part of it can ease that.
- **To help with seizures.** Surgery can make seizures easier to control with
  medicine.

Often it is more than one of these at once.

**The goal is not always "take it all".** The usual aim is to remove as much as
can be removed without affecting how your brain works. Surgeons have a name for
that: maximal safe resection. Where the tumor sits decides how much that is.
Ask which of these your operation is for. It changes what a good result looks
like.

## What happens, step by step

### Before the day

You will have a scan first, usually an MRI. Some of it is for the surgeon to
navigate by on the day. [What an MRI is like, and what to
ask](/tests/mri) goes through that appointment.

You will have blood tests. You will meet the doctor who puts you to sleep, the
anesthesiologist, to go through your allergies and anything that has happened
to you under anesthetic before. You will meet the surgeon again to go over the
plan and the risks.

Follow the hospital's instructions for the day or two before. They differ from
place to place, so use the ones you were given rather than anything you read
anywhere else.

Bring your advance directive or living will if you have one. It says what you
want if you cannot speak for yourself.

Asking for a second opinion first is normal and it is not an insult to anyone.
If there is time, it is worth doing.

### The day

You change into a gown and take off jewelry and contact lenses. An IV line goes
into your arm, for fluids and medicines.

Part of your head may be shaved. It is usually only the area around the cut,
not all of it. Ask beforehand if this matters to you, because it varies between
hospitals and surgeons.

You are put to sleep. Your head is then held still in a frame so that it cannot
move while the surgeon works.

Your scalp is numbed as well, even though you are asleep. **The brain itself has
no feeling in it.** That is the reason some of these operations can be done as an
awake craniotomy. That is a different plan, for tumors sitting next to speech or
movement, and your team would talk it through with you well beforehand.

While you are asleep, the surgeon uses more than a pair of eyes:

- **A map built from your scan.** It shows where the tumor is under the surface.
- **A microscope**, because the difference between tumor and healthy brain can
  be very hard to see.
- **A medicine that makes tumor glow.** For some tumors you are given something
  beforehand that the tumor takes up. Under a special light it glows, so the
  surgeon can tell it from normal brain. It is called 5-ALA.
- **Checks on the brain itself.** The surgeon can stimulate an area and watch
  what it does, to find out whether it is doing a job you would notice losing.
- **A scanner in the room.** Some hospitals can take an MRI part way through to
  see what is left.

A piece of the tumor goes to the lab while you are still in the operating room.

### Waking up

You wake up in a recovery area and then go somewhere you can be watched
closely. Most people need intensive care at first.

Being in intensive care means being checked on very often, including overnight.
Someone will keep asking how you are and testing how you are doing. It is
tiring, and it is not a sign that anything is wrong. Problems can show up right
away or days later, so noticing a change early is what the checks are for.

You may have a thin tube coming out of the wound to let fluid drain away. It
usually comes out after a few days.

### The days after

If nothing is going wrong, the team will have you up and moving the day after
surgery.
That feels early. It is deliberate: moving keeps your blood moving, helps your
chest, and keeps your insides working.

You may see a physical therapist, an occupational therapist or a speech
therapist,
depending on where in the brain the tumor was. A tumor in the balance part at
the back tends to mean work on moving. One near the speech areas tends to mean
work on words.

You go home, or to a rehab unit, when you are stable, nothing has gone wrong,
and there is a plan for where you are going.

## How long does it take?

The operation itself takes hours, and how many depends on where the tumor is
and how much is being done. Your family will be waiting a long time. That is
normal and it is not a sign of trouble.

- **In hospital:** usually about four to six days. Bigger or more awkward
  tumors, and other health problems, make it longer.
- **A scan to see what came out:** usually one to three days after the
  operation.
- **Stitches or staples out:** usually about a week to ten days. Some are the
  kind that dissolve on their own.
- **The wound healing over:** several weeks.
- **Feeling like yourself again:** it can take a month or two, and for many
  people longer than they were expecting.

## What does it feel like?

Waking up is strange, and it is different for everyone. One person who has done
it twice described feeling like a floating consciousness, apart from their own
body.

Being confused for a while is normal. So is not remembering things that happened
around the surgery. It gets better as the anesthetic wears off.

You will have a headache. There is medicine for it. Some hospitals give pain
medicine on a fixed schedule rather than waiting for you to ask. That is worth
having, because pain creeps up before you notice it. **If yours does not do it
that way, ask whether you can have it on a schedule.** You are allowed to ask.

Feeling sick is common. Say so, because it can be treated, and there is no
prize for putting up with it.

Around the wound you may get numbness, odd shooting pains, and itching as it
heals. You may also get swelling and bruising around your eyes. That can look
alarming, and it is a normal part of healing.

If you wake up and something is not working the way it was, that is frightening
in a way that is hard to describe. Say what you notice, straight away, to the
person in front of you. A lot of what shows up right after surgery does get
better.

## "How much did you get out?" {#how-much-came-out}

This is the first question almost everyone asks, and the words that come back
are easy to misread. Here is what they actually mean.

For the first two words below, the answer does not come from what the surgeon
saw in the room. It comes from a scan done in the first few days after the
operation, and what that scan can see is what the words describe. The third is
about the aim rather than the result.

- **!%gross total resection%Gross total resection.** No tumor can be seen on
  that scan. Some reports call it a complete resection.
- **!%subtotal resection%Subtotal resection.** Some tumor can still be seen.
  Also called a partial resection.
- **!%debulking%Debulking.** The aim was to take out bulk to relieve pressure,
  rather than to take out all of it.

**Now the part that is rarely said out loud.** Gross total resection means
nothing was visible on a scan. It does not mean cure. In a diffuse glioma, the
cells spread out into the brain around the tumor, which is why the edge is not
a clean line. Cells left behind that way are far too small for any scan to
show. They are still there after the best operation anyone can do.

That is not a reason to think the surgery failed. It is the reason radiation or
medicines usually follow it, and the reason you get scanned again later. If
nobody explains this, then "we got it all" followed months later by "it is back"
sounds like somebody lied to you. Nobody lied. The two sentences were about
different things.

The other half is just as true. **For some tumor types, surgery alone can deal
with it.** Which group you are in depends on what the tumor turns out to be.
That answer comes from the lab, not from the operating room.

And a subtotal resection is not a failed operation. When a surgeon stops short,
it is almost always because going further would have cost you something you
would not want to lose. That is a decision made for you, in the moment, with
your life afterwards in mind. It is the right call far more often than not.

## What can go wrong

Every operation carries risk, and brain surgery is a serious one. Your team will
go through your risks with you before you sign anything.

There is no percentage on this page, and that is deliberate. The published
figures for these risks vary so widely between studies that any single number
would mislead you more than it helps. Your surgeon can tell you what your
risk looks like, which is the only version that is about you.

The general risks are bleeding, infection, a reaction to the anesthetic, blood
clots, a leak of the fluid that surrounds the brain, and seizures. Bleeding,
infection and reactions to the anesthetic are **not common**, which is the
honest shape of it without a number. Swelling of
the brain is a particular concern after this operation, which is why steroid
medicine is usually given before surgery and for a few days after.

**Where the tumor is decides most of the rest.** Some parts of the brain do a
job you would notice losing. Surgery in or beside one of them carries the risk
of affecting that job. Surgeons call a part of the brain like that an eloquent
area, and you may hear the word or see it in your notes. That is why so much of
the planning is about location, and why mapping and monitoring are used at all.

Problems do not all show up at once. Something can appear right after surgery,
or days or even weeks later. That is why you are watched closely at first and
why you are told what to look out for at home.

**And the message that matters most:** many new problems after brain surgery get
better over the following weeks. Some do not. Your team can tell you which they
think yours is, and they would rather you asked than lay awake guessing.

### If your tumor was near the supplementary motor area {#sma-syndrome}

This one has its own section because it is terrifying if nobody warns you, and
ordinary if they do.

There is an area towards the front and middle of the brain that helps get
movement and speech started. Surgery in or near it can leave someone, right
afterwards, unable to start speaking and unable to move one side of the body
properly. It arrives immediately. It looks exactly like the worst thing you were
afraid of.

It has a name, SMA syndrome, and the important thing about it is that it is
expected to pass. It usually begins to improve within a few days, and keeps
improving over the weeks and sometimes months after that. People who are not
told about it beforehand often believe they have been left permanently
disabled, and spend that first week in despair before anybody explains it.

If your tumor is in this area, ask about this before your operation. Ask again
afterwards if it happens. Ask your team what they saw and what they expect.

## What is different in the weeks after

This is the part people most often say they were not prepared for, and it is
the part that goes on longest.

**Day two or three often feels worse than day one.** Thinking can feel foggier,
and words can be harder to find, exactly when you expected to be getting better.
This is a known pattern and it is expected. It should be improving over the
following month.

**Tiredness, for weeks.** More than ordinary tiredness, and it does not lift
after one good night. Wanting to sleep in the daytime is normal, and a nap
every day is a reasonable plan rather than giving in.

**Attention is usually the thing that changes.** A speech specialist describes
it as the filter you use to focus on one thing and ignore everything else being
less airtight. Noise, crowds and several people talking at once become much
harder work. A conversation with one person is far easier than a conversation
with three. A room full of visitors can finish you inside ten minutes.

**Words can be slower to come.** Trouble finding a word is the language problem
that lasts most often. It usually improves, and there are speech therapists
whose job is exactly this.

**Nobody can see any of it.** Once hair grows back over the scar people stop
making allowances, because there is nothing left to see. That is the point
people say is the hardest, and it arrives after everybody else has decided you
are better. Say so when it happens. Telling the people around you that the
difficulty outlasts the scar is worth doing, because they cannot see it and
will not guess.

### Is this the tumor, or is it the medicine?

Ask this before you assume the worst, because two medicines given after this
operation cause things that look exactly like the tumor getting worse.

- **Dexamethasone**, the steroid used to settle swelling, causes muscle
  weakness in the legs, arms, shoulders and neck. Someone who cannot get out of
  a low chair may be having a drug effect, not a tumor effect. It also brings a
  bigger appetite, weight gain, trouble sleeping and mood swings, and it can
  hide a fever, which matters when you are watching for infection.
- **Levetiracetam** is one of the commonest medicines used to prevent seizures.
  It is linked with irritability, low mood and anger. Families blame the
  personality change on the tumor, or on everything the person has been
  through. Sometimes it is the pills.

Neither of these is a reason to stop a medicine on your own. Steroids in
particular have to be reduced slowly, on your team's instructions. It is a
reason to tell your team what you are seeing, because doses can be changed and
medicines can be swapped.

## Is there a way to do this without opening the skull?

Sometimes, for some tumors. It is worth asking about, and it is worth
understanding what it is.

One alternative is **laser ablation**, also called LITT. A thin probe with
a laser on the end is put through a small hole in the skull and into the tumor,
guided by MRI. The laser heats the tumor and destroys it, while limiting damage
around it.

It suits tumors that are hard to reach, or hard to operate on in the usual way.
Recovery from it is generally quicker than from a craniotomy. It is still a
fairly new technique, so doctors are still working out where it is the best
choice.

What it is not is a gentler route to the same result. It is a different
operation, for a different set of tumors, and it is not offered everywhere.
Ask your team whether it applies to you rather than asking for it.

## What you need first, and what to bring

Before the day, expect:

- An MRI, sometimes more than one, including one for the surgeon to navigate by.
- Extra scans if the tumor is near the areas for speech or movement, to map
  where those areas sit in your particular brain.
- Blood tests, and heart or chest checks if the anesthesiologist wants them.
- A review of everything you take, including anything you buy yourself.
- Tests of how you are doing now, so there is something to compare with
  afterwards.

Bring:

- Your advance directive or living will.
- A list of your medicines, with doses.
- Your questions, written down. You will not remember them otherwise.
- Someone with you, if you can.
- Very little else. You will not want your own clothes for the first few days,
  and things go missing.

## For the person caring for someone after surgery

[CAREGIVER]

Surgery is where a lot gets handed to you, usually with very little warning.
What follows is what people say they wish they had been told.

**The wound.** Keep it dry. They can shower, but the cut should not get wet, and
hair should not be washed until the stitches or staples are out. No creams,
lotions or ointments on it unless the team says so. If there are strips of tape,
leave them alone until they fall off. No hair dye until the team says it is fine.

**Learn what a healing wound looks like before you need to know.** Ask someone
to show you the wound. Ask what they expect it to look like in a week. It is
very hard to judge "is this worse?" against nothing.

**Ask how the wound is closed, and how it comes undone.** Stitches or staples
usually come out about a week to ten days after surgery, at an appointment. Some
are the kind that dissolve on their own and need no appointment at all. Find out
which before you leave the hospital, and make sure any appointment exists.

**Medicines.** There will be more of them than you expect, and the doses change.
The steroid is the one to be careful with: it is usually reduced in steps over
time, and it must not be stopped suddenly. Write the schedule down somewhere you
can both see it. A pill box with the days marked on it is worth more than a
good memory at week three.

**What normal looks like, day by day.** Very tired for weeks. Sleeping in the
day. Headaches. Trouble concentrating. Swelling or bruising around the eyes.
Numbness, shooting pains and itching near the wound. Feeling worse on day two or
three than on day one. Being flatter, quieter or shorter-tempered than usual.
None of that means something has gone wrong. Do mention the mood and temper
changes at the next appointment, though, because they can be the medicines
rather than the surgery, and medicines can be changed.

**Two rules they will be given, and will be tempted to bend.** No driving until
their team says it is allowed. No heavy lifting until their team says it is
allowed, and ask what counts as heavy, because the limit is usually lower than
people expect.

**Keep it quiet, and keep it small.** One visitor at a time. Short visits. A
room full of people who came to be kind can wipe them out in ten minutes, and
they may not be able to say so.

**When to call the team the same day.** Ask for this list in their own words
before you leave. Ask what temperature counts as a fever for them. Generally it
is any of these:

- The wound opens, or gets more painful, red, warm or swollen.
- Anything leaks from the wound.
- A fever, or a stiff neck.
- A headache that is new, or worse, or comes with throwing up.
- Confusion, or trouble thinking clearly.
- A sudden change in vision.
- Sleeping much more than they are awake.
- A fall, or a hit to the head.
- Pain that the pain medicine is not touching.

**When to call an ambulance.** These cannot wait:

- They are not breathing properly.
- You cannot wake them.
- A seizure that is their first, or that lasts more than five minutes, or that
  runs straight into another. (Not every seizure needs an ambulance. The
  seizure page below says which do.)
- They suddenly cannot speak, move, or see.
- Chest pain, or coughing up blood.

If you are not sure which list this is, call.

**How long are you doing this for?** The intense part is the first few weeks.
Most people take a month or two to feel like themselves. The invisible part, the
tiredness and the attention and the words, often runs on past that. Plan for
longer than the discharge letter suggests. At the follow-up appointment, ask out
loud: what should we expect this to look like in a month?

**And you cannot do all of it.** The person you are looking after has a whole
team. You are allowed to call them. You are allowed to say you are not managing.
Say yes when someone offers help.

## Why am I having another operation?

Being told you need surgery again is a hard conversation, and it is not a sign
that the first one failed.

The usual reasons are that a tumor has come back or grown, that what was left
behind needs dealing with now, or that something needs relieving again. For some
tumor types, more than one operation over the years is simply how it goes.

Ask what is different this time, and what the goal is now. The goal often
changes between a first operation and a second one, and it is the thing worth
being clear about before you agree to it.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result?

Two different answers come out of this operation, at two different speeds.

**How much came out** comes from the scan in the first few days. Your surgeon or
a member of the team goes through it with you.

**What the tumor actually is** comes from the lab, and it takes longer. The
tissue is examined, then usually tested for gene changes, and the full answer
arrives in stages over days to weeks. [Waiting for your pathology
results](/tests/waiting-for-results) explains what is happening in that gap and
why the name can change while you wait.

When the report comes, [your pathology report, part by
part](/tests/pathology-report) goes through the document. [The words on your
gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes the short words on it one at a
time.

Your case may also go to a tumor board.

[TUMOR-BOARD]

Before you leave the hospital, ask who will give you the results, roughly when,
and whether that will be in a clinic or by phone. Ask one more thing: will it
appear in your online records first? For many people it does, and reading it
alone on a phone is a hard way to find out. If you would rather hear it from a
person, say so, and ask them to call you when it lands.

## What to ask your team

- What is the goal of my operation: to take it all out, to take some out, to
  relieve pressure, or to get tissue?
- What is next to my tumor, and what could I lose?
- Will you use mapping or monitoring during the operation?
- How much of my hair will be cut, and where will the scar be?
- Will I be scanned afterwards to see how much came out, and when?
- How long in intensive care, how long in hospital, and am I going home or to a
  rehab unit?
- If something is not working when I wake up, how will we know whether it is
  temporary?
- Which of my medicines am I going home on, and which one is being reduced?
- Who do I call after I go home, and what counts as calling today rather than
  waiting?
- When will we know what the tumor is, and will I need more treatment after
  this?
