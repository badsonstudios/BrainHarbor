## The short version {#short-version}

!%dexamethasone%A steroid takes down the swelling that a tumor causes in the
brain around it. It is not chemotherapy, and for most brain tumors it does
nothing to the tumor itself. That is why you can feel much better within a day
or two while the tumor is
exactly as it was. Most people improve on it. Not everybody does. The trade is
that a steroid has strong effects of its own: hunger, broken sleep, changes in
mood, and weakness in the big muscles of the thighs and upper arms. Those can
look like the tumor getting worse when they are not, and your team can often do
something about them. **Do not stop a steroid on your own.** It is brought down
on a plan, and the plan is worth having in writing.

## What is it, and what is it doing? {#what-it-is}

It is a medicine that brings down swelling inside your head.

The brain around a tumor gets waterlogged. Doctors call that swelling edema. It
takes up room in a skull that cannot stretch, and a lot of what you feel comes
from that rather than from the tumor itself: headaches, feeling sick, and
whatever that part of the brain does being done worse.

A steroid dries that swelling out. The commonest one used for this is
dexamethasone. Some teams use a different one. They work the same way.

These are not the steroids that athletes get into trouble for. Those are built
to add muscle. This kind copies a hormone your own body makes every day, and at
the dose used here it works against swelling.

**For most brain tumors the steroid works on the swelling, not on the tumor.**
So the relief is real and the tumor has not changed. People find that confusing,
and some take it as bad news. It is not. The swelling is causing symptoms, so
treating the swelling treats symptoms.

**There is one exception, and it matters before any sample is taken.** For
lymphoma in the brain, a steroid can shrink the tumor itself, and quickly. That
sounds like good luck and it can cost you the diagnosis, because what is left
may be too changed to name under a microscope. This is why a team may want the
sample first if they can safely wait. **If you have already been started on a
steroid, say so and do not stop it yourself.** It happens: a steroid is
sometimes started in an emergency room, days before anyone says the word
lymphoma. It is a known situation, and it is your team's to work around.
[CNS lymphoma](/tumors/cns-lymphoma) covers that. For every other tumor on this
site, the steroid is working on the swelling and not on the tumor.

## Why am I taking one? {#why}

Because something is swelling, or is about to.

- **The scan showed swelling around the tumor.** This is the usual reason, and
  the steroid is aimed at that.
- **You are having an operation.** A steroid is often given before and for a few
  days after. [Brain surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) covers those days.
- **You are having radiation.** Treatment can cause swelling of its own.
  [Radiation therapy](/treatments/radiation-therapy) covers that stretch.
- **Something happened that your team thinks is swelling.** A headache that is
  building, or an old symptom coming back.

**If you are not on one, that is a decision too.** Guidelines say a steroid is
for people with symptoms, and there is no good evidence for giving one to
somebody whose tumor is quiet. Being left off it is not being left out.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

A course of steroids has a shape, and knowing the shape makes the changes make
sense.

1. **You start on a dose that is higher than you will stay on.** The point is to
   get the swelling down.
2. **You notice a change quickly.** Most people start to feel better within
   hours, and the full effect is usually there in a day to three days. **Not
   everybody improves**, and the studies that counted it disagree about how many
   do, so nobody can promise you will. If it is not helping you, say so rather
   than waiting it out.
3. **The dose comes down to the smallest one that holds the symptoms off.**
   That is the aim of the whole thing: the lowest dose that works, for the
   shortest time.
4. **The dose may go up again for a while.** During radiation especially. That
   is expected, and it is not a setback.
5. **When the reason for it has gone, you come off it in steps.** That is called
   a taper. Your team writes the steps down for you.
6. **You are watched while it comes down.** If old symptoms start to return as
   the dose drops, that is something to report, not something to push through.

Nobody agrees on the exact doses or the exact taper. There are no standard
rules for it, which is why the plans differ between hospitals and why yours has
to come from your own team.

## How long will I be on it? {#how-long}

**It depends entirely on why you are on it, and the honest range is days to
months.**

After an operation it can be a few days. Around radiation it is usually weeks.
Some people with ongoing swelling stay on a low dose for months. Many people
come off it once their treatment is over.

Coming off is done in stages, and a few weeks is common for that part. The
longer you have been on it, the slower the last part tends to go.

So the useful question is not how long in general. It is: what is my plan, and
what would change it?

## Why can I not just stop taking it? {#why-taper}

**Because your body stops making its own while you are taking this one.**

You make a steroid hormone of your own, every day, and you need it. When a
higher dose arrives from outside, the part of you that makes it goes quiet.
Take that outside supply away all at once and there is nothing to step in, and
that can make you very ill.

So the dose is walked down in steps instead, which gives your body time to start
again. The other reason for going in steps is simpler: the swelling can come
back, and coming down slowly lets your team catch that while it is small.

Two things follow from this, and they are the practical heart of the page.

**Do not stop, skip or halve a dose on your own, and do not let the bottle run
out.** If a side effect is bad enough that you want to stop, that is a call to
your team, and it is a reasonable call to make.

**Stopping is their decision, and it is not always slow.** After a short course,
a few days for example, a team may simply stop it, and that is fine because your
own supply never had time to shut down. The rule is not "always slowly". The
rule is "not on your own".

**And tell anybody treating you that you are on a steroid, or recently came off
one.** If you get seriously ill or need an operation, the dose may have to go up
for a few days to cover it. That is a known thing with a known answer, and it
only works if the person in front of you knows.

## What does it feel like? {#what-it-feels-like}

The first few days are often the best you have felt since this started. The
headache eases. You can think again. People say they feel unusually well, and
that feeling is part of what the drug does.

Then the rest of it arrives. You are hungry in a way that is hard to explain to
anyone. You are awake at three in the morning with your brain running. Your
face changes shape, rounder and puffier than it was, and you notice it in
photographs before anyone says anything.

None of this means anything has gone wrong. These are the known effects of the
medicine, they are stronger on a higher dose and after longer on it, and the
hunger, the sleep and the puffiness all settle again after you stop.

## Is it the tumor, or is it the steroid? {#tumor-or-steroid}

This is the question this page exists for.

A steroid causes several things that look exactly like a tumor getting worse.
The big one is muscle weakness.

### The weakness in your legs and arms {#muscle-weakness}

**It shows up as trouble getting out of a low chair, climbing stairs, or lifting
your arms above your head.** It affects the big muscles closest to the body, the
thighs, hips, shoulders and upper arms, and it usually comes on over weeks
rather than all at once. Doctors have a name for it: proximal myopathy. Your
team will test your strength, your feeling and your reflexes, and what they find
is part of how they tell the two apart.

It is one of the most common problems with a long course, and how often it
happens is genuinely unsettled: studies have counted it in different doses over
different lengths of time and come back with very different answers, so there is
no honest number to give you. What they all agree on is the direction.
**It gets more likely the higher the dose and the longer you are on it**, and it
is unusual in the first days.

Now the part that makes it hard. Weakness in somebody with a brain tumor can
come from the tumor or the swelling. It can also come from the steroid treating
that swelling. The two need opposite things: more steroid, or less. That is why
this is not something to work out at home, and it is also why changing your own
dose is the one thing not to do.

**What to do is simple.** Tell your team what you have noticed and when it
started. Say which movements are harder, because the pattern is a large part of
how they tell the difference. It is a phone call, not a wait for the next
appointment.

And it does get better. When the dose comes down, the strength comes back,
though slowly: weeks to months rather than days. Ask about seeing a physical
therapist, and ask what movement is safe for you now. Keeping active is thought
to help protect the muscles, and a therapist can tell you what is worth doing
and what is not, which matters if you are already unsteady.

### The other things that get blamed on the tumor {#not-the-tumor}

- **Mood and temper.** A steroid can make you restless, irritable, anxious or
  low, and it can swing you between them. Families read this as the person
  changing. It usually settles when the dose comes down.
- **Not sleeping.** Broken sleep is one of the commonest effects of this drug,
  so it is worth knowing it is not all worry.
- **Feeling flat when it is reduced.** Coming down can bring aches, tiredness
  and low mood of its own.
- **Being confused, or not yourself.** Steroids can do this at higher doses. It
  is one to report the same day rather than to watch.

Seizure medicines cause their own version of this, and the two are often given
together. [Brain surgery](/treatments/craniotomy#tumor-or-medicine) goes through
that pair.

None of this is a reason to stop a medicine, and all of it is a reason to tell
somebody. A dose can come down. A medicine can be traded for a different one.
Neither happens while nobody knows.

## The rest of the side effects {#side-effects}

Which of these you get varies from person to person, and they are stronger on a
higher dose or after a while on it. **Most fade once the steroid stops. The
changes to skin and bone are the ones that may not**, which is part of why your
team keeps trying to get the dose down.

**In the first days and weeks:**

- **Hunger, and weight going on.** Sweet things especially. Your appetite goes
  back to normal afterwards.
- **Trouble sleeping.** Taking the dose earlier in the day helps, and your team
  can tell you how early.
- **Indigestion.** Take it with food or a milky drink. You may be given a
  second medicine to protect the stomach.
- **Higher blood sugar.** Being very thirsty and needing to pee a lot are the
  signs. It can be checked with a simple test. If you already have diabetes,
  expect to check your levels more often and to have your other medicines
  adjusted.
- **A puffy face, ankles or feet.**
- **Mouth infections.** A sore mouth, or creamy white patches on the tongue.
  Treatable, usually in a few days. Brushing and rinsing regularly helps.
- **Infections generally, and less of a fever to warn you.** A steroid quiets
  the immune system and can hide a temperature, so any fever at all is worth
  reporting even if you feel fine otherwise.
- **Hiccups that will not stop.** An odd one, and it is a known effect of the
  dose.

**With a longer course:**

- **The muscle weakness above.**
- **Thin skin, easy bruising, stretch marks and acne.**
- **Weaker bones.** Which is why a fall matters more than it used to, and why
  new hip pain is worth mentioning.
- **A higher chance of chest infections**, including an unusual pneumonia that
  some teams give a preventive antibiotic against.

Your team weighs all of this against what the swelling is doing. That is the
whole reason they keep trying to get the dose down.

## Why does my team ask about my steroid dose before a scan? {#steroids-and-scans}

Because the steroid changes what the scan shows.

It tightens up the leaky blood vessels the contrast dye follows, so a scan taken
after the dose has changed can look different even when the tumor has not moved.
The rules doctors use to judge whether a tumor is growing take your steroid dose
into account, which is why they ask, and why a dose change just before a scan is
worth mentioning without being asked.
[Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers how one scan is compared with
the last.

## When to call, and what about {#when-to-call}

This list is about the steroid. It does not replace the list your own team gives
you for your tumor, and [get help now](/get-help-now) has the numbers to keep on
a phone.

**Call your team the same day if:**

- You cannot keep your pills down. A steroid you cannot take is not a small
  problem, and there are other ways to give it.
- A headache keeps building, or an old symptom comes back. This matters at any
  point, and most of all while the dose is coming down.
- You feel faint, dizzy standing up, sick, or wrung out after a dose was missed
  or lowered. That is the shortage this page describes, and it is treatable.
- You have a fever, even with nothing else wrong. **If you are also having
  chemotherapy, a fever is a call right away, at any hour, and not a wait for
  the morning.** [The fever rule](/treatments/chemotherapy#fever-rule) explains
  why.
- You are being sick again and again.
- You are confused, or somebody says you are not making sense.
- There is new weakness in an arm or a leg, including a chair or the stairs
  becoming hard when they were not.
- There is new trouble speaking, or with your vision, or you are sleeping much
  more than you are awake.
- There is blood in your stool, or you have bad stomach pain. A steroid can take
  the edge off the warning signs, so pain that would normally send you to a
  doctor still counts even if it feels less than it should.
- You stumble or fall. Your bones are more easily hurt on a steroid, so a fall
  is not something to shrug off.
- You have been near somebody with chickenpox, shingles or measles. There is
  treatment for that, and it works best early.

**Call an ambulance for chest pain or trouble breathing.** Call 911, or your
local emergency number. The same goes for suddenly not being able to speak, move
one side, or see, or for somebody who cannot be woken.
[What to do in a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) draws the line for seizures.

**Tell your team about these as well:**

- Being very thirsty and needing to pee a lot.
- Gaining more than five pounds in a week.
- A rash, or sores in your mouth.
- A cough that will not go, or stinging when you pee.
- Any wound or cut that is slow to heal.

If you are not sure which of these it is, phone. That call is a smaller thing
than any of the problems on this page.

## What you can do {#what-you-can-do}

- **Get the plan in writing.** The dose now, the steps down, and the dates. Ask
  for a new copy every time it changes.
- **Do not run out.** Order the next prescription before you are near the end of
  this one. Stopping because the bottle is empty is still stopping.
- **Take it with food, and earlier in the day**, unless you were told otherwise.
- **Keep a short diary.** The date, the dose, how you felt, anything new. One
  hospital's steroid booklet is laid out as exactly that, for a good reason:
  dose decisions get made on what you noticed last week.
- **Tell anyone else who treats you.** Your family doctor, your dentist, the
  pharmacist, anyone in an emergency room. Carry a note of the dose.
- **Keep moving if you safely can**, and ask about a physical therapist before
  the stairs become the problem.
- **Say if you feel unlike yourself.** That is information, not complaining.

## For the person caring for someone on steroids {#caregiver}

[CAREGIVER]

Three things that are specific to this medicine.

**The changes you are seeing may be the drug.** The short temper, the hunger,
the three-in-the-morning energy, the struggle to get out of a chair. It is fair
to say all of it out loud at the appointment, in front of them, as something the
team may be able to fix.

**The dose changes hands.** It is started by one doctor, changed by another, and
often prescribed by a third. If a change is made over the phone, write down who
said it, so the record they are all working from is the same one.

**Two on that list you are likelier to spot than they are**: not being able to
keep the pills down, and old symptoms creeping back as the dose drops. Somebody
feeling rough often puts both down to the day they are having. Both are usually
sorted out when a team hears about them early.

## Why has my dose gone up again? {#dose-up}

**Usually because there is more swelling to deal with, not because the tumor has
won.**

The commonest time for it is during radiation, when the treated area can swell.
It is common enough that it is planned for. A headache is the usual reason a
dose gets put back up.

It can also happen if a taper was tried and the symptoms came back. That is
information, not failure: it tells your team the swelling still needs treating,
and they will try again later.

What matters is that the increase is a decision somebody made and wrote down,
with a plan to come back down again. Ask for that plan the same way you asked
the first time.

## Who is in charge of my steroid? {#who-is-in-charge}

**Ask, because the answer is often less obvious than it looks.**

A surgeon may start it, an oncologist may change it during treatment, and your
family doctor may end up writing the prescriptions. Many teams have a nurse who
handles the day-to-day of it, and that is usually the best number to have.

This is worth being blunt about. In one audit at a UK center, over three months,
most of the people started on a steroid had nothing written down about how they
would come off it at that point. The same audit found more problems in the people whose course ran
past a couple of weeks, and in the people with no written plan at all.

So the two questions that protect you are: who owns this medicine, and where is
my plan written down? Neither is a difficult thing to ask, and both are the
kind of thing that falls between people when nobody asks.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Why am I on this, and what is it treating?
- How long do you expect me to be on it?
- What is the plan for coming off, and can I have it in writing?
- Who do I call about the steroid, and who changes the dose?
- What should I watch for that would mean the dose needs changing?
- Which of the things I am feeling are the steroid rather than the tumor?
- Should my blood sugar be checked?
- Do I need something for my stomach?
- Is there anything I should be doing to keep my strength up?
- Can I see a physical therapist?
- What do I do if I am sick and cannot keep it down?
- What do I do if I miss a dose?
- Does the steroid interact with my other medicines?
- Is there anything else that would let me take less of it?
