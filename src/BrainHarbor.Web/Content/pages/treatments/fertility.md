## The short version {#short-version}

Some brain tumor treatments can make it harder, or impossible, to have children
later. **There are ways to save eggs or sperm first, and most of them work best
before treatment starts.** So if having children one day might matter to you,
say so now, even if you are not sure. You can ask to see a fertility specialist.
Saving sperm can take a day or two, and saving eggs takes about 2 to 3 weeks.
If treatment cannot wait that long, there may still be a faster choice.

## What does treatment have to do with having children? {#what-it-is}

Some treatments can harm eggs or sperm, or the signals that tell your body to
make them.

That can be for a while, or for good. How likely it is depends on the
treatment, how much of it you have, how long it goes on, and how old you are.

Saving eggs, sperm or tissue before treatment, so you can try to use them
later, has a name. It is called fertility preservation. A doctor who does it is
called a fertility specialist. You may also hear "reproductive endocrinologist".

## Why ask now, before treatment starts? {#why-now}

**Because many choices are lost once chemotherapy or radiation begins.** The
American Society of Clinical Oncology, a large group of cancer doctors, says
your team should talk with you about this as early as possible. That means
before treatment starts, while you still have every choice.

It also says anyone who wants to save eggs or sperm should be sent to a
fertility specialist. So should anyone who is not sure. You do not have to have
made up your mind to ask.

You may have to bring it up yourself. In one US study of young adults with a
brain tumor, most were never sent to a fertility specialist. Of the ones who
were sent, most went on to save eggs or sperm.

Talking it through can help even if you decide to save nothing. The National
Cancer Institute says research found that people who met a fertility specialist
had fewer regrets later. That was true whether or not they went on to save
anything.

## Which treatments can affect it? {#which-treatments}

**Chemotherapy.** Some chemotherapy drugs can harm eggs and sperm. Lomustine is
on the American Cancer Society's list of drugs most likely to do this, and so
are carmustine and procarbazine. Temozolomide is in the same family of drugs.
Its label says it may affect a man's fertility, and it says nothing either way
about eggs. The National Brain Tumor Society says drugs in this family are the
most harmful to eggs. So if you have ovaries, ask about temozolomide too.
[Chemotherapy](/treatments/chemotherapy) goes through these drugs one at a time.

**Radiation to the brain.** Two small parts of the brain, the pituitary gland
and the hypothalamus, send the signals that tell the ovaries and testicles to
work. Radiation that reaches them can lower those signals. Whether it does
depends on where the radiation is aimed and how much is given. The National
Cancer Institute says proton therapy may affect fertility less than standard
radiation.

**Radiation to the spine.** Some tumors need radiation along the spine as well
as the brain. That radiation can reach the ovaries. Ask your team whether
anything can protect them.

**Surgery.** An operation can affect fertility when the tumor is near the
pituitary gland or the hypothalamus. Surgery also matters for timing, because
the weeks after it can be the time to save eggs or sperm.

**Other treatments.** The effect of drugs being tested in a clinical trial is
not always known. Ask about each one you are offered.

Your team can tell you how likely your own plan is to affect this. Ask them
before treatment starts.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

1. **Tell your team that having children one day might matter to you.** Do this
   as early as you can. Not being sure is a good enough reason.
2. **Ask to see a fertility specialist.** Fertility clinics can often see people
   with cancer within a few days.
3. **Talk through your choices.** The specialist goes through what fits your
   age, your body, your treatment, and how much time there is.
4. **Your cancer team and the specialist agree on timing.** They decide together
   whether there is time, and how it fits around surgery, radiation and
   chemotherapy.
5. **Anything you save is frozen and stored** until you want to use it.
6. **Treatment starts.**

## The choices, if you make sperm {#sperm}

**Saving sperm is the main choice, and it can be quick.** You give a sample by
masturbating, usually in a private room at the clinic or sperm bank. Some
places let you collect it at home and bring it in. Once frozen, sperm can be
stored for decades.

The National Brain Tumor Society says no medicine or preparation is needed, so
you can decide one day and give a sample the next. Doctors prefer more than one
sample if there is time, but any sample saved gives you a chance later.

**Give the sample before treatment starts.** Sperm may be harmed after even one
treatment.

If you cannot give a sample, sperm can sometimes be taken straight from the
testicle in a small operation.

A shot of hormones to "rest" the testicles is not a way to save fertility.
Cancer doctors say it does not work.

## The choices, if you have ovaries {#eggs}

**Freezing eggs.** You take hormone shots for a week or two so your ovaries
ripen several eggs at once. Then a doctor collects them with a thin needle
passed through the vagina, guided by an ultrasound. The eggs are frozen. The
whole process takes about 2 to 3 weeks.

**Freezing embryos.** This starts the same way. The eggs are then mixed with
sperm from a partner or a donor, and the embryos that form are frozen.

**Freezing ovarian tissue.** A surgeon takes out an ovary, or part of one,
often through small cuts in the belly. The tissue is frozen. **It needs no
hormone shots, so it can be done right away**, which matters if treatment
cannot wait. Cancer doctors now call this an established method.

**Moving an ovary out of the way.** If radiation is aimed near the ovaries, a
surgeon can sometimes move one out of its path. It does not always work.

**A shot that rests the ovaries during chemotherapy.** You may be offered a
hormone shot that quiets the ovaries during treatment. Cancer doctors say it
should not be used in place of freezing eggs, embryos or tissue. Outside
breast cancer, it is not yet known how well it works, or how safe it is.

## Children and teens {#children}

**Bring a child or teen into this conversation once they can understand it.**
A parent or guardian gives permission. The child should agree too.

**Boys.** By about age 12, boys usually make enough sperm to freeze. For boys who
have not gone through puberty, there is not yet a proven way. Freezing tissue
from the testicle is being tried in research studies. It is often done during an
operation the child is already having.

**Girls.** Ask the team whether her treatment puts her at high risk. Every girl
who has not gone through puberty should be checked for this. Many are not at
high risk, and do not need eggs or tissue saved. For a girl at high risk, freezing
ovarian tissue may be a choice, and it can be done before puberty.

## How long does it take? {#how-long}

- **Saving sperm:** a day or two. You can decide one day and give a sample the
  next.
- **Freezing eggs or embryos:** about 2 to 3 weeks, most of it hormone shots.
- **Freezing ovarian tissue:** no wait for shots. It is an operation, so it is
  done when a surgeon can fit it in.

## Will it hold up my treatment? {#delay}

**Sometimes a little, and your team decides whether that is safe.** For many
people with a brain tumor, there are a few weeks between surgery and the start
of radiation or chemotherapy. The National Brain Tumor Society says that gap can
leave time to freeze eggs or embryos. If surgery is not an emergency, saving
sperm may be possible before it. If not, it can be done in that gap.

In that same small US study, both of the women who went ahead with it had
their treatment held up a little.

If treatment cannot wait, say so to the fertility specialist. Freezing ovarian
tissue needs no hormone shots, and saving sperm is quick. There may still be
something you can do.

## What if my tumor is sensitive to hormones? {#hormones}

Egg freezing uses hormone shots. **If you have a meningioma, nobody yet knows
whether those hormones are safe for you.** There is too little research to
say. Some meningiomas grow in response to hormones. Ask your neurosurgeon and the
fertility specialist to talk to each other before you start any hormone
treatment. [Meningioma](/tumors/meningioma) has more on hormones and this tumor.

Freezing ovarian tissue does not use hormone shots, so ask about it too.

## What is hard about it, and what can be done {#hard}

**It comes at the worst time.** You are being asked about a future family in
the same week you heard about a tumor. That is a lot. You do not have to decide
everything at the first meeting. You can ask the specialist to tell you how
long you have to decide.

**It can cost a lot, and insurance may not pay.** The American Cancer Society
says, as of 2025, that freezing eggs, embryos or ovarian tissue can cost
$10,000 or more, plus a yearly fee to store them. It says saving sperm costs
about $500 to $1,000, plus about $200 to $500 a year for storage.

What can help:

- **Some US states require some insurance plans to cover it** when treatment
  may harm fertility. The [Alliance for Fertility
  Preservation](https://www.allianceforfertilitypreservation.org/expenses/laws-legislation/)
  keeps a list of the laws.
- **Ask about money help.** [Livestrong
  Fertility](https://livestrong.org/how-we-help/livestrong-fertility/) has a
  discount program with clinics, and free hormone medicine for people who
  qualify. The Alliance for Fertility Preservation lists [other
  programs](https://www.allianceforfertilitypreservation.org/expenses/financial-assistance-programs/).
- **Ask the clinic what it will cost before you start**, including storage.

**Nothing is certain.** No method works every time. Saving eggs or sperm gives
you a chance later, not a promise. Ask the specialist what your own chances
look like.

## Getting pregnant during treatment {#pregnancy}

**Do not get pregnant during treatment, and do not get a partner pregnant.**
Many cancer treatments can harm a baby or cause a miscarriage.

- **You can get pregnant even when your periods have stopped.** Use birth
  control either way.
- **Temozolomide's label says that women should use birth control during
  treatment and for 6 months after the last dose.** For men, it says to use
  condoms during treatment and for 3 months after, and not to donate sperm in
  that time.
- **Ask about each drug you take.** The time differs from drug to drug, and
  some drugs can stop some kinds of birth control from working.
- **If you take a seizure medicine and might get pregnant, tell your team.**
  Some seizure medicines are safer in pregnancy than others. Do not change or
  stop one on your own. [Seizure medicines](/treatments/anti-seizure-medicines)
  goes through them.

## After treatment: can I find out? {#after}

**Yes. Fertility can be checked after treatment.** For men, a sperm test counts
the sperm. For women, a blood test can show roughly how many eggs are left.

Some people's fertility comes back after treatment, and for others it does
not. For sperm, coming back can take years. Periods coming back does not prove
you can get pregnant.

If brain radiation lowered the signals from the pituitary gland, hormone
treatment can help fix this, as long as the ovaries or testicles were not
damaged too. It does not always solve it.

Some people can still freeze eggs after treatment. Many doctors suggest waiting
a while after treatment before trying for a baby. Ask how long, and why.

## Who does it, and how do I find out how it went? {#who}

A fertility specialist does it, at a fertility clinic or a sperm bank. Your
cancer team refers you, or you can ask for the referral yourself.

The sperm bank tests each sample to see how many sperm it holds. The clinic
tells you how many eggs or embryos were frozen. Ask them to explain what that
number means for you, and how you will hear.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Could my treatment affect whether I can have children?
- How likely is that, for my plan?
- Can I see a fertility specialist before treatment starts?
- How much time do I have before treatment has to start?
- Is there time between surgery and the next treatment to save eggs or sperm?
- Is proton therapy an option for me, and would it help with this?
- If I have egg freezing after brain surgery, is there anything to watch for?
- Is my tumor sensitive to hormones?
- Which kind of birth control is safe with my medicines, and for how long?
- Is my seizure medicine one of the safer ones if I get pregnant?
- How will we check my fertility after treatment?
- How long should I wait before trying for a baby?
