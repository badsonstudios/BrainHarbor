## The short version {#short-version}

Proton therapy is radiation given with a different kind of particle. A proton
beam stops at the tumor instead of carrying on through, so less of it reaches
the tissue behind. That is the whole of the difference. Protons are not stronger
and they are not aimed at a different goal. Being in the room is much the same
as ordinary radiation. The hard part is usually not the treatment. It is how far
away the nearest center is, and what the insurance says.

## What is proton therapy? {#what-it-is}

It is radiation therapy given with protons instead of x-rays.

Most radiation for a brain tumor uses x-ray beams, which are also called photon
beams. A photon beam travels through your head. It gives its energy on the way
in, at the tumor, and on the way out the other side.

A proton is a much heavier particle than an x-ray is made of. A proton beam gives
up most of its energy at a set depth and then stops. Your team chooses that depth. So the
tissue behind the tumor gets very little.

That is the reason anyone would use it. It is not a stronger beam and it is not
a different treatment. It is the same job, with less of the beam landing on
parts of the brain nobody wanted to treat.

The machines have different names too. Ordinary radiation comes from a linear
accelerator. Protons come from a machine called a cyclotron or a synchrotron.
Those machines are large and they cost a great deal, which is most of why there
are so few of them.

## Would it be better for me? {#is-it-better}

The honest answer is that it depends on your tumor, and for many brain tumors
nobody knows yet.

**Where the case is strongest.** Protons suit a tumor with a clear edge, sitting
next to something you cannot afford to damage. The American Cancer Society names
chordoma as that kind of tumor, and says protons may help more when a tumor is
near vital structures. The American Cancer Society also lists certain childhood cancers among the ones
it is used for.

**Where it is not settled.** The same page says it is not clear whether protons
are as useful for tumors that grow into the brain around them. It names the
astrocytoma and the glioblastoma. Those are most of the tumors this site is
about. European guidelines say much the same thing. Protons are worth thinking
about when a tumor sits close to a part of the brain at risk. But trials are
still needed before anyone can say how they compare.

**So the word to be careful with is "better".** Protons are better at missing
things. Whether missing those things leaves you better off, years later, has not
been settled for most brain tumors. A center that offers it should be able to
say what they expect it to spare in your case.

**That is a fair thing to ask straight out.** Ask your radiation oncologist
whether proton therapy is an option for you, and why or why not. A no with a
reason is useful. A no you never asked for is not.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

The same steps as ordinary radiation to the head, with two differences.

1. You meet the radiation oncologist and go through your scans.
2. You have a planning scan, a CT or an MRI, lying in the position you will be
   treated in. A mask is made to hold your head still.
3. The team builds your plan and checks it. That is a gap of a few days with
   nothing happening, and it worries people.
4. You come in on weekdays for several weeks. Pictures are taken each time to
   check your position, then the beam is given.
5. Someone from the team goes through how you are doing as the weeks pass.

[Radiation therapy: the mask, the daily visits, and what comes
after](/treatments/radiation-therapy) walks through every one of those steps in
detail, including the planning visit and the mask. Everything about the weeks of
treatment is the same.

**The first difference is the room.** The machine is a different one, and it is
much bigger. Depending on where your tumor is you may sit in a chair rather than
lie on a table.

**The second is the paperwork**, and it happens before any of the rest. Your
plan usually has to be approved by your insurance first. That is the part of
this treatment with its own section further down.

## How long does it take? {#how-long}

About the same as ordinary radiation, once it starts.

- **Each visit:** usually about a quarter of an hour to half an hour. Most of
  that is getting you into exactly the right position.
- **The whole course:** weekdays, over several weeks. How many weeks depends on
  what is being treated.
- **Before any of it:** the planning scan, then a few days while the plan is
  built, then however long the insurance decision takes. Ask for that last one
  as a separate answer. It is the one nobody quotes you.

## Is it the same to be in? {#what-it-feels-like}

Yes, near enough.

The beam itself does not hurt. You do not see it or feel it. You lie or sit
still, you are alone in the room while it runs, and somebody is watching and
listening the whole time.

Tiredness turns up during the weeks and can carry on afterward, the same as with
ordinary radiation. The skin in the treated area can go red, dry, itchy or sore,
and hair goes where the beam goes.

The rest of what a course of radiation is like, week by week, is on
[the radiation therapy page](/treatments/radiation-therapy#side-effects). It is
written for x-ray beams, and the weeks are the same here. **What should make you
call your team the same day, and what is an ambulance, is on that page too.**

**What protons are trying to buy you is fewer late effects**, not an easier six
weeks. That is worth knowing before you rearrange your life for it.

## Where are the centers, and what would getting there mean? {#access}

This is the part that decides it, and it is rarely the part anybody is told
about early.

**There are not many of them.** The American Cancer Society says there are a
limited number of proton centers in the United States, and that more are being
built. No number is printed here, because the ones in circulation come from
directories rather than from a study, and they go out of date. Ask your team
where the nearest one is. That is the answer you actually need.

**So people travel, and the travel is not small.** One large cancer center
compared everybody it treated with radiation over six years. The people who had
proton therapy had come, on average, from well over twice as far away as the
people who had ordinary radiation.

That turns into weeks away from home, because the treatment runs on weekdays.
Work the real cost out loud, with somebody, before you decide:

- Somewhere to stay for several weeks, near the center.
- Time off work, for you and possibly for the person going with you.
- Who is looking after children, or a parent, or a dog.
- Getting to the center every weekday once you are there.

**None of that is a reason to rule it out by yourself.** Ask the center what
they have. Lodging schemes, discounted rates with nearby hotels and a social
worker whose job is exactly this are all things centers can have. Ask before you
decide it is impossible, because it is the kind of help that is not advertised.

**And ask even if nobody has offered.** Studies have looked at who ends up
getting proton therapy. The tumor is not the only thing that decides it. In that
same center's figures, people living farther away were more likely to get it,
not less. That sounds backwards next to the paragraph above, and it is the
point: what stops people is not being sent, rather than the distance itself. The
same studies keep finding that Black patients are less likely to receive it than
white patients with similar cancers. So ask directly. Do not wait to be
offered.

## What if my insurance says no? {#insurance}

A no is not a verdict on your case, and it is not the end of the road. There is a
route out of it, it is written into law, and this section is what it looks like.

Proton therapy costs more than ordinary radiation. That is why plans look at it
harder. One review found that radiation oncology has the heaviest approval burden of any
medical specialty, and that proton plans are among the most likely of all to be
flagged.

**There are two rounds, and the second one is not your insurer.** First you
appeal to the plan itself. If the plan says no again, the law gives you an
outside review by an independent organization. In some plans that is federal
law and in others it is your state's, and your center will know which.
Those reviewers work for neither your hospital nor your plan, **and their
decision is binding on the plan.** The review costs you nothing. The plan pays
for it.

**The outside review is worth using, and it often is not used.** The researchers
who studied it say centers skip it, and give four reasons: nobody there knows
the route, nobody knows the law, there is no process for it, or there is nobody
to do the paperwork. In their study of the published records from three states,
California, New York and Washington, a little over four in every ten proton
requests were decided in the patient's favor. Brain and spinal cord tumors in
that set came out about the same. That is one study of three states and it is
not a forecast about your plan. It is a reason not to stop at the first letter.

**Four things make an appeal stronger**, from the same study:

- It points to the published guidelines for your tumor.
- It names the studies behind the request.
- It says whether you would be eligible for a trial.
- It includes a letter about you, not a form letter.

Your center writes these. Ask who does it there, and ask to read what they send.

**Ask for it to be treated as urgent.** Plans have to answer an urgent request
far faster than a routine one, and the researchers say cancer cases are exactly
the ones that should be marked that way. In their figures the difference was the
best part of a month. Ask whether yours can be marked urgent, and ask at the
first appointment rather than after a refusal.

**And ask the question that matters more than any of this: would waiting hold up
my treatment?** Sometimes the answer is no, and your radiation oncologist will say there is room
to argue. Sometimes it is yes, and starting ordinary radiation on schedule is
the better plan. Your
radiation oncologist can tell you which, and it is the one question on this page
you should not leave the room without.

**If you are on Medicare, the picture is different.** Medicare does not require
this kind of approval before radiation. Medicare Advantage plans are run by
insurers and often do. Check which one you have, because people use the word
Medicare for both.

## What you need first {#what-you-need-first}

Before a proton center can start, expect:

- **The same scans as for ordinary radiation.** An MRI with dye, and a planning
  CT in your mask. [Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) and [your CT scan](/tests/ct-scan)
  cover those appointments.
- **Your pathology and gene results**, because they are part of what the plan is
  argued from. [The words on your gene results](/tests/molecular-markers) takes
  them one at a time.
- **The insurance paperwork itself**, which is the slowest part. Ask who at the
  center handles it and how you will hear.
- **A plan for where you would stay**, if the center is far away. Ask about that
  at the first appointment, not after approval comes through.

Everything else on
[what to bring and arrange before radiation
starts](/treatments/radiation-therapy#what-you-need-first) applies here too,
including the dental work, the beard and the questions about having children.

## For the person going with them {#caregiver}

[CAREGIVER]

Proton therapy asks something of you that ordinary radiation usually does not,
and it is worth saying out loud early.

**This one may take them away from home.** If the center is not local, somebody
is living somewhere else for several weeks. Decide together, early, whether that
is you, or someone else, or in shifts. Trying to work it out in the first week is
how people end up doing it alone.

**Take the paperwork off them if you can.** There is a lot of it, and it arrives
in the same weeks as the diagnosis. Ask the center who handles the insurance side
and get that person's name. Ask to be copied in. Keep every letter, including the
refusals, because an appeal is built out of them.

**Do not let a refusal letter be the last word in the house.** It reads like a
verdict and it is not one. There is an appeal, and then an outside review by
people who work for neither side. The section above says what makes one stronger.
Ask the center's team whether they are doing it.

**Ask about help with the costs of being there**, not just the treatment. Lodging
schemes and travel help exist at many centers and are rarely offered without
being asked for. It is a good job for the person who is not the patient.

**The rest of it is the same as any course of radiation.** The driving, the
tiredness that builds and does not stop on the last day, the skin rules.
[The radiation therapy page](/treatments/radiation-therapy#caregiver) has all of
that, and every word of it is true here.

## Who decides, and how will I hear? {#who-decides}

There are two decisions, and they are made by different people.

**Whether protons would suit your tumor** is your radiation oncologist's call,
usually with the rest of your team. That conversation can happen at your first
radiation appointment, and you can start it.

**Whether it gets paid for** is your insurer's, and then, if they refuse, an
outside reviewer's. You are not in that room either time.

Ask how you will be told, and when. Ask whether somebody calls you or whether it
appears in your records first. And ask what happens to your start date while the
answer is being waited for. All three have answers, and none of them is a
difficult question to ask.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Is proton therapy an option for me, and why or why not?
- What would it spare, in my case, that ordinary radiation would not?
- Is it known to be better for my kind of tumor, or is that still being worked
  out?
- Where is the nearest center, and would you refer me?
- How long would I be away from home, and does the center help with somewhere to
  stay?
- Who handles the insurance request, and can I see what you send?
- Will you have to build an ordinary radiation plan alongside it to compare, and
  does that add time?
- Can the request be marked urgent?
- If it is refused, will you take it to an outside review?
- Would waiting for a decision hold up the start of my treatment?
- If it is refused in the end, what is the plan instead, and is it a good one?
