## The short version {#short-version}

!%stereotactic radiosurgery%
Nothing is cut. Stereotactic radiosurgery is radiation, not an operation, and
the name is the most misleading thing about it. A lot of faint beams are aimed
at your head from different angles, and they cross at one small spot. Only where
they cross is the dose large enough to matter. You are awake the whole time
and you feel nothing while it runs. It is usually one day rather than weeks of
visits, and you usually go home the same evening. The longest part of the day is
the wait while your plan is built, and this page spends time on it because most
others do not.

## What is it? {#what-it-is}

It is a way of giving a large dose of radiation to one small target in a single
visit, or in a small number of visits.

Each beam on its own is weak. On its own it would do very little to anything it
passes through. The beams come in from many different directions and cross at
the tumor, and only where they cross is the dose large. That is how the tissue
around the target is spared.

**There is no knife and no cutting.** The American Cancer Society says it
plainly, and so does Cleveland Clinic: despite the name, this is not surgery.
There is no incision, no general anesthetic, and nothing is taken out. The name
is about how exactly the beams can be aimed. Cleveland Clinic puts it as being
as accurate as a surgical knife, without cutting into your body. That is where
the word comes from, and it is a poor word for what happens to you.

**You will hear machine names, and they are doing the same job.** A Gamma Knife
aims a couple of hundred thin beams at the target from fixed positions, all at
once. A CyberKnife, or a linear accelerator set up for this, uses one beam and
moves around your head to come at the target from many angles. Which one your
center has is a fact about your center.

**Two sets of initials turn up on letters.** SRS means the whole dose in one
visit. SRT, sometimes written as fractionated radiosurgery, means the same idea
split over a few visits. If your letter says one of these, that is what it is
telling you. Ask how many visits yours is, and why that number. It
depends on what is being treated and where it sits.

## Why this and not an operation, or weeks of radiation? {#why-this}

Because of what it can reach, and what it can be aimed at.

**It suits a small target with an edge that can be drawn, and it takes nothing
out, so there is no tissue for the laboratory.** The team has to be able to say
exactly where the treated area stops, and nothing comes back from it that a
pathologist can look at, so it does not answer a question about what the tumor
is. The size of what is being treated is one
of the things that decides whether this is an option at all, so ask where yours
falls.

**It can reach places an operation cannot.** It is used for tumors in parts of
the brain that cannot safely be operated on, and for people who are not well
enough for an operation.

**If cancer has spread to your brain from somewhere else**, guidelines from
three American cancer societies say this treatment on its own should be offered
when there are up to four spots that have not been operated on, with one
exception for small cell cancer. They also say it should be offered to the
place a spot was removed from, after an operation for one or two of them, when
that area can be treated safely. If that is your situation, those are two
sentences worth taking to your appointment.
[Cancer that has spread to the brain](/tumors/brain-metastases) is the page for
the rest of it.

**If you have a glioma, the answer is more careful.** European guidelines say
that for a newly diagnosed glioblastoma this has not been shown to do better
than an ordinary course of radiation. For a tumor that has come back, it is one
of the treatments considered. So it is a reasonable question at a recurrence and
a less likely answer at the start.

**It is also used after an operation**, on what is left behind or on a spot that
has come back.

**Ask what it is meant to achieve.** Stopping growth, shrinking, or easing a
symptom are three different goals, and they change what counts as this working.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

One day, start to finish, for most people.

1. **You arrive in the morning.** You will usually have been asked not to eat or
   drink after midnight, but ask, because centers differ.
2. **A line goes into a vein in your arm.** It is used for the dye that makes
   the scan clearer, and it can give you something to help you relax if you want
   it.
3. **Your head is fixed in place**, with either a frame or a mask. That step has
   its own section below.
4. **You have a scan with the frame or mask on**, usually an MRI, sometimes a CT
   as well. This takes around half an hour. Its whole job is to say exactly
   where the target is, right now, in the position you are in.
5. **You wait while your plan is built.** A neurosurgeon, a radiation
   oncologist and a physicist work out the angles and the dose from that scan.
   This is the long part.
6. **The treatment.** You lie on the treatment table, your head is held in position,
   and
   the machine does the rest. You stay awake. You can talk to the team, and they
   can see and hear you.
7. **The frame or mask comes off**, and you go home, usually the same day.

## How long does it take? {#how-long}

Longer than people expect, and almost none of it is the treatment.

- **The treatment itself:** somewhere between about a quarter of an hour and a
  couple of hours. It depends on the size and shape of what is being treated and
  where it sits.
- **The planning wait:** up to an hour at one large hospital, one to two hours
  at another. Ask yours.
- **The whole day:** plan for several hours at the center. One hospital tells
  people to allow up to twelve hours.

**So plan the day, not the treatment.** Nobody should be sitting in a parking lot
wondering what has gone wrong at hour five. Ask your center what a typical day
runs to, and tell whoever is with you.

## What does it feel like? {#what-it-feels-like}

The treatment itself feels like nothing at all. The parts around it are where
the sensation is.

**The frame, if you have one, is the part people ask about.** Four spots are numbed
with an injection: each side of your forehead, and the back of your head. Then
the pins are tightened. People describe pressure
rather than pain. The frame itself weighs less than two pounds, and your head is
not shaved for it.

**A mask, if you have one instead, is the same mesh mask used for ordinary
radiation.** [What the mask is like, and what to ask for if it frightens
you](/treatments/radiation-therapy#the-mask) covers that in full, and every word
of it applies here.

**During the treatment you feel nothing.** There is no heat. There is no noise.
You do not see the beam. People often doze off, and that is fine.

**You are awake and you can speak.** You are not put to sleep, and you are not
alone in the sense that matters: the team is just outside, watching and
listening, and you can talk to them.

**You can ask for something to help you relax.** You do not have to be in distress
to ask, and plenty of people do. Ask at the appointment before the day, not on
the morning.

## The wait, and what to do with it {#the-wait}

This is the part of the day that gets explained the least, so here it is in
full.

After your scan, you sit and wait while the team builds your plan from it. At one
hospital that is a neurosurgeon, a radiation oncologist and a physicist.
You are usually still wearing the frame or the mask holder while they do. It can
run to a couple of hours. Nothing is wrong. This is the step that makes the rest
of it accurate, and it cannot be done in advance because it is built from the
scan you just had.

Things that make it easier, and that centers are used to being asked for:

- **Ask in advance how long the wait usually runs at your center.** The answer
  varies by hospital and by how complicated the plan is, and knowing the number
  is most of it.
- **Ask whether the person with you can sit with you.** It is worth asking rather
  than assuming either answer.
- **Bring something to listen to**, and something to drink if you are allowed
  it. Reading with a frame on is harder than people expect.
- **Ask for something to take the edge off** if the waiting is the part you are
  dreading. It is the same medicine as above.
- **Ask what you can do about the feel of it.** Pressure from the frame, or a
  dry mouth, or needing the bathroom are all ordinary things to raise, and none of
  them is a silly thing to ask about.

You are not being left. It is a fair thing to ask somebody to come and tell you
where the plan is up to.

## Side effects, in the days after {#side-effects-soon}

What turns up in the first few days tends to be mild. Here is the list your center
will go through with you.

- **Headache**, and feeling tired.
- **Feeling sick, or being sick.**
- **Sore pin sites**, if you had a frame. There may be a little bleeding when
  the pins come out, a dressing goes on, and there can be bruising, a change in
  skin color, and numbness or tingling in the scalp there.
- **Grogginess on the way home**, from the relaxing medicine. This is why
  somebody else drives.

One hospital tells people they should be able to get back to work or school the
next day, and may be asked to go easy on anything strenuous for a few days. Ask
what yours says, because that is a permission rather than a prediction.

**Looking after pin sites, if you had a frame.** One hospital's instructions are
to take the dressing off the day after, clean the sites twice a day with a
little antibiotic ointment for three or four days, keep your head up on a couple
of pillows for the first week, and leave washing your hair for two days. Your
center will give you their own version. Follow theirs, and ask for it in writing
before you leave. **Pin sites also have their own rule about when to call, and
it is at the bottom of the next section, under the list of things that cannot
wait.**

## Side effects that can come later {#side-effects-later}

Two things can turn up well after the day, and both have answers.

**Swelling at the treated spot.** The area can swell, either soon afterward or
months later. It can press on brain around it and bring on symptoms. Steroids often settle it, and your team may give
you one around the treatment to head it off. [Steroids](/treatments/steroids) is the page for that medicine.

**A change on a later scan that is not the tumor.** Months or years afterward,
something can turn up on a scan of the treated spot that looks a great deal like
the tumor coming back, and is not. It has a name, radiation necrosis, and there
is treatment for it.
[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans#much-later)
explains what it is, how your team tells the two apart, and what the timing
usually looks like after this particular treatment. That page owns it. Read it
when you get to that scan rather than now.

**Hair loss depends on where the target sits.** One hospital lists it as happening
only when a tumor is close to the scalp. If yours is, ask whether to expect it
over that spot.

[ESCALATION]

**If you had a frame, the pin sites are this page's own rule.** The list above
does not have them in it, because no other treatment on this site leaves pin
holes in your scalp. **Call your team the same day** if a pin site turns hot to
the touch, if anything cloudy or bad smelling leaks from one, or if you run a
fever. The same day, for any one of those three. That is the
same answer [brain surgery](/treatments/craniotomy) gives for a warm wound, a
leaking wound and a fever after an operation.

The fever lines in the list above are written for people having chemotherapy and
for the weeks after an operation. Neither of those is you. Yours is the one in
this paragraph.

## What you need first, or need to bring {#what-you-need-first}

Ask your center for their own list, because these vary. In general:

**Before the day:**

- **A recent, detailed MRI with dye.** [Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) covers that
  appointment.
- **Tell them about anything implanted.** A pacemaker, a cochlear implant, a
  nerve stimulator, a medicine pump.
- **Ask about your blood thinners**, if you take any, well before the day. Pins
  go into your scalp.
- **Say if lying flat on your back for half an hour to an hour would be hard**,
  because of your back, your breathing or reflux. Say it early, not on the
  table.
- **Arrange somebody to drive you both ways.** Not just home.

**The night before and the morning:**

- **Ask when you have to stop eating and drinking.** Do not assume.
- **Wash your hair, and leave it loose.** No clips, pins, bands or hairpieces on
  the day.
- **Take your morning medicines with sips of water** unless you are told
  otherwise, and bring every medicine you take, including the ones you buy
  yourself and any inhalers.
- **Wear loose clothes and shoes you can slip off.** Not a shirt that has to
  come off over your head, because of the frame.
- **Bring something to fill several hours.**

This one is a single long day rather than a course, so what it asks of you is
concentrated into it.

**You are the ride, and that is not optional.** One hospital tells people to
arrange a driver both ways, whether they are having a frame or a mask. If they
took the relaxing medicine they will be groggy afterward as well.

**Clear the whole day.** Several hours at the center is normal, and one hospital
tells people to allow up to twelve hours. The treatment is the short part. Most of the
day is the scan and the wait while the plan is built.

**The wait is the hard part, not the treatment.** They may be sitting in a frame
for a couple of hours with nothing happening. Ask whether you can sit with them.
If you cannot, ask somebody to come and tell you both how it is going, because
silence in that gap is where the fear grows.

**That evening and the day after.** They can usually do ordinary things the next
day, and are often told to go gently for the first day or two. If there were
pins, there is a small dressing and there are pin sites to clean for a few days.
Ask to be shown how before you leave, and do it once while somebody watches.

**The pin sites are the part no other page on this site covers.** Hot to the touch,
leaking anything cloudy or bad smelling, or a fever means calling the team the
same day. Everything else to watch for is the same list as for any brain tumor,
and it is above.

**And know that the answer is not coming today.** There is nothing to be told
that evening. The first real news is a scan weeks or months away. Knowing that
in advance stops the drive home being a wait for a phone call that was never
going to come.

## Why am I having another one? {#another-one}

Being offered this a second time is not automatically bad news.

This treatment can be repeated. Sometimes that is for the same spot. Sometimes it
is because a new spot has turned up somewhere else in the brain, which is a
different event from the treated one failing, and is one of the reasons you keep
having scans.

Your team may also have planned more than one session from the start, if what is
being treated is larger or sits somewhere awkward.

Ask which of those it is. "The one we treated has grown", "something new has
appeared" and "this was always going to take two" are three different
conversations, and people sit through the wrong one for weeks.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result? {#result}

There is no result on the day, and it is worth knowing that before you go home.

Nothing is taken out and nothing is sent to a laboratory, so there is nothing to
report that evening. The answer comes from a scan some weeks or months later,
and then from the ones after that.

**Success often looks like nothing happening.** The goal is called tumor
control, and it means the tumor stays the same size or gets smaller. Both count.
A tumor that has stopped growing has done what the treatment asked of it.

That matters because of what the scans show. A tumor that is not cancer often
stops growing right away without ever getting smaller. A cancerous one is more
likely to become stable or to get smaller over weeks to months. Full effects can take
months, and sometimes a year or more.

**So "no change" on your report may be the good outcome**, and it does not read
like one. Ask your team, before you leave, what they will be looking for on the
first scan and when it is. Then ask who tells you, and whether the report will
appear in your records before anybody calls.
[Follow-up scans, and what the results mean](/tests/follow-up-scans) goes
through the words on the report.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Will I have a frame or a mask, and why that one?
- How long is the whole day here, start to finish?
- How long does the planning wait usually take, and can somebody sit with me?
- Can I have something to help me relax, and when do I ask for it?
- Is this one session or several?
- What is this meant to do: stop it growing, shrink it, or help a symptom?
- Will I need a steroid afterward, and who adjusts it?
- What should make me call you in the first week?
- When is my first scan afterward, and what will you be looking for on it?
- How will you tell later whether a change on a scan is the tumor or the
  treatment?
- If a new spot turns up, can this be done again?
