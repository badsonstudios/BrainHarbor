## The short version {#short-version}

!%watch and wait%Watch and wait means your team follows a tumor with regular
scans and check-ups instead of treating it now. **It is a plan, not a delay.** It
is chosen when treating the tumor now would likely cost you more than watching
it. If the tumor grows more than expected, or you get new symptoms, the plan is
looked at again, and you do not have to wait for the next scan to tell your
team. **The waiting can be hard, and that is worth saying out loud.** It is part
of the plan too, and you do not have to carry it on your own.

## What is watch and wait? {#what-it-is}

It means no treatment aimed at the tumor for now, and a close eye on it instead.

Your team may call it active monitoring, active surveillance, or observation.
They all mean the same thing: scans and check-ups at set times, so a change is
seen early.

It is used for tumors that grow slowly and are not causing trouble. The ones it
is most often used for are:

- **Some meningiomas**, especially small ones found by chance on a scan done for
  something else.
- **Some low-grade gliomas**, often after an operation to take out what can
  safely be taken out.
- **Some acoustic neuromas**, which doctors also call vestibular schwannomas.
- **Some pituitary tumors** that are small and not causing symptoms.

## Why would my team suggest it? {#why}

Because every treatment has a cost, and for some tumors the cost of treating now
is higher than the cost of watching.

An operation has risks. Radiation can affect thinking and memory years later.
Treating a slow, quiet tumor today means taking on those risks today, when they
could be put off, sometimes for years.

**So watching is a decision, not the absence of one.** Your team is choosing it
because, for you and for now, they judge it to do the least harm.

It is not offered to everyone. Whether it suits you depends on what the tumor is
likely to be, where it sits, how big it is, whether it is causing symptoms, your
general health, and what you want. If a tumor is pressing on something important
or causing problems, treating it is usually the plan instead.

## What happens, step by step {#step-by-step}

The short answer is scans and check-ups at set times, and you telling your team
about changes in between.

1. **A first scan becomes the starting point.** Every later scan is compared with
   it, so a small change can be seen.
2. **You have scans at set times.** Almost always these are MRI scans.
   [Your MRI scan](/tests/mri) covers what the scan itself is like, and
   [follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans) covers how each one is compared with
   the last.
3. **You see your team.** They ask how you have been and examine you.
4. **Some people have other tests as well.** Hearing tests are part of watching an
   acoustic neuroma. Eye tests, which check how far out to the sides you can see,
   and hormone blood tests are part of watching some pituitary tumors.
5. **Between scans, you report changes.** Something new, or something getting
   worse, is a reason to get in touch. You do not have to wait for your next scan.
   If something sudden, severe and new to you happens, treat it as an
   emergency. [Get help now](/get-help-now) says what to do, and
   [what to do in a seizure](/seizures/what-to-do) says when a seizure needs an
   ambulance and when it does not.
6. **If something changes, the plan is looked at again.** That can mean
   treatment. It can mean a scan sooner than planned, which
   [follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans#again-so-soon) explains. Or it can
   mean going on as before.

The scans run on a timetable. Symptoms do not, which is why step five belongs to
you.

## How often, and for how long? {#how-often}

**It depends on the tumor, so there is no single timetable to print here.** A UK
charity for people with brain tumors puts the range at anything from every few
months to every couple of years. Scans are usually closer together at the start,
and they spread out if nothing changes.

So ask for your own timetable, in writing, and ask what would make it change.

As for how long: for some people watching goes on for years. For some people
with a small meningioma, acoustic neuroma or pituitary tumor, it never turns into
treatment at all. For others it is a stretch of time before a treatment that was
always likely to come, so that its costs are put off for as long as is safe.

For some people with a meningioma, it can be reasonable to talk about scanning
less often, or stopping: if it has settled, or for older people whose other
health problems matter more. That is something to talk through with your team, not a
choice to make on your own.

## What is it like to be watched? {#what-it-is-like}

Watching itself does nothing to your body. The tests are the only part that is
done to you.

It is the other part that people describe. It can be hard to be told you have a
tumor and that nobody is going to treat it yet. You might feel you are doing
nothing to fight it. You might feel treatment is being kept from you. The time
between appointments can feel long, and sometimes it is.

Feeling like this does not mean watching is the wrong plan for you, and it does
not mean you are handling it badly. It means you are living with something
unfinished. Say so at your next appointment. How you are coping is part of what
your team should be asking about.

## Worry and low mood, and what you can do {#worry}

**Worry and low mood have been studied in people being watched, and what the
studies found is worth knowing.** Most of that research is in people with a
meningioma or an acoustic neuroma.

In those studies, worry was common in people with a meningioma whether it was
being watched or had been taken out. Having it removed did not make that go
away. One study from Germany found that low mood, high enough to count as a
problem, was more common in the people being watched than in people whose
operation had gone well. Another study found no such difference, and low mood
was uncommon in both groups. For an acoustic neuroma, people being watched said
they felt about as well as people who had been treated, or better. Less is known
about people being watched for a glioma.

So the honest summary is this. For a meningioma, the worry is real and common
whichever plan you are on, so it is not a sign that you chose wrong. Whatever
your tumor, it is also not something you have to sit with on your own.

What you can do about it:

- **Tell your team how you are, not only how the tumor is.** The researchers
  behind the German study said that routine checks for distress, and support,
  might be helpful, even when the finding on the scan is fairly harmless. You do
  not have to wait to be asked.
- **Know your plan.** What they are watching for, and what would change it. The
  questions at the bottom of this page are there for that.
- **Talk to someone who knows brain tumors.** A counselor is an option, and so is
  a free helpline. [Get help now](/get-help-now) has the numbers.
- **The days around each scan are their own kind of hard.**
  [Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans#waiting) goes through the wait for a
  result, and what has been tried for it.

If it is more than worry, and your mood keeps sinking, you do not have to wait
for an appointment to say so. The numbers on [Get help now](/get-help-now) can
be called today.

## What if it grows while we are watching? {#if-it-grows}

**Then the plan is looked at again, and catching that is what the scans are
for.** Some growth is expected with some tumors, and it does not always mean
treatment. What growth means depends a great deal on the kind of tumor, so this
part is split up by tumor.

**Meningioma.** Most meningiomas that are watched do grow a little. In many of
them, the growth then slows down or stops. Even so, in a study from Norway of people
whose meningioma was found by chance, more people ended up having treatment
than not over the years, nearly all of it early on, and the rest had not needed
it by the end. Nobody in that study got symptoms from the tumor before
treatment began, and waiting did not make the treatment harder.

**Low-grade glioma.** This is different, and it is worth knowing plainly. A
diffuse low-grade glioma usually keeps growing slowly, and over years it can
change into a faster-growing type. That is why watching a glioma means watching
it closely, and why it is not offered to everyone. For a glioma found by chance,
one recent guideline says an operation should still come first, as long as it
can be done without harming how you live. If you are being watched without an
operation, it is a fair question to ask why your team chose that. For many
people, watching comes after an operation, as a way of putting off radiation
and chemotherapy. In one trial, starting
radiation right after surgery held the tumor back for longer and meant fewer
seizures. But it has not been shown to change how long people live, which is why
some teams wait and keep radiation's cost for later. For some
people with a grade 2 glioma that has an IDH change, there is now also a
medicine taken by mouth, vorasidenib. It was tested against a placebo in people
who would otherwise have been watched after surgery, and a biopsy counted as
surgery, so ask whether it could apply to you.
[Low-grade glioma](/tumors/low-grade-glioma) has a section on it, and
[clinical trials](/treatments/clinical-trials#placebo) explains what a placebo
is and what it means for the group you would be in.

**Acoustic neuroma.** Many small ones do not grow. But hearing on that side often
gets worse while the tumor is being watched, which is why hearing tests are part
of the plan and part of the decision.

**Pituitary tumor.** These usually grow slowly. If one grows toward the nerves
for sight, eye tests check whether your sight is changing, which is why some
people have them.

Whatever the tumor, ask your team how much change would make them act.

## How can they know what it is without a sample? {#without-a-sample}

**They cannot be completely sure, and it is fair to know that.** A scan can
suggest what a tumor is. Only a sample of tissue can say for certain.

So a tumor that is being watched without an operation has a best guess for a
name, not a confirmed one. Your team makes that guess from how it looks on the
scan and where it sits.

This is one more reason the scans matter. The way a tumor behaves over time adds
to what the first scan showed. If something thought to be slow grows fast, that
is a reason for your team to think again about what it is, and whether it needs
treating.

It is fine to ask how sure they are, and what would make them want a sample.

## What you can do while you are being watched {#what-you-can-do}

Most of this is out of your hands. These parts are not:

- **Get the plan in writing.** What they are watching for, when the next scan is,
  and what would make them change course.
- **Ask what you should get in touch about between scans**, and who to contact.
  The answer depends on your tumor, so get your own list rather than trusting a
  general one.
- **Keep a short diary of anything new**, with the date it started. It makes the
  next appointment about facts rather than memory.
- **Keep copies of your scans and reports** in one place.
- **Ask for a second opinion if you want one.** It is an ordinary thing to do,
  and it does not mean you have stopped trusting your team.
- **Take someone with you when results are due**, if you can.

## Who reads it, and how do I get the result? {#results}

A radiologist reads each scan and compares it with the earlier ones. The report
goes to the doctor looking after you, and that is the person to go through it
with you. [Follow-up scans](/tests/follow-up-scans#results) has more on reading a
report that compares scans.

Ask before you leave how you will hear, and when. Otherwise it is easy to spend
days waiting for a call that was never planned.

## What to ask your team {#questions}

- Why are we watching rather than treating now?
- When is my next scan, and who will tell me the result?
- What exactly are you watching for?
- What would make you change the plan?
- What should I contact you about between scans, and what counts as an emergency
  for my tumor?
- Does waiting make treatment later any harder for me?
- If I have a grade 2 glioma, could vorasidenib apply to me?
- What would treating it now involve, and what would it cost me?
- How sure are you what this is? Would you ever want a sample?
- Is there anything on my scan that suggests how fast it might grow?
- Will I have hearing tests, eye tests or blood tests as well as scans?
- Can I get a second opinion and still be looked after here?
- Is there a point where the scans would get further apart, or stop?
- Who can I talk to if the waiting is getting to me?
