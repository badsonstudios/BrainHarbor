# 1. Watch and wait (active monitoring / active surveillance)

## What it is
A plan in which no tumor-directed treatment is given yet, and the tumor is followed with regular
scans and clinic visits. NCI's PDQ patient summary lists **active surveillance** as a formal
treatment option, defined as "closely monitoring a patient's condition without giving any treatment
until signs or symptoms appear or change," used for slow-growing tumors, in order to "avoid or
delay the need for treatments such as radiation therapy or surgery."
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)

**The core reframe for the page:** this is a decision, not an absence of one. It is chosen because
the risk of treating now is judged higher than the risk of watching. That framing is what patients
report they are not given.

## Which tumors
- **Grade 1 meningioma** — NCI lists active surveillance first among options for grade 1
  meningioma (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/). The EANO meningioma guideline states
  observation is the first option in incidental, asymptomatic, suspected meningioma
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34181733/;
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2020.01523/full)
- **Low-grade / slow-growing glioma** — The Brain Tumour Charity: the most common tumours managed
  this way are newly diagnosed low grade gliomas (grade 1/2 astrocytoma, grade 2 oligodendroglioma)
  and grade 1 meningioma
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/watch-and-wait/).
  A Cochrane-hosted review notes diffuse low-grade glioma may be followed under a "watchful
  waiting" concept mainly where surgical resection is judged high risk
  (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6953319)
- Also used for small vestibular schwannoma and small pituitary incidentalomas in practice (see
  page 17); confirm before publishing.

## Step by step, from the patient's point of view
1. A tumor is found — often incidentally, on a scan done for something else (headache, injury).
2. The team explains that it looks slow-growing and is not causing problems, and proposes watching.
3. A **baseline MRI** is the reference every later scan is compared against.
4. A follow-up MRI at a set interval. EANO's schedule for asymptomatic/small meningioma: contrast
   MRI at 6 months after diagnosis; if still asymptomatic, annually; after 5 years the interval can
   be doubled (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2020.01523/full)
   `[BOUNDARY — scan intervals]`
5. Between scans, the patient is asked to report new or changed symptoms — this is the part that is
   usually under-explained. The Brain Tumour Charity frames the plan as scans "from every few
   months to every couple of years"
   (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/watch-and-wait/)
6. If the tumor grows or symptoms appear, the plan changes to surgery, radiation, or drugs.

## What is actually being monitored
Size and imaging features compared over time on MRI
(https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/watch-and-wait/);
plus clinical symptoms and neurological exam. In meningioma, growth is not uniformly progressive —
in a 5-year prospective series, more than 60% of tumors displayed a self-limiting growth pattern,
supporting serial imaging until persistent radiological or symptomatic growth
(https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2020.01523/full)

## The psychological load — this deserves its own section
A prospective single-center study found meningioma patients on a wait-and-watch strategy had a
**4.26-fold higher risk of a pathological depression score**, and concluded psychological distress
in this group is high and often unaddressed
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7761113/;
https://mdpi.com/2072-6694/12/12/3503/htm)
`[Publish as: "Many people find waiting harder than treatment. That is normal, and there is help."
Do not publish the odds ratio — it is a risk statistic, and while not a prognosis figure, it reads
like one at 6th grade.]`

Also worth naming on the page: **scanxiety** (the days before a scan), the pull toward "just take
it out," and that asking for a second opinion is normal.

## Tests required before / during
- Contrast-enhanced MRI of the brain (baseline and each follow-up)
- Neurological examination each visit
- Visual field testing where the tumor is near the optic pathway (NCI lists visual field testing
  among CNS tumor tests) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)

## Questions patients should ask
- What exactly are you watching for?
- What will make you change the plan?
- When is my next scan, and who tells me the result?
- What symptoms should make me call you before the next scan?
- Does watching now make treatment later harder?
- Can I get a second opinion without losing my place in this plan?

# 2. Biopsy

## What it is
Removal of a small piece of tumor so a pathologist can say what it is. NCI lists two forms for CNS
tumors: **stereotactic biopsy** (needle guided by a computer and imaging, through a small hole in
the skull) and **open biopsy** (a craniotomy is made and tissue taken)
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

Key idea for the page: **a scan can suggest, tissue decides.** Modern classification also needs
molecular testing (see "what it cannot tell you").

## Step by step (stereotactic needle biopsy)
1. Planning MRI or CT, often with a frame or fiducial markers placed on the scalp.
2. General anesthesia, or local anesthesia with sedation depending on center.
3. A small area of hair is shaved; a small hole (twist-drill or burr hole) is made in the skull.
   Twist-drill vs. burr-hole approaches are both in routine use
   (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39448506/)
4. A needle is passed to the target and several small samples are taken.
5. A pathologist often looks at a sample in the room (frozen section) to confirm diagnostic tissue
   was obtained before the patient leaves the OR.
6. Closure with a stitch or staple. Post-op CT is common to check for bleeding.
7. Usually an overnight stay for neurological observation; some centers do same-day discharge.

## Step by step (open biopsy)
An open biopsy is done through a craniotomy — see page 3 for the full experience. It is chosen when
the surgeon also wants to decompress, or when the lesion cannot be safely needled.

## What a biopsy is for
- To name the tumor type and grade
- To get tissue for molecular/genetic testing that now drives treatment choice (IDH mutation,
  1p/19q co-deletion, BRAF V600E, MGMT). NCI lists gene testing, immunohistochemistry and
  cytogenetic analysis among the tests done on CNS tumor tissue
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/). Those results decide whether drugs like
  vorasidenib or dabrafenib+trametinib are even options (see page 9).

## What a biopsy cannot tell you
- **It may not get diagnostic tissue.** Rate of non-diagnostic ("negative") biopsy is around 5% in
  brain tumor series, which can mean a second procedure
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734929/)
- **Sampling error.** A needle samples a small part of a tumor that may vary internally; the grade
  found may under-represent the tumor.
- It does not treat the tumor and does not relieve pressure.
- It does not tell you how you will do — deliberately out of scope for BrainHarbor.

## Risks
- Perioperative complication rate for stereotactic needle biopsy is roughly **6–12%**, with
  intracranial hemorrhage the most frequent; hemorrhage on imaging in ~7–10%, of which about half
  (≈3.4–5.2%) are symptomatic (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734929/)
- Other risks: seizure, brain swelling, infection
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9734929/)
- Needle biopsy carries lower mortality than open biopsy in specialized centers (0.2% vs ~1%);
  morbidity 0.4–17.2% (average 4.9%) for stereotactic vs 2–14% for open
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4543436/)
  `[Publish as "small but real risk of bleeding"; the percentages are risk statistics — flag to
  owner whether any numerals are allowed. See §Boundary calls.]`

## Recovery
Typically much shorter than a craniotomy: overnight observation, headache at the site, small
incision. Results usually take several days to two weeks because molecular testing takes longer
than the initial slide read. **The wait for results is a distinct emotional event and should be
named on the page.**

## Which tumors
Any suspected brain tumor where tissue is needed and safe removal is not the first step: deep or
midline lesions, multifocal lesions, suspected lymphoma, brainstem lesions, patients not fit for
craniotomy, and lesions where the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain.

## Tests required before
- Contrast MRI (planning / navigation sequences)
- Blood tests including clotting and platelet count (bleeding is the main risk)
- Review and often pausing of blood thinners
- Anesthesia assessment

## Questions to ask
- Why a biopsy instead of removing the tumor?
- Needle or open — and why that one for me?
- What is the chance you won't get enough tissue, and what happens then?
- What tests will you run on the tissue, and how long will each take?
- Will I need to stay overnight?
- Will this delay other treatment?

# 3. Brain surgery (craniotomy)

## What it is
An operation in which a piece of skull is temporarily removed so the surgeon can reach and remove
as much tumor as is safe; the bone is put back at the end. ABTA's description: a craniotomy
involves removing a portion of the skull so the neurosurgeon can find the tumor and remove as much
as possible, with the piece of skull replaced after surgery
(https://www.abta.org/about-brain-tumors/treatments-side-effects/). NCI frames surgery as removal
of tumor tissue to decrease pressure on surrounding brain, often followed by radiation or
chemotherapy as adjuvant treatment (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

## Step by step, arrival to discharge

### Before the day
- Pre-op instructions 24–48 hours ahead; a **preoperative MRI** (often a special navigation
  protocol); pre-op labs; anesthesia consult; consent discussion of the surgical plan and risks;
  bring advance directive / living will
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Discussion of second opinion is explicitly encouraged
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- If fluorescence guidance is planned, an oral agent (5-ALA) is given some hours before surgery so
  tumor tissue glows under blue light; it improves complete removal of the enhancing tumor
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3665818/) `[BOUNDARY — timing of the 5-ALA drink]`

### The day
- Change into a gown, remove jewelry and contacts, IV line placed
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Hair: only the area around the planned incision is usually clipped; many centers shave a strip
  rather than the head. **Patients ask about this constantly — the page must answer it plainly.**
  (Confirm with an ABTA/NBTS-sourced statement before publishing; current sources describe scalp
  numbing but not the shave extent —
  https://www.abta.org/about-brain-tumors/treatments-side-effects/)
- Head is held still in a clamp; the scalp is numbed even under general anesthesia. **The brain
  itself does not feel pain**; the scalp and surrounding tissues are numbed
  (https://www.abta.org/about-brain-tumors/treatments-side-effects/)
- Navigation (image guidance), and often intraoperative monitoring or mapping, is used to keep the
  surgeon away from critical tracts; combining navigation, DTI fiber tracking and 5-ALA maximizes
  safe removal in eloquent regions
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9555356/)
- Some centers use intraoperative MRI to check how much tumor is left before closing; combined with
  5-ALA it improves resection outcomes
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10143-017-0907-z)
- Tissue is sent for pathology and molecular testing during the operation
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

### Waking up
- Most patients spend the first night in the ICU
  (https://www.abta.org/about-brain-tumors/treatments-side-effects/)
- Disorientation on waking is normal. A patient quoted by NBTS: *"It feels like you're this floating
  consciousness. It's very strange."*
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Common early: confusion, temporary memory gaps, nausea, pain arriving as anesthesia wears off,
  and sometimes facial or motor changes
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Practical tip worth publishing: ask for **scheduled** pain medicine rather than waiting for pain
  to build (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
  `[BOUNDARY — "every 4 hours" is in the source; that is a schedule. Recommend publishing the
  principle without the interval.]`
- Frequent neurological checks (name, date, squeeze my hands, follow my finger) through the night —
  patients find these exhausting and should be warned
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

### Days 1 to discharge
- If stable, patients are got up and moving on day 1
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Therapy needs are partly predictable by tumor site: cerebellum → PT/OT for balance;
  temporal/parietal → speech therapy
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- **Cognition often feels worse on day 2–3 and then improves** — a speech specialist quoted by
  NBTS describes "that subtle change to feel worse on second or third day after surgery." Telling
  patients this in advance prevents panic
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- Stay length varies with complexity, tumor size/location and health — typically several days or
  longer (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/;
  awake craniotomy source gives "several days to one week or longer":
  https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
  `[BOUNDARY — hospital stay length]`
- Discharge requires stable vitals, no active complication, and a plan for home or inpatient rehab
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

### At home
- Staples or sutures are removed roughly 7–14 days after surgery (sources vary: "7 to 10 days";
  "10–14 days", longer for repeat surgery through the same incision)
  (https://www.ummhealth.org/health-library/discharge-instructions-for-craniotomy;
  https://www.uclahealth.org/sites/default/files/documents/Patient-Discharge-Craniotomy071117.pdf)
  `[BOUNDARY — days to staple removal]`
- Driving, flying, lifting and returning to work all require clearance from the surgeon; timelines
  are individual and driving is additionally governed by seizure law
  (https://www.uclahealth.org/sites/default/files/documents/Patient-Discharge-Craniotomy071117.pdf;
  https://healthy.kaiserpermanente.org/health-wellness/health-encyclopedia/he.craniotomy-what-to-expect-at-home.zy1337)
  `[BOUNDARY — "a month or two to recover" per Kaiser]`
- The invisible part: attention is the most commonly affected domain, with reduced filtering and
  increased distractibility; single conversations are easier than groups; word-finding difficulty is
  common; people are less patient because the difficulty is not visible
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

## The vocabulary — "how much did you get out?"
This belongs on the surgery page, in plain terms:
- **Gross total resection (GTR)** — no tumor visible on the post-op MRI. Defined as removal of all
  tumor as gauged by MRI (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5358612/;
  https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/64710)
- **Subtotal resection (STR)** — some tumor is still visible on post-op imaging
  (https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/64710)
- **Supratotal resection** — removal extends past the MRI-visible border into surrounding tissue
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10053695/)
- **Debulking** — removing tumor bulk, usually to relieve pressure and symptoms, without expecting
  to get it all (https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/64710)
- The honest framing: GTR is not always the goal, because the brain's anatomy makes complete removal
  risky near eloquent structures and can significantly reduce quality of life
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7093492/)
- Note terminology drift: "gross total" is defined by the post-op MRI, not by what the surgeon saw.
  Microscopic tumor cells remain in diffuse glioma even after GTR — say this plainly, it prevents a
  cruel misunderstanding.

## Risks, and how they vary by location
- General surgical risks: bleeding, infection, anesthesia reaction, seizure, swelling, CSF leak,
  blood clots.
- **Location drives the neurological risk.** Motor cortex tumors present with weakness, ataxia, gait
  problems and spasticity, and surgery there risks worsening them
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6399000/)
- **Supplementary motor area (SMA) syndrome** — a transient loss of speech initiation and movement
  on one side, appearing right after surgery and typically recovering over days to weeks/months.
  Patients who are not warned believe they have been left permanently disabled
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12025504/). *This is one of the highest-value things
  BrainHarbor can explain.*
- Distance from the corticospinal tract and from language tracts predicts new deficit; absence of
  intraoperative language mapping increases language-deficit risk
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7281396/)
- Temporary vs permanent: in an awake-mapping series, 17% had new or worse deficits at 1 month but
  7% had a persistent deficit at 3 months
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19404147/) — the message to publish is
  **"many new problems after surgery get better over weeks; some do not."**

## Which tumors
Nearly all: glioma (all grades), meningioma, metastasis, ependymoma, medulloblastoma,
craniopharyngioma, and others — NCI's option lists start with surgery for most CNS tumor types
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/). Exceptions where surgery is not first: brainstem
glioma (radiation), CNS lymphoma, and germ cell tumors.

## Tests required before
- Contrast MRI, often with navigation protocol; functional MRI and DTI tractography when the tumor
  is near motor or language areas (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7281396/)
- Sometimes navigated TMS motor mapping pre-operatively
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11493810/)
- Bloods incl. clotting; ECG/chest imaging as required by anesthesia; medication review
- Baseline neurological, cognitive and (if relevant) language testing
- Visual field testing for tumors near the optic pathway
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)

## Questions to ask
- What is the goal of my operation — remove it all, remove some, relieve pressure, or get tissue?
- What is near my tumor, and what could I lose?
- Will you use mapping or monitoring?
- Will you scan me afterwards to see how much came out, and when?
- How much hair will be cut, and where will the scar be?
- How long in ICU, how long in hospital, and will I go home or to rehab?
- Who do I call after I go home, and for what?
- Will I need more treatment after surgery, and when will we know?

# 4. Awake craniotomy and brain mapping

## Why this needs its own page
Because it terrifies people, and because the fear is based on a misconception (that they will feel
the surgery). It also has a distinct patient job — the patient is an active participant. NBTS
treats it as a separate explainer for that reason
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/).

## What it is and why anyone would do it
The tumor sits near or inside brain that controls speech, language, movement or vision, and the
only reliable way to know where those functions live *in this particular brain* is to test them
while the surgeon works. A neurosurgeon quoted by NBTS: "An awake craniotomy gives the surgeon
really good feedback in the moment instead of just a before and after picture." Roughly 46–61% of
gliomas sit in eloquent regions
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/).

## Step by step
1. **Before:** baseline testing with a speech-language pathologist using the exact materials that
   will be used in the OR, so any change during surgery is recognizable
   (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
2. **Before:** the team walks the patient through what the room looks like, how they'll be
   positioned, what they'll feel, and pain management
   (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
3. **Asleep for the opening:** deeper sedation while the skull is opened and again while it is
   closed (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
4. **Awake for the mapping and removal:** sedation is lightened so the patient can talk and move
   (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
5. **Mapping:** the surgeon applies a small electrical current to a small area for a brief interval
   (typically under 4 seconds) and watches what happens — the "mark the utility lines before
   digging" analogy
   (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
6. **The patient's job**, depending on tumor site: name pictures, repeat words or sentences, read
   aloud, answer simple questions, count, recite the alphabet; squeeze a ball, lift an arm or leg,
   move fingers or toes, sometimes play an instrument; report sensations or visual changes
   (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
7. **Asleep again** for closing.

## What it actually feels like
- No pain from the brain itself — the brain has no pain-sensing nerve endings; the scalp is numbed
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
- Discomfort mostly comes from lying still in one position
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
- Stimulation produces odd sensations, not pain: motor stimulation makes a specific muscle contract;
  sensory stimulation produces a tingle in a specific body part
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
- A very common and frightening moment: **during mapping the patient may briefly be unable to find a
  word or move a limb, and it comes back.** That is the test working. Say this on the page.
- Waking in surgery is disorienting but manageable with the team's support
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)

## Risks specific to being awake
Pooled from a systematic review and meta-analysis of anesthesia for awake craniotomy: awake
craniotomy failure ~2%, intraoperative seizure ~8%, new neurological dysfunction ~17%, conversion
to general anesthesia ~2%
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4882028/). A separate 424-patient series reported 6.4%
"failed" awake craniotomies, mostly from loss of intraoperative communication (4.2%) or seizures
(2.1%) (https://thejns.org/view/journals/j-neurosurg/118/2/article-p243.xml). Intraoperative seizure
rates in the literature vary widely (reported 2.9–54% across a multicenter mapping series;
commonly quoted 3.4–20%) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7713838/).
**Publish as:** "Sometimes it doesn't work out and the team puts you to sleep. That is planned for."
`[Percentages are risk statistics — see boundary calls.]`

## Recovery
Same as a standard craniotomy: recovery room, then hospital room; several days to a week or longer;
follow-up with neurosurgeon and neuro-oncologist; possible PT/OT/speech
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)

## Which tumors
Gliomas (especially low-grade and high-grade in or near eloquent cortex); occasionally metastases
and epilepsy-associated lesions in eloquent areas
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19404147/)

## Tests required before
- Contrast MRI plus **functional MRI** and **DTI tractography** to plan
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7281396/)
- Sometimes navigated TMS mapping (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11493810/)
- **Baseline neuropsychological and speech-language testing**
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-during-an-awake-craniotomy-for-brain-tumor-removal/)
- Anesthesia assessment specifically for awake technique (airway, anxiety, ability to lie still,
  ability to cooperate)

## Questions to ask
- Why do I need to be awake?
- What exactly will you ask me to do?
- Will I feel anything? What will the odd feelings be?
- What happens if I panic, or if I can't do the tasks?
- Who will be talking to me the whole time?
- What if I have a seizure on the table?
- Can I practice the tasks first?

# 5. Radiation therapy (external beam / IMRT) — the hub page

## What it is
High-energy x-rays or other radiation aimed at the tumor to kill tumor cells. NCI names external
beam radiation, conformal radiation, IMRT, and stereotactic radiosurgery as approaches
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

## Step by step

### 1. Consultation with the radiation oncologist
History review, benefits and risks, and questions about timeline, side effects, cognitive effects,
fertility and appearance
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)

### 2. Simulation and the mask — the fear point, give it its own section
- The appointment is a **planning** appointment; no radiation is given
  (https://www.roswellpark.org/cancertalk/202401/your-radiation-mask-what-expect)
- A sheet of plastic mesh is warmed and molded over the face and head; it "feels a little like a
  warm flannel," has holes so you can breathe, and hardens in a few minutes
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/child-treatments/radiotherapy/)
- MSK: therapists warm the mesh and mold it while you lie on the table; there is "no trouble
  breathing, hearing, or seeing"; once dry the mask is marked and you are photographed for
  positioning (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- NBTS describes the mesh conforming and drying over 10–15 minutes
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
  `[BOUNDARY — minutes for mask setting; recommend keeping, it is orienting not prescriptive]`
- The whole simulation visit runs about 2 hours at MSK
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain) `[BOUNDARY]`
- Practical prep: comfortable removable clothing; no jewelry, powder, lotion or cream on the
  treatment area; pain or anxiety medicine can be taken beforehand if arranged
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- A CT scan is done in the mask, in treatment position; an IV contrast may be used
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- **Say this plainly:** if the mask feels overwhelming, tell the radiographer/nurse — this is
  common and they can help
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/child-treatments/radiotherapy/)
- The mask exists so every treatment hits the same place, and so the head stays still
  (https://www.roswellpark.org/cancertalk/202401/your-radiation-mask-what-expect)

### 3. Planning gap
Radiation oncologist, dosimetrist and medical physicist build the plan — beam angles, shapes,
dose. MSK: 1 to 10 business days between simulation and first treatment
(https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain;
https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
`[BOUNDARY — waiting time; recommend publishing as "usually a few days to a couple of weeks"]`

### 4. Daily treatment
- Check in, lie on the table, get positioned exactly as at simulation, mask on
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- Imaging is done first each day to verify position; first day setup takes longer (30 min–1 hour)
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- The linear accelerator rotates around you; **you feel nothing and see nothing**
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
- The therapists step out but watch on camera and talk over an intercom
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- The beam itself is only a few minutes; total room time 15–60 minutes
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain); NBTS says the
  appointment is 15–30 minutes
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
  `[BOUNDARY — session length. Sources disagree slightly; recommend "most of the visit is getting
  set up; the treatment itself takes a few minutes."]`
- Odd sensory experiences are reported (one patient smelled chlorine)
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)

### 5. Fractionation, in plain terms
The total dose is split into small daily doses so normal tissue can repair between them. Dr. Erik
Sulman: "If we give too much radiation in a single day, even normal tissues will be damaged"
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/).
Typical brain course: **30 weekday sessions over about 6 weeks**
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/);
the standard glioblastoma regimen is 60 Gy in 30 fractions Monday–Friday over 6 weeks
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3827088/). MSK notes schedules also include a single
treatment, 3–5 treatments, or daily Monday–Friday for weeks
(https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain).
Shorter courses (e.g. 40 Gy in 15 fractions over 3 weeks) are an accepted option for older patients
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3472503/;
https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1611977)
`[BOUNDARY — "Monday to Friday for about six weeks". Gy figures = dose, DO NOT PUBLISH.]`

## Acute (short-term) side effects
- **Fatigue** — the most common. Over 80% of brain tumor patients report fatigue during radiation;
  it can start within a week of the first treatment and increases with the number of fractions;
  it continues after treatment ends
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/;
  https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-radiotherapy-adults/).
  MSK describes it starting after 2–3 weeks and lasting weeks to months
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- **Hair loss** — begins 2–3 weeks in, only where the beam enters/exits; regrows in about 3–6
  months, sometimes a different texture or color; a wig may be covered by insurance with a
  prescription (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain;
  https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
  `[BOUNDARY — the "2–3 weeks" and "3–6 months" timings are orienting; recommend keeping]`
- **Skin reaction** — pink after 2–3 weeks, progressing to red or darker depending on skin tone;
  dryness, itching, flaking, blistering; may peak the week after treatment ends and heals in about
  3–4 weeks (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- **Skin care rules** — mild unscented soap, pat dry, fragrance-free moisturizer without lanolin,
  no tight caps, no perfume, no shaving the treated area, sun protection SPF 30+
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)
- **Brain swelling** — headache not relieved by acetaminophen, seizures, nausea, vision change,
  unsteadiness, mental status change, speech difficulty; call the team
  (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain). Often managed with
  steroids (see page 11).
- **Somnolence syndrome** — a period of excessive drowsiness, poor concentration and fatigue
  starting roughly 4–6 weeks after radiation to the head; it usually resolves on its own over a few
  weeks without treatment
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/resources/jargon-buster/somnolence-syndrome/).
  **This is badly explained everywhere and terrifies families who think the tumor is growing.**
- Cognitive and speech changes during treatment; emotional flattening/sadness reported by patients
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)

## Late (long-term) effects
- **Cognitive change** — mental slowing, memory and concentration difficulty; dose-dependent, with
  the hippocampus implicated in the mechanism
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7017115/)
- **Radiation necrosis** — dead tissue at the treated site that can look like tumor on scans and
  cause symptoms; distinguishing necrosis from recurrence is a real clinical problem
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6963489/;
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0303846708003910)
- **Endocrine (hormone) failure** — hypopituitarism after cranial radiation is common and increases
  with dose and time; a meta-analysis reported a prevalence of 0.66 for any degree of hypopituitarism
  after radiotherapy for brain tumors
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11721507/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK532082/). Practical publishable point: **ask whether your
  hormone levels will be checked in follow-up** — this is under-done.
- Others named in the literature: leukoencephalopathy, optic neuropathy, cranial neuropathy, carotid
  stenosis/stroke, and second tumors
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11721507/;
  https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-radiotherapy-adults/)
- Delayed fatigue can appear up to five years later
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-radiotherapy-adults/)

## Managing fatigue
NCCN recommends combining endurance and resistance exercise to reduce radiation-related fatigue
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)

## Which tumors
Almost every CNS tumor type appears with radiation somewhere in NCI's option lists: brainstem
glioma (radiation is primary), astrocytoma, glioblastoma, oligodendroglioma, ependymoma,
medulloblastoma (brain and spine), craniopharyngioma, meningioma, spinal cord tumors, and
metastases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)

### Whole-brain radiation — a sub-section, not a page
For multiple brain metastases. The important modern point: **hippocampal-avoidance whole-brain
radiotherapy plus memantine** reduces cognitive toxicity versus standard WBRT plus memantine and is
described as a standard of care to offer
(https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.19.02767; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32058845/;
https://www.cancer.gov/news-events/cancer-currents-blog/2018/brain-metastases-radiation-therapy-hippocampal-avoidance).
Publishable as a question to ask: "Can my radiation avoid the memory part of my brain?"

## Tests required before
- Planning CT simulation (with mask)
- Diagnostic contrast MRI, often fused to the planning CT
- Pathology/molecular results (they influence whether radiation is given and when)
- Pregnancy test where applicable; fertility discussion (see page 19)
- Baseline cognitive assessment (variable by center)
- Dental/skin assessment is not usually needed for brain fields but skin condition is noted

## Questions to ask
- What is the goal — cure, control, or symptom relief?
- How many sessions, how many weeks, and how far do I have to travel each day?
- Will I lose my hair, where, and will it come back?
- What will you do about the mask if I'm claustrophobic?
- Am I likely to need steroids during treatment?
- What are the late effects for my treatment area, and how will you check for them?
- Will my hormones be checked afterwards?
- Can I drive myself to treatment?
- Will this affect my ability to have children?

# 6. Proton therapy

## Recommendation: own page, but short, and framed as "a different kind of radiation"

## What it is and how it differs
Protons deposit their energy at a set depth and then stop, rather than passing through the far side
of the head; this reduces dose to surrounding normal tissue
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/).
The clinical rationale is dose reduction to organs at risk that matter for neurologic function,
neurocognition and quality of life
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5080304/).

**The honest framing:** protons are not "stronger" and are not a different intent of treatment.
They aim to reduce collateral dose. Patients routinely believe protons are better at killing tumor.

## Who it is actually offered to
- **Children and young adults**, where long-term normal-tissue sparing matters most; pediatric
  low-grade glioma is the archetype (typical trial eligibility ages 1–25)
  (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01288235;
  https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(19)30162-2/pdf)
- Slow-growing tumors in long-lived patients — "proton therapy may be best positioned to reduce
  toxicity in slow progressing tumors and pediatric patients"
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/107352/html)
- Selected adult low-grade glioma — in the Netherlands, proton therapy is standard insured care for
  selected low-grade glioma patients
  (https://www.thegreenjournal.com/article/S0167-8140(20)31158-0/fulltext)
- In practice also: skull base tumors, craniopharyngioma, and re-irradiation. `[Verify with a
  preferred source before publishing.]`
- Generally excluded: prior radiotherapy to the same site, distant metastases (per trial criteria)
  (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01288235)

## The access reality — this is the section patients actually need
- Roughly **45–50 proton centers operating in the US**, spread across more than 25 states and
  clustered in large metros and academic systems; many patients travel long distances
  (https://www.beckersoncology.com/oncology/45-cancer-centers-with-proton-therapy-in-the-us/;
  https://proton-therapy.org/; https://protonbob.com/about-proton-therapy/us-proton-centers)
- Cost is materially higher than photon radiation (commonly cited as ~60% more)
  (https://scienceinsights.org/where-is-proton-radiation-therapy-available/)
- **Insurance denial is common and is a normal part of the process, not a verdict on your case.**
  Some insurers classify it as investigational; appeals frequently succeed
  (https://www.floridaproton.org/blog-spot/proton-therapy-insurance-coverage;
  https://www.debofsky.com/proton-therapy-insurance-denial/)
- Medicare typically covers proton therapy for eligible people
  (https://scienceinsights.org/where-is-proton-radiation-therapy-available/)
- Practical consequences: relocating for weeks, lodging costs, time off work, childcare —
  BrainHarbor should say this out loud.

## What the patient experience is like
Essentially identical to photon radiation: mask, simulation, daily weekday sessions of about 30
minutes over several weeks
(https://mdproton.com/is-proton-therapy-right-for-me/can-it-treat-my-cancer/brain/)
`[BOUNDARY — "five days a week for several weeks"; session ~30 min]`
Acute side effects (fatigue, skin, hair in the beam path) are similar; the aim is fewer late
effects.

## Which tumors
Pediatric low-grade glioma, pediatric brain tumors generally, medulloblastoma/craniospinal,
craniopharyngioma, selected adult low-grade glioma, skull base tumors.

## Tests required before
Same as photon radiation, plus insurance pre-authorization documentation and often a comparative
photon-vs-proton plan.

## Questions to ask
- Is proton therapy an option for me, and why or why not?
- Would it change my side effects, and which ones?
- Where is the nearest center and how long would I be away from home?
- Will my insurance cover it, and does the center help with appeals?
- Would waiting for proton approval delay starting treatment?

# 7. Stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife, CyberKnife)

## What it is
A single large dose (or a few doses) of radiation aimed very precisely at the tumor. NCI describes
stereotactic radiosurgery as a single large dose of radiation aimed directly at the tumor
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

**The name is the problem.** There is no incision and nothing is cut. Say it in the first line.

## Frame vs frameless
- **Frame-based (classic Gamma Knife):** a lightweight frame is fixed to the skull with pins. The
  neurosurgeon injects local anesthetic at four points — each side of the forehead and the back of
  the head — then the pins are tightened; patients report pressure rather than pain
  (https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/16559-gamma-knife-surgery;
  https://www.froedtert.com/cancer/treatment/radiation/gamma-knife/faq)
- **Frameless (Gamma Knife Icon mask-based, CyberKnife):** a mask instead of pins. Frameless is
  "a pain-free procedure which offers the advantage of better patient compliance by avoiding local
  anesthesia and the discomfort due to wearing the frame"
  (https://journals.lww.com/neurosurgery/fulltext/2020/04000/frameless_stereotactic_radiosurgery_on_the_gamma.7.aspx;
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452109421000944)
- Local control appears similar between frame-based and frameless
  (https://journals.lww.com/neurosurgery/fulltext/2020/04000/frameless_stereotactic_radiosurgery_on_the_gamma.7.aspx)
- CyberKnife is image-guided and frameless, and can spread treatment over 1–5 sessions
  (https://cyberknife.com/brain-tumors/) `[BOUNDARY — session count]`

## Step by step (frame-based Gamma Knife day)
1. Arrive in the morning; usually an outpatient day
   (https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/16559-gamma-knife-surgery)
2. Frame placement under local anesthetic (four injections), pins tightened, pressure sensation
   (https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/16559-gamma-knife-surgery)
3. Imaging with the frame on — MRI and/or CT, sometimes angiography — to locate the target exactly
   (https://www.elekta.com/patients/gamma-knife-treatment/;
   https://www.froedtert.com/cancer/treatment/radiation/gamma-knife/faq)
4. **The wait.** The team builds the plan while the patient waits, still wearing the frame. This
   waiting period is the least-explained part of the day and should be on the page.
5. Treatment: lie on the couch, the head is docked, and radiation is delivered. The patient does not
   see or feel anything (https://www.froedtert.com/cancer/treatment/radiation/gamma-knife/faq)
6. Frame removed; small pin sites; usually home the same day
   (https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/16559-gamma-knife-surgery)
- Whole day typically at least 4–5 hours; the radiation delivery itself ranges from about 15 minutes
  to over an hour depending on complexity
  (https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/procedures/16559-gamma-knife-surgery)
  `[BOUNDARY — day length and treatment length]`
- Relaxing medication can be given if needed
  (https://www.elekta.com/patients/gamma-knife-treatment/)

## Side effects
- Same-day: headache, pin-site soreness and small scabs (frame), nausea, fatigue.
- Later: swelling around the treated spot (may need steroids), and **radiation necrosis** months
  later at the treated site, which can mimic recurrence on scans
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6963489/). LITT is one option used for radiation
  necrosis (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32298824/)
- Hair loss is usually limited or absent because the dose is so focused. `[Verify with a preferred
  source before publishing.]`

## Which tumors
- Brain metastases (single or limited number) — NCI lists SRS for metastatic brain tumors
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)
- Grade 1 meningioma (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)
- Vestibular schwannoma / acoustic neuroma (standard in practice — verify with preferred source)
- Small residual or recurrent tumors after surgery
- Generally not the primary treatment for diffuse gliomas, which infiltrate beyond any target
  volume.

## Tests required before
- High-resolution contrast MRI (stereotactic protocol), sometimes CT and angiography
  (https://www.elekta.com/patients/gamma-knife-treatment/)
- Blood thinner review before frame pinning
- Anesthesia/sedation assessment only if sedation is planned

## Questions to ask
- Frame or mask — which will I have, and why?
- How long will the whole day be, and can someone stay with me?
- Will I need steroids afterwards?
- How will you tell later whether a change on my scan is tumor or treatment effect?
- Can this be repeated if a new spot appears?

# 8. Chemotherapy

## What it is
Drugs that stop cancer cells from growing, given by mouth, into a vein, or placed directly into the
brain or CSF (NCI lists oral, IV, intrathecal and regional delivery to brain tissue or CSF)
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

The barrier that matters for the page: most chemo drugs do not cross into the brain well, which is
why the short list of brain-tumor chemo drugs looks nothing like the list for other cancers.

## The drugs

### Temozolomide (Temodar / TMZ)
- **What it is for:** the standard chemotherapy given with and after radiation for glioblastoma and
  other high-grade gliomas; also used in some grade 2/3 gliomas
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3827088/)
- **How taken:** capsules by mouth at home. `[DO NOT PUBLISH — doses. The clinical facts, for the
  writer only: given daily including weekends during radiation, then in monthly cycles of 5 days on
  / 23 days off for six cycles (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3827088/)]`
- **Common side effects:** loss of appetite, nausea, constipation, low blood counts, hair loss,
  headache, fatigue, seizures, rash
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-temozolomide/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548816/)
- **Blood counts:** myelosuppression (low platelets and low neutrophils) is the dose-limiting
  toxicity, usually in the first few cycles, not cumulative, recovering on average within about 14
  days of the lowest point (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00392171). Low white cells → more
  infection risk; low platelets → bruising/bleeding; low red cells → fatigue and breathlessness
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-temozolomide/)
- **Lymphopenia and infection:** TMZ causes profound lymphopenia
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3601076/). Prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis
  jirovecii* pneumonia is recommended during concurrent temozolomide and radiation and continued
  until lymphocyte counts recover; options include trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, inhaled
  pentamidine, or dapsone
  (https://www.drugs.com/monograph/temozolomide.html;
  https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-024-04588-4). **Patients almost never understand
  why they are on an antibiotic — explain it.** `[DO NOT PUBLISH — the specific prophylaxis doses.]`
- **Anti-nausea support:** anti-nausea medicine is given before each dose to prevent nausea and
  vomiting, which is likeliest on the first day of each cycle
  (https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-temozolomide/;
  https://braintumorcenter.ucsf.edu/about-brain-tumors/treatment/medical-therapy/temozolomide-faqs)
- **Rare:** aplastic anemia (<1%) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548816/); liver injury is
  described in LiverTox (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548816/)

### PCV (procarbazine + lomustine (CCNU) + vincristine)
- **What it is for:** oligodendroglioma (especially 1p/19q co-deleted) and some other gliomas
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2719586/;
  https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/treatment/drugs/pcv)
- **How given:** a combination — procarbazine and lomustine by mouth, vincristine by IV
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/treatment/drugs/pcv)
- **Common side effects:** low blood counts (grade 3 anemia, thrombocytopenia and neutropenia
  reported in 19%, 27% and 46% respectively), vomiting, constipation, peripheral neuropathy,
  abnormal liver enzymes
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7676953/)
- **Vincristine specifically:** peripheral neuropathy (numbness/tingling/weakness in hands and feet)
  and constipation are the characteristic problems
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7676953/)
- **Tolerability is a real issue:** a real-world national series found a full year of PCV "poorly
  tolerated" in low-grade glioma
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7869199/); neurotoxicity often forces stopping
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9540059/)
- **Procarbazine has diet and drug interactions** (MAO-inhibitor-like; alcohol reaction). Confirm
  with a non-AHFS source before publishing; this is a genuine safety item.

### Lomustine (CCNU) alone
- **What it is for:** recurrent glioblastoma; widely used as a comparator/standard in that setting
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305737220300670)
- **How taken:** capsules by mouth, taken infrequently rather than daily
- **The distinctive feature — delayed low blood counts.** Myelosuppression is delayed and
  cumulative, typically 4–6 weeks after a dose, lasting 1–2 weeks; blood counts are monitored for at
  least 6 weeks after each dose and doses are spaced 6+ weeks apart for that reason
  (https://www.cancercareontario.ca/en/drugformulary/drugs/monograph/44171;
  https://reference.medscape.com/drug/gleostine-ccnu-lomustine-342127)
  `[BOUNDARY — "about every six weeks" and "blood counts checked for six weeks after each dose".
  The 6-week gap is arguably a cycle schedule, but the 6-week blood-count monitoring window is a
  safety fact patients need. Owner decision.]`
- **Long-term:** pulmonary fibrosis is a rare but serious late toxicity, typically after 6+ months
  and high cumulative exposure; delayed cases reported many years later after childhood nitrosourea
  plus cranial radiation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9155160/)
- Thrombocytopenia and leukopenia are the dose-limiting toxicities
  (https://reference.medscape.com/drug/gleostine-ccnu-lomustine-342127)

### Carmustine wafers (Gliadel)
- **What it is:** dissolvable wafers containing carmustine, placed by the surgeon into the cavity
  where the tumor was removed, releasing chemotherapy locally
  (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2018/020637s029lbl.pdf;
  https://news.cancerconnect.com/frequently-asked-questions-about-gliadel-wafer-carmustine-implant/)
- **What it is for:** high-grade glioma at initial surgery and recurrent glioblastoma
  (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2018/020637s029lbl.pdf)
- **The wafers dissolve** — more than 70% of the copolymer degrades within about three weeks
  (https://news.cancerconnect.com/frequently-asked-questions-about-gliadel-wafer-carmustine-implant/)
- **Side effects:** brain swelling, seizures, slow or abnormal wound healing, CSF leak, raised
  intracranial pressure, intracranial infection; meningitis occurred in 4% in recurrent glioma
  patients receiving wafers
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9259966/;
  https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2018/020637s029lbl.pdf)
- **Important practical consequence:** wafers can complicate later clinical-trial eligibility and
  make imaging harder to interpret. Its use has declined
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9259966/ — "Is it still an option?"). Flag as an
  area where sources are dated / practice varies.

## Blood-count monitoring — publish as its own section
Blood tests before and during chemo; what "counts are too low" means (treatment delayed, dose
reduced, or a break); the three lines (infection risk, bleeding risk, fatigue)
(https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-temozolomide/);
and the practical rule that **fever during chemo is an emergency call, not a wait-and-see.**

## Anti-nausea support — its own section
Anti-nausea medicine is given routinely, before the chemo, not after nausea starts
(https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/side-effects-brain-tumour-treatment/side-effects-temozolomide/).
Also relevant: depressed patients report chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting more severely, so
mood treatment is part of nausea control
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8886159/).

| Tumor | Chemo typically named |
|---|---|
| Glioblastoma (new) | temozolomide with and after radiation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |
| Glioblastoma (recurrent) | lomustine; carmustine wafers at re-operation (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305737220300670) |
| Anaplastic astrocytoma / grade 3 | temozolomide (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |
| Oligodendroglioma (1p/19q co-deleted) | PCV; temozolomide in some settings (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2719586/) |
| Grade 2 astrocytoma | temozolomide or PCV after radiation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |
| Medulloblastoma | chemotherapy after surgery and craniospinal radiation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |
| Brain metastases | systemic therapy chosen by the primary cancer, not by the brain (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |
| Pediatric low-grade glioma | carboplatin + vincristine has been the standard comparator (https://ascopost.com/news/march-2023/fda-approves-dabrafenibtrametinib-for-pediatric-patients-with-braf-v600e-mutated-low-grade-glioma/) |

## Tests required before
- Full blood count, liver and kidney function
- Pathology and molecular results (MGMT methylation status, IDH, 1p/19q) — these decide whether
  chemo is offered (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)
- Pregnancy test / contraception discussion; fertility discussion (page 19)
- Baseline MRI to compare against

## Questions to ask
- Which drug, and what is it supposed to do for my tumor?
- Pills at home or infusion at the hospital?
- How will you check my blood, and how often?
- What do I do if I get a fever?
- What anti-sickness medicine will I have, and when do I take it?
- Why am I on an antibiotic?
- What would make you stop or change the drug?
- Could this affect my fertility?

# 9. Targeted therapy and other systemic drugs

## What it is, and how it differs from chemo
Chemotherapy attacks dividing cells broadly. Targeted therapy blocks a specific protein or a
specific mutation found in the tumor — which is why the tumor's molecular test result decides
whether it is even an option. NCI groups monoclonal antibodies (e.g. bevacizumab), tyrosine kinase
inhibitors and VEGF inhibitors under targeted therapy
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

**The teachable line:** targeted drugs need a target. If your tumor doesn't have the mutation, the
drug won't help — that is not bad luck about you, it is how the drug works.

## Bevacizumab (Avastin)
- **What it is:** an antibody that blocks VEGF, the signal tumors use to grow new blood vessels
  (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm/about/how-avastin-works.html)
- **What it is for:** recurrent glioblastoma; also used clinically to reduce brain swelling and
  steroid need, and for symptomatic radiation necrosis
  (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm.html;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6136096/)
- **How given:** IV infusion. `[DO NOT PUBLISH — dose/interval.]`
- **Side effects:** high blood pressure is very common (reported in >20% and up to nearly all
  patients depending on series), which can cause headache or blurred vision; nosebleeds; protein in
  the urine; bleeding (including serious bleeding); blood clots (arterial and venous); GI
  perforation; posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome
  (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm/treatment/possible-side-effects.html)
- **The surgery interaction is critical and under-explained:** wounds may heal slowly or not heal;
  bevacizumab should not be used for at least 28 days before or after surgery and until surgical
  wounds are fully healed
  (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm/treatment/possible-side-effects.html)
  `[BOUNDARY — "at least 28 days before and after surgery". This is a safety interval, not a dosing
  schedule. Strong recommendation to publish.]`
- **Imaging caveat worth a line:** because it reduces contrast enhancement, scans can look better
  without the tumor being smaller. `[Verify with a preferred source before publishing.]`
- Source-quality note: the best-structured patient-side-effect list found was the manufacturer site
  (avastin.com). Replace with an NCI/ABTA/NBTS-sourced list before publishing.

## IDH inhibitors — the genuinely recent shift
### Vorasidenib (Voranigo)
- **Approved 6 August 2024** for adults and children 12+ with grade 2 astrocytoma or
  oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1 or IDH2 mutation, **following surgery**
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/;
  https://cancerletter.com/drugs-and-targets/20240809_10a/)
- **First targeted therapy approved for grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma**
  (https://servier.us/blog/serviers-voranigo-vorasidenib-tablets-receives-fda-approval-as-first-targeted-therapy-for-grade-2-idh-mutant-glioma/)
- **Why it matters to patients:** it offers an option between "watch and wait" and "radiation plus
  chemotherapy" for people with grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma who have had surgery — a group that
  previously faced a stark choice. `[Frame carefully; no efficacy figures.]`
- **How taken:** a tablet by mouth once a day, with or without food
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/) `[BOUNDARY — "once a day" is arguably a
  schedule; recommend publishing "a pill taken by mouth every day" as orienting.]`
  `[DO NOT PUBLISH — the 40 mg / 20 mg figures.]`
- **Eligibility detail:** trial population had confirmed grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma, prior surgery
  (biopsy, subtotal or gross total resection), no prior IDH inhibitor, at least one measurable
  lesion (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/)
- **Common side effects (≥15%):** fatigue, headache, COVID-19, musculoskeletal pain, diarrhea,
  nausea, seizure (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/;
  https://www.ajmc.com/view/vorasidenib-approved-by-fda-for-grade-2-idh-mutant-glioma)
- **Liver monitoring is the key practical burden:** hepatotoxicity is a labeled warning; raised
  ALT/AST leads to dose interruption; ~3.3% discontinued permanently, primarily for raised ALT
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/). Publishable as: "you will have regular
  blood tests to check your liver."
- **Drug interaction:** avoid strong/moderate CYP1A2 inhibitors (2.5–7.2× exposure increase)
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/). Publishable as "tell your team about every
  medicine and supplement you take."

### Ivosidenib
- An oral IDH1 inhibitor studied in IDH1-mutant glioma; **not FDA-approved for glioma** and used
  off-label in some centers (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/11/2/199/7317592;
  https://academic.oup.com/noa/article/7/1/vdae227/7928930)
- Vorasidenib was chosen over ivosidenib for phase 3 in glioma because it penetrates the brain
  better and suppresses the target more consistently
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10313524/;
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-022-02141-2)
- **Recommendation:** mention ivosidenib in one sentence only, as "a related drug used mainly for
  other cancers and sometimes off-label," to avoid confusion. It is currently over-searched relative
  to its role in glioma.

## BRAF / MEK inhibitors
- **Dabrafenib + trametinib approved 16 March 2023** for pediatric patients aged 1 year and older
  with low-grade glioma carrying a BRAF V600E mutation who need systemic therapy — the first
  systemic first-line therapy approved in that group; new liquid formulations were approved for
  children who cannot swallow pills
  (https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs/fda-approves-dabrafenib-trametinib-pediatric-patients-low-grade-glioma-braf-v600e-mutation;
  https://ascopost.com/news/march-2023/fda-approves-dabrafenibtrametinib-for-pediatric-patients-with-braf-v600e-mutated-low-grade-glioma/)
- **What they are:** two oral drugs given together; dabrafenib blocks the mutated BRAF protein,
  trametinib blocks MEK downstream of it.
- **Side effects:** fever (pyrexia) carries a specific label warning
  (https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/30/2/263/733187/FDA-Approval-Summary-Dabrafenib-in-Combination);
  rash, fatigue, dry skin, headache, diarrhea are typical of the class. `[Verify the full list from
  the FDA label before publishing.]`
- Also used in BRAF V600E-mutant adult glioma and other CNS tumors in some settings — verify.

## Which tumors → which drug
| Molecular finding | Drug family | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| IDH1/IDH2 mutation, grade 2 astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma, after surgery | vorasidenib | approved (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/) |
| BRAF V600E, pediatric low-grade glioma needing systemic therapy | dabrafenib + trametinib | approved (https://www.fda.gov/drugs/resources-information-approved-drugs/fda-approves-dabrafenib-trametinib-pediatric-patients-low-grade-glioma-braf-v600e-mutation) |
| Recurrent glioblastoma; symptomatic swelling / radiation necrosis | bevacizumab | approved / used (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm.html) |
| Brain metastases | drugs chosen by the primary cancer's biomarkers | (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/) |

## Tests required before
- **Molecular/genetic testing of tumor tissue** — the gate for everything on this page. NCI lists
  gene testing, immunohistochemistry and cytogenetic analysis
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/)
- Liver function tests (vorasidenib) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/)
- Blood pressure and urine protein checks (bevacizumab)
  (https://www.avastin.com/patient/rgbm/treatment/possible-side-effects.html)
- Cardiac assessment for MEK inhibitors (ejection fraction) and eye assessment — verify
- Full medicine and supplement list (interactions)

## Questions to ask
- Has my tumor been tested for IDH, 1p/19q, BRAF and MGMT? Can I have a copy of the report?
- Is there a targeted drug for my tumor's specific change?
- Is this instead of, or as well as, radiation and chemo?
- What blood tests will I need, and how often?
- What should make me call you right away?
- Is it approved for my tumor, or would it be off-label or in a trial?

# 10. Tumor Treating Fields (Optune Gio)

## What it is
A wearable device that delivers low-intensity alternating electric fields through adhesive
transducer arrays placed on the shaved scalp, aiming to disrupt dividing tumor cells. Unlike a drug,
the effect is only expected while the device is switched on
(https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK602919/)

## Who it is for
- **Newly diagnosed supratentorial glioblastoma**, in adults 22 years and older, together with
  temozolomide, after maximal debulking surgery and completion of radiation with concurrent
  chemotherapy (https://www.optunegio.com/; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK602919/)
- **Recurrent glioblastoma** in the supratentorial brain after chemotherapy
  (https://www.optunegio.com/)
- FDA-approved for recurrent GBM in 2011 and newly diagnosed GBM in 2015
  (https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm)
- **Not for** patients with an active implanted medical device, a skull defect with no bone
  replacement, or bullet fragments (https://www.optunegio.com/)

## What wearing it actually involves, day to day
- **The scalp must be shaved close** to place the arrays, then washed, sanitized and dried
  (https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm)
- **Arrays are changed and the scalp re-shaved one to two times per week** to keep good contact
  (https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm) `[BOUNDARY — frequency of array changes. Recommend
  publishing: it is a lived-experience fact, not a prescription.]`
- **Target wear time is at least 18 hours a day**, with monthly compliance >90% encouraged
  (https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm) `[BOUNDARY — the 18 hours. Recommend publishing; it is the
  single most important thing a person deciding about Optune needs to know.]`
- The device is small and portable, under three pounds, with a battery pack carried over the
  shoulder or plugged into a wall outlet; it can be worn at work and socially with the doctor's
  approval (https://www.memorialcare.org/treatments/optune-glioblastoma)
- **A support person matters.** Success is described for people who are motivated to keep the device
  on for the required hours and who have a strong support system to help apply arrays, monitor
  toxicity, and check the scalp (https://virtualtrials.org/optune.cfm)
- The honest sentence BrainHarbor should write: *the burden is visible and constant — a shaved head,
  wires, a bag, and being plugged in at night.*

## Side effects and scalp care
- **Skin under the arrays is the main problem.** Beneath-array dermatologic events are the most
  commonly reported adverse events
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7399624/)
- Prevention/management described in the literature: rotate array positions by 2–3 cm at each
  change; use a topical steroid or a zinc-oxide barrier if redness develops
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7399624/) `[BOUNDARY — "2–3 cm" is a technique
  instruction; recommend publishing as "shift the pads slightly each time" without the measurement.
  Topical steroid mention = a drug instruction; recommend "your team may give you a cream."]`
- Other reported: headache, scalp itch/pain, fatigue, and (for some) heat under the arrays.
- Because skin problems reduce wear time and wear time matters, skin care is not cosmetic — it is
  part of the treatment (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7399624/)

## Which tumors
Glioblastoma only, in the supratentorial brain (https://www.optunegio.com/). Studied in grade 2/3
astrocytoma in trials (https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/48/NCT03906448/ICF_001.pdf) but not
approved there.

## Tests required before
- Confirmed histology and supratentorial location on MRI
- Completion of radiation with concurrent chemo (newly diagnosed indication)
- Check for implanted devices, skull defects, metal fragments (https://www.optunegio.com/)
- Practical assessment: can the patient or caregiver manage the device; home setup; insurance

## Questions to ask
- Would this be instead of, or on top of, my other treatment?
- Do I have to shave my head, and does it stay shaved?
- Who helps me change the pads?
- What do I do about my scalp when it gets sore?
- Can I sleep with it, shower, work, travel?
- What does it cost me, and is it covered?
- What happens if I can't manage the hours?

# 11. Steroids (dexamethasone) — SPLIT OUT as its own page

## Why its own page
Steroids affect almost every brain tumor patient, are started fast and often without explanation,
cause the side effects patients complain about most, and are one of the highest-yield
quality-of-life topics available. Bundling them into a general "supportive care" page buries them.
NCI lists supportive care as including "drugs to control seizures and fluid buildup or swelling in
the brain" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

## What it is and why it's given
Dexamethasone reduces swelling (edema) around a brain tumor, which relieves headache, drowsiness
and focal symptoms. Many patients with glioblastoma take glucocorticoids to reduce brain edema,
which improves function (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6136096/). It does **not**
treat the tumor — say this clearly, because people believe it does.

## Side effects — the under-explained part

> **CORRECTED AT WI-524. The four percentages below are NOT in PMC12406498.**
> That paper contains "proximal" zero times; its only "28" is 28.8%, the share
> of patients with a documented steroid *plan*. The figures are **Sturdza et al
> 2008** (Support Care Cancer 16:1041-8) — 88 patients, one institution, **brain
> metastases**, palliative radiation, 45% of physicians using 16 mg/day —
> reported secondhand by cns.org. The wider literature does not agree with them:
> myopathy is "approximately 10%" (PMC4059813), "2% to 60%" (PMC4483077), "10%
> to 90%" (PMC6007454), 10.6% of 216 primary brain tumor patients and 60% of 15
> heavily dosed ones (both via PMC12948405), and 4.5% at >=12 mg/day
> (PMC6700052). **Publish no frequency**; publish the direction they agree on
> (more likely the higher the dose and the longer the course).
> What PMC12406498 *does* support, and what `/treatments/steroids` uses it for:
> of 316 referrals, 210 were started on a steroid and only 6% had a documented
> weaning plan at that point; complications rose with courses past two weeks and
> with the absence of a written plan.

In brain tumor patient series, the most commonly reported were (Sturdza 2008,
via https://www.cns.org/guidelines/treatment-adults-metastatic-brain-tumors/role-of-steroids-in-treatment-of-adults-with-metas):
- Increased appetite / weight gain — 46%
- **Proximal muscle weakness — 28%** (difficulty rising from a chair or climbing stairs; often
  mistaken by patients for the tumor progressing)
- Insomnia — 24%
- Gastrointestinal symptoms — 20%

Also documented:
- Mood disturbance, anxiety, irritability, agitation, depression, mood swings; neuropsychiatric
  effects including steroid psychosis in roughly 5–10%
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/;
  https://www.ulh.nhs.uk/patients/patient-information-library/taking-dexamethasone-when-you-have-a-brain-tumour/)
- Raised blood sugar, steroid-induced diabetes, thirst and frequent urination
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/;
  https://www.ulh.nhs.uk/patients/patient-information-library/taking-dexamethasone-when-you-have-a-brain-tumour/)
- Increased infection risk from immunosuppression
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/)
- Acne, skin changes, facial swelling ("moon face")
  (https://dipg.org/treatment/steroids/)
- Steroids also contribute to lymphopenia alongside temozolomide
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-024-04588-4)
- Weight gain is largely reversible once steroids are weaned
  (https://dipg.org/treatment/steroids/)

## Practical rules for the page
- **Never stop suddenly.** The dose must be reduced gradually, because the body stops making its own
  steroid and reacts badly to abrupt withdrawal
  (https://www.ulh.nhs.uk/patients/patient-information-library/taking-dexamethasone-when-you-have-a-brain-tumour/)
- Take it earlier in the day where possible to reduce insomnia. `[Verify with a preferred source;
  timing = schedule, flag as boundary.]`
- Stomach protection is commonly prescribed alongside. `[Verify.]`
- Carry a steroid card / tell any other doctor you are on steroids. `[Verify — UK convention;
  confirm US equivalent before publishing.]`
- Report: very high blood sugar symptoms, signs of infection, black stools, severe mood change.
- The goal is the **lowest dose that controls symptoms, for the shortest time** — patients should
  know a taper is expected, not a punishment.
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S104084282200049X)

## Which tumors
Any tumor causing edema or mass effect: high-grade glioma, brain metastases, large meningioma,
posterior fossa tumors; also during radiation if swelling occurs
(https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain)

## Tests required / monitoring
Blood glucose (and HbA1c if prolonged), blood pressure, weight; PJP prophylaxis consideration with
prolonged steroids plus chemoradiation
(https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-024-04588-4)

## Questions to ask
- Why am I on this, and what is it doing?
- How long will I be on it, and what is the plan to come off?
- Which of my new problems are the steroid and not the tumor?
- What do I do about the sleep, the appetite, the mood?
- Do I need my blood sugar checked?
- What must I never do (stop suddenly)?

# 12. Anti-seizure medicine — SPLIT OUT as its own page

## What it is
Medicines to prevent or reduce seizures caused by a brain tumor ("brain tumor-related epilepsy").
NCI includes drugs to control seizures under supportive care
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/).

## Which drugs
Levetiracetam and lacosamide are the most commonly used for brain tumor-related epilepsy, though
there is no clear evidence establishing a single best choice
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10951696/). Levetiracetam is widely used because it is
easy to titrate and has low interaction with cancer treatments
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8787304/), and has shown superiority over valproic acid
for seizure control in glioma-related epilepsy
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10951696/).

## Side effects that patients need warned about
- **Levetiracetam: irritability, agitation, aggression.** Not typically associated with cognitive
  side effects, but patients with pre-existing depression or behavioral problems are at greater risk
  of increased aggression, and suicidality has been reported
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3717617/;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8787304/). **This is the single most valuable warning on
  this page** — families interpret personality change as the tumor, and it may be the drug.
- Also: sleepiness, tiredness, weakness, poor coordination
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3717617/)
- Prophylactic levetiracetam in this population is associated with a higher psychiatric burden
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8787304/)

## Practical topics
- **Preventive use after surgery is often short.** A trial compared 1 week vs 6 weeks of
  levetiracetam in surgical brain tumor patients
  (https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/22/NCT02334722/Prot_SAP_000.pdf) — the publishable
  point is "some people take it only for a short time after surgery; ask which you are."
- **Driving.** Laws vary by state and depend on a seizure-free period; the page must say "ask your
  team and check your state's rule" rather than give a number. No authoritative source found in
  this pass — flag as a gap.
- Never stop suddenly without medical advice.
- Interactions: enzyme-inducing older drugs (phenytoin, carbamazepine) interact with chemotherapy
  and steroids, which is part of why newer drugs are preferred
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8787304/)
- Seizure first aid for the caregiver — a distinct, high-value block.

## Which tumors
Any tumor that has caused a seizure; commonest with low-grade glioma and cortical tumors. Preventive
use after craniotomy is variable practice
(https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/22/NCT02334722/Prot_SAP_000.pdf)

## Tests required
Usually none before starting the newer drugs; EEG sometimes; blood levels for older drugs.

## Questions to ask
- Am I on this to treat seizures I've had, or to prevent ones I haven't?
- How long will I take it?
- Could this be changing my mood or temper?
- Can I drive? What does my state require?
- What should someone do if I have a seizure?

# 13. Shunts and hydrocephalus — SPLIT OUT as its own page

## What it is
When a tumor blocks the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, fluid builds up in the ventricles
(hydrocephalus) and raises pressure in the head. NCI lists drugs for "fluid buildup or swelling in
the brain" under supportive care (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/); when drugs are not
enough the fluid is drained.

- **EVD (external ventricular drain):** a temporary tube from a ventricle to a bag outside the body,
  used in hospital, often around surgery.
- **VP shunt (ventriculoperitoneal shunt):** a permanent internal tube from a ventricle, under the
  skin behind the ear, down the neck and chest, into the abdomen, where the fluid is absorbed. It
  relieves pressure on the brain caused by fluid buildup
  (https://www.healthline.com/health/ventriculoperitoneal-shunt)
- **ETV (endoscopic third ventriculostomy):** an alternative in some obstructive cases. `[Verify
  with a preferred source before publishing.]`

## Step by step (VP shunt)
1. General anesthesia; small shaved area behind/above the ear.
2. A small hole in the skull; catheter passed into the ventricle.
3. A valve sits under the scalp; a tube is tunneled under the skin down to the abdomen.
4. Small incisions at the head, sometimes neck, and abdomen.
5. Typically a short hospital stay; the patient can usually feel the tube under the skin of the
   neck — worth saying, because it surprises people.
6. Programmable valves may be adjusted in clinic with a magnet, and can be affected by strong
   magnets/MRI — settings are checked after MRI. `[Verify.]`

## Risks and the honest part
- Complications include bleeding, infection, anesthesia reactions, under-draining (symptoms return),
  catheter blockage, incision or catheter infection, and abdominal problems
  (https://www.healthline.com/health/ventriculoperitoneal-shunt;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7877257/)
- **Shunts fail.** Overall shunt failure in brain tumor patients with hydrocephalus is 27.8%; 13%
  had a single revision and 14% had multiple revisions
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8976775/). Tumor histology, age and prior procedures
  (ventriculostomy, Ommaya reservoir) predict revision
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8976775/)
- Early failure after craniotomy for tumor is a specific recognized problem
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8827213/)
- **Publishable framing:** "A shunt is not always a one-time fix. Know the signs it isn't working."

## Warning signs to report (the highest-value block on this page)
Return of the original symptoms: headache (especially worse lying down or in the morning), vomiting,
drowsiness, double vision, balance change, confusion; redness, swelling or fluid along the tube;
fever (https://www.healthline.com/health/ventriculoperitoneal-shunt)

## Which tumors
Posterior fossa tumors, medulloblastoma, ependymoma, pineal region tumors, colloid cyst,
intraventricular tumors; also glioblastoma
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6257011/) and after craniotomy in adults
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8827213/)

## Tests required before
CT or MRI showing ventricle size; sometimes CSF sampling; bloods and clotting; anesthesia review.

## Questions to ask
- Why do I need this — is it temporary or permanent?
- Where will the tubing run, and will I feel it?
- What are the signs it has stopped working, and who do I call?
- Does it affect flying, MRI, contact sports, or magnets?
- How often does it need checking?

# 14. Rehabilitation (physical, occupational and speech therapy) — SPLIT OUT as its own page

## What it is
Therapy to recover or compensate for function lost to the tumor or its treatment. Delivered as
multidisciplinary occupational, physical and speech-language therapy, in hospital, in an inpatient
rehab unit, or as outpatient/home therapy
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9410350/)

## What each does
- **Physical therapy:** range of motion, strength, balance and coordination, gait training
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9410350/)
- **Occupational therapy:** dressing, washing, cooking, work tasks, fatigue management, equipment
  and home adaptations.
- **Speech and language therapy:** word-finding, comprehension, reading, and swallowing. Brain tumor
  patients work with SLPs on coping strategies; improvement occurs but difficulties may persist
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- **Neuropsychology / cognitive rehab:** attention, memory, planning.

## When it starts and where
Often the same admission as surgery. Therapy needs are partly predictable by tumor location —
cerebellum → PT/OT for balance; temporal/parietal → speech
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/). Discharge planning
decides home vs. inpatient rehab
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

## Does it work
Yes — studies demonstrate significant positive functional benefit from inpatient neuro-oncological
rehabilitation for primary brain tumor patients
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6168853/), and gains are largely sustained at 6 and 12
months after discharge (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9410350/). Having a caregiver
at home was positively associated with outcome
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9410350/) — a good, humane point to make.
`[The 76.7% / 69.3% GOS figures are outcome statistics — do not publish.]`

## The invisible disabilities section
Attention problems (reduced filtering, distractibility) and word-finding are the commonest
post-surgical complaints; a therapist quoted by NBTS notes "people aren't as patient with them
because it's not apparent they might have challenges"
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/). Practical advice from
the same source: one conversation at a time rather than groups; pace yourself.

## Which tumors
All — need is driven by location and treatment, not tumor type.

## Tests required before
Functional assessment (e.g. FIM), cognitive screening, swallow assessment where relevant.

## Questions to ask
- Do I need rehab, and inpatient or outpatient?
- Who will I see, and how often?
- What is realistic to get back, and by when?
- Will insurance cover it, and for how long?
- What can my family do at home?
- Can I be re-referred later if things change?

# 15. Palliative care — SPLIT OUT as its own page

## The one thing this page exists to say
**Palliative care is not hospice.** It is care to improve quality of life for people with a serious
illness, given *alongside* treatment aimed at the tumor, at any stage, starting at diagnosis
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet).
Hospice is a specific program for people near the end of life who have stopped treatment aimed at
curing or controlling the disease
(https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/hospice-and-palliative-care/what-are-palliative-care-and-hospice-care).
All hospice care is palliative care; not all palliative care is hospice
(https://www.caringinfo.org/types-of-care/what-is-the-difference-between-palliative-care-and-hospice-care/)

## What it covers
Physical symptoms (pain, fatigue, nausea, breathlessness); emotional concerns (depression, anxiety,
fear); spiritual questions; caregiver support and stress; and practical matters — finances,
insurance, legal issues, employment
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)

## Who provides it
A multidisciplinary team — palliative specialists working with oncologists, nurses, therapists,
chaplains, psychologists and social workers
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)

## Where and how to get it
Hospitals, outpatient clinics, long-term care facilities, or at home. Ask your oncologist for a
referral; the Center to Advance Palliative Care and NHPCO keep provider directories by state
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)

## Cost
Private insurance, Medicare Part B and Medicaid cover various palliative services; Medicaid coverage
varies by state
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet)

## Which tumors
All, at any stage.

## Questions to ask
- Can I see the palliative care team while I'm still on treatment?
- Does asking for this mean giving up? (Answer on the page: no.)
- Can they help my family too?
- Who handles my symptoms between appointments?
- Is it covered by my insurance?

# 16. Clinical trials as a treatment option

## Framing rule for BrainHarbor
"Ask your team" — never matching, never recommending a specific trial. NBTS's own framing supports
this: patients should take potential trial options *to* their health care team to discuss
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/diagnosis-treatment/mytumorid/clinical-trials/)

## What a clinical trial is
A study that tests whether a new treatment is safe and works. Trials run in phases
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/diagnosis-treatment/exploring-novel-treatment/):
- **Phase I** — small group (20–80), mainly safety and side effects
- **Phase II** — larger group (100–300), does it work, plus more safety
- **Phase III** — large groups (1,000–3,000), compared against standard treatment
- **Phase IV** — after approval, longer-term safety and best use
`[BOUNDARY — participant counts. These are trial-design facts, not clinical schedules, but they add
numerals. Recommend publishing phase meanings without the counts.]`

## Two myths to kill on the page
- **"A trial is a last resort."** NBTS: talk to your care team about clinical trials as early as
  possible, ideally right at diagnosis; there are five windows of eligibility, sometimes even before
  surgery or before starting treatment
  (https://braintumor.org/news/when-to-consider-a-brain-tumor-clinical-trial/)
- **"I'll get a sugar pill."** NCI: placebos are rarely used in cancer treatment trials; usually one
  group gets the new treatment and another gets the already-approved standard; when a placebo is
  used, patients are always told beforehand, and no one is given a placebo when an effective
  treatment is available (https://www.cancer.gov/research/participate/clinical-trials/how-trials-work)

## Randomization
Randomization avoids bias; neither you nor your doctor chooses which group you are in
(https://www.cancer.gov/research/participate/clinical-trials/how-trials-work)

## Eligibility
Trials specify age, tumor type, grade, biomarkers, and prior treatment
(https://braintumor.org/news/when-to-consider-a-brain-tumor-clinical-trial/). **Critical practical
point: some trials exclude patients who have already had certain treatments — which is exactly why
the conversation should happen before, not after.**

## Practical burden — say it out loud
Extra visits, extra scans, travel to a center, possible extra biopsies, time off work, and costs
that may or may not be covered.

## Tests required before
Confirmed pathology with molecular profile; recent MRI; performance status; organ function bloods;
sometimes archived tissue or a fresh biopsy.

## Questions to ask
- Is there a trial that might be right for me, here or elsewhere?
- What is being tested, and what is the alternative if I don't join?
- Could joining now stop me joining something else later?
- What extra visits, scans or procedures will I have?
- What does it cost me — travel, lodging, time?
- Can I leave the trial, and what happens if I do?
- Who do I call if something goes wrong at 2 a.m.?

**Site links:** NBTS Clinical Trial Finder
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/diagnosis-treatment/stages-of-treatment/clinical-trial-finder/);
NCI clinical trials information
(https://www.cancer.gov/research/participate/clinical-trials/how-trials-work)

# 17. PROPOSED NEW PAGE — Pituitary surgery (transsphenoidal / endoscopic endonasal)

## Why propose it
Pituitary adenoma is one of the commonest brain tumors, and its surgery is nothing like a
craniotomy — no head incision, no shaved head, a nasal recovery, and a hormone follow-up. Putting it
inside the craniotomy page would mislead a large group of readers.

## What it is
The tumor is removed through the nose and sphenoid sinus; a neurosurgeon often works with an ENT
surgeon. No incision on the head, shorter stay and faster recovery than open approaches
(https://www.aurorahealthcare.org/services/neuroscience/brain-skull-base-care/brain-tumor/pituitary-tumor/pituitary-tumor-surgery;
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-endopitsurg.htm)

## Step by step and recovery
- Modern direct transnasal technique minimizes or eliminates nasal packing; many patients can
  breathe through both nostrils immediately, though stents, packing or a gauze dressing under the
  nose may be used and removed at the surgeon's discretion
  (https://ccpd.ucsf.edu/treatment/surgery/faq-about-transsphenoidal-surgery;
  https://www.barrowneuro.org/resource/recovery-guidelines-for-pituitary-patients/)
- Expect nasal congestion, runny nose and a sinus headache improving over a few weeks; fatigue for
  two to three weeks; reduced sense of smell (and therefore taste) for weeks to months
  (https://www.barrowneuro.org/resource/recovery-guidelines-for-pituitary-patients/)
  `[BOUNDARY — recovery week counts.]`
- Nose-blowing and straining restrictions to protect against CSF leak. `[Verify.]`

## Hormones — the part that lasts
Hormone replacement may be needed after surgery, potentially thyroid hormone, cortisol, growth
hormone, estrogen or testosterone; desmopressin or steroid replacement is continued
post-operatively if started before, with electrolyte monitoring; morning cortisol and prolactin are
checked the day after surgery in patients with normal preoperative function
(https://www.aurorahealthcare.org/services/neuroscience/brain-skull-base-care/brain-tumor/pituitary-tumor/pituitary-tumor-surgery;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK556142/)

## Tests required before
Full pituitary hormone panel; **visual field testing** (NCI lists visual field testing among CNS
tumor tests) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/); dedicated pituitary MRI; ENT/sinus
assessment.

## Questions to ask
- Will I need hormone tablets afterwards, and for how long?
- Will my vision improve?
- What are the signs of a fluid leak from my nose?
- When can I blow my nose, fly, lift?

# 18. PROPOSED NEW PAGE (low priority) — Laser ablation (LITT)

## What it is
A thin laser fiber is placed into the tumor through a small hole in the skull under MRI guidance
and heats the tissue to destroy it. Minimally invasive alternative to open craniotomy
(https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/treatment-tests-and-therapies/laser-interstitial-thermal-therapy)

## Who it is for
Recurrent or enlarging enhancing lesions (often after radiosurgery), deep tumor locations, modest
sized lesions (<3 cm), and patients whose other conditions favor a minimally invasive procedure;
also patients who are otherwise high surgical risk
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8633752/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1878875022008804). Commonest indications in a
238-patient single-institution series: glioma 70.2%, radiation necrosis 21.0%, metastasis 8.8%
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32298824/). Patient selection matters — poor baseline performance
status predicted worse outcomes (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32298824/)

## Recommendation
Do **not** build this as a top-level page in v1. Add it as a short section on the surgery page
("other ways of treating a tumor without a big operation") and promote it later if traffic warrants.
Availability is limited to certain centers.

# 19. PROPOSED NEW PAGE (cross-cutting) — Fertility and brain tumor treatment

## Why propose it
It is time-critical (options mostly close once treatment starts), it is systematically missed, and
it cuts across chemo, radiation and surgery pages. ABTA and NBTS both publish on it
(https://www.abta.org/mindmatters/fertility-preservation-for-adolescents-and-young-adults-with-brain-tumors/;
https://braintumor.org/news/navigating-fertility-preservation-a-guide-for-patients-with-brain-tumors/)

## Key content
- Chemotherapy drugs can be gonadotoxic, harming sperm and egg cells, causing temporary or permanent
  infertility; whole-brain or cranial radiation can affect the hypothalamus and pituitary and
  disrupt the hormones needed for fertility; spinal/pelvic radiation can damage reproductive organs
  (https://www.abta.org/mindmatters/fertility-preservation-for-adolescents-and-young-adults-with-brain-tumors/)
- ASCO recommends discussing fertility risk and preservation options with all patients of
  reproductive age **as early as possible** and referring those interested to a reproductive
  specialist
  (https://www.abta.org/mindmatters/fertility-preservation-for-adolescents-and-young-adults-with-brain-tumors/)
- Counseling before treatment reduces long-term regret and improves quality of life afterwards
  (https://www.abta.org/mindmatters/fertility-preservation-for-adolescents-and-young-adults-with-brain-tumors/)
- Referral rates for young adults with primary brain tumors are low, though most who are referred
  do pursue preservation
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-026-05511-9)
- Proton therapy can help avoid dose to hormone-producing tissue
  (https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/blog/2026/05/fertility-and-other-considerations-when-treating-cancer-in-adolescents-and-young-adults.html)

## The one action
"If having children in the future might matter to you, say so before treatment starts — even if you
are not sure."

# 21. Recommended page list and rationale for merges/splits

**Keep as their own pages (from the candidate list):**
1. **Watch and wait** — distinct decision, distinct emotional load, distinct monitoring content.
2. **Biopsy** — different procedure, different risk profile, different purpose from resection; also
   the gateway to molecular testing that drives the targeted-therapy page.
3. **Craniotomy** — the biggest single page. Absorbs GTR/STR/debulking vocabulary, 5-ALA,
   navigation, intraoperative MRI, and location-based risk (including SMA syndrome).
4. **Awake craniotomy and brain mapping** — SPLIT from craniotomy. Justification: the patient has a
   job to do, the fear is specific and misplaced, the preparation (baseline SLP testing) is unique,
   and the risks (intraoperative seizure, conversion) are unique. Merging it would bury exactly the
   content that reduces fear.
5. **Radiation therapy** — hub page. Sections: what it is, consultation, simulation and the mask,
   the planning gap, a day of treatment, fractionation, acute effects, late effects, whole-brain
   radiation, and questions.
6. **Proton therapy** — SPLIT from radiation, but short and explicitly "a kind of radiation."
   Justification: the questions people bring are about *access* (who gets it, where, insurance,
   travel), which do not fit inside a modality page, and readers arrive searching the term directly.
7. **Stereotactic radiosurgery** — SPLIT from radiation. Justification: the name misleads (people
   believe it is surgery), the day is completely different (one day, frame or mask), and it applies
   to a different tumor set (metastases, meningioma, schwannoma).
8. **Chemotherapy** — one page, four drugs/regimens, with a comparison block. Do **not** split into
   per-drug pages at launch: it fragments the shared content (blood counts, nausea, fever rules)
   which is where the practical value is.
9. **Targeted and other systemic drugs** — one page. Lead with "why your tumor's test result decides
   this," then bevacizumab, IDH inhibitors, BRAF/MEK.
10. **Tumor Treating Fields (Optune Gio)** — own page. Justification: it is a lived-experience
    decision (18 hours a day, shaved head, caregiver dependency), not a clinical one, and no other
    page's structure fits that.
16. **Clinical trials** — own page, framed as "ask your team."

**Split "supportive and symptom care" into five pages (11–15).** Rationale: it was the largest
candidate by far and each part has a different audience, a different moment, and a different reader
question. Steroids and anti-seizure medicine in particular are daily-life issues that get lost in an
omnibus page, and both contain a high-value "this might be the drug, not the tumor" message that
deserves a URL of its own. Keep a light "Managing symptoms and side effects" index page that links
all five, so tumor pages can link once.

**Proposed additions:** pituitary surgery (17), laser ablation as a *section* not a page (18),
fertility (19).

**Explicitly NOT recommended as separate pages:**
- Whole-brain radiation → section of the radiation page (only relevant to metastases).
- IMRT / conformal radiation → sections of the radiation page; they are planning techniques.
- Individual chemo drugs → sections.
- Immunotherapy / vaccines / CAR-T → not yet; almost entirely trial-stage in CNS tumors. Point to
  the clinical trials page instead.

## Proposed section order (applies to every treatment page)
1. What it is, in one sentence
2. Why it might be offered to you
3. What happens, step by step
4. How long it takes / how long recovery takes
5. What it feels like
6. Side effects — soon
7. Side effects — later
8. What tests you need first *(links into the tests library)*
9. Questions to ask your team
10. Related pages

# 23. Where sources disagreed, or looked outdated

1. **Radiation session length.** MSK says 15–60 minutes in the room; NBTS says the appointment is
   15–30 minutes. Both agree the beam is a few minutes. Resolve by describing the shape of the visit
   rather than a number. (https://www.mskcc.org/cancer-care/patient-education/radiation-therapy-brain
   vs https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-radiation-therapy-as-a-patient-with-a-brain-tumor/)
2. **Staple/suture removal timing.** "7 to 10 days" (UMass) vs "10–14 days, or 14–21 for a repeat
   incision" (UCLA). Publish a range and defer to the surgeon.
   (https://www.ummhealth.org/health-library/discharge-instructions-for-craniotomy;
   https://www.uclahealth.org/sites/default/files/documents/Patient-Discharge-Craniotomy071117.pdf)
3. **Intraoperative seizure rate in awake craniotomy.** Reported 2.9%–54% across a multicenter
   series; commonly quoted 3.4–20%; meta-analysis pooled ~8%. Enormous spread — do not publish a
   number. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7713838/;
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4882028/)
4. **Carmustine wafers may be effectively deprecated.** A 2022 paper is literally titled "Carmustine
   Wafers Implantation in Patients With Newly Diagnosed High Grade Glioma: Is It Still an Option?"
   and a 9-year national retrospective examines declining use. Much of the accessible patient
   material about Gliadel is old. **Recommendation: mention it briefly and neutrally as "used in
   some places, less often than before," and re-verify current practice before publishing.**
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9259966/;
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31974802/)
5. **Frame vs frameless SRS.** Literature is actively arguing whether to "abandon the frame."
   Practice varies by center and by indication. Do not state one is standard.
   (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2452109421000944)
6. **NCI PDQ patient summary vocabulary is behind WHO 2021.** It still uses "anaplastic
   astrocytoma" and organizes by older histologic terms, whereas current practice is
   molecular-first (IDH status defines astrocytoma vs glioblastoma). BrainHarbor's tumor pages must
   use WHO 2021 terms, and the treatment pages must match them.
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK66023/ vs the vorasidenib approval framing at
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/)
7. **NCI PDQ does not yet reflect vorasidenib** in the summary text retrieved. The IDH-inhibitor
   shift (Aug 2024) is the single biggest recent change for grade 2 glioma patients and is the most
   likely thing to be missing from any older page they read elsewhere. This is a differentiator for
   BrainHarbor — and a maintenance risk.
8. **Proton center counts differ** (45 vs 50 vs "over 45"). Publish "about 50 centers in the US" or
   avoid the count and link the National Association for Proton Therapy directory.
   (https://www.beckersoncology.com/oncology/45-cancer-centers-with-proton-therapy-in-the-us/;
   https://proton-therapy.org/)
9. **Lomustine pulmonary fibrosis.** Described as a real risk in drug monographs and case series,
   but one review notes it "has not been documented to be a toxicity of concern in clinical trials
   where toxicity was carefully documented for adult glioblastoma patients." Phrase carefully.
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9155160/)
10. **PJP prophylaxis during chemoradiation.** Recommended in monographs, but a recent systematic
    review found a pooled PJP incidence of only 0.74% and could not establish an association with
    lymphopenia. Practice is not uniform. Publish "some teams give an antibiotic to prevent a lung
    infection — ask yours why."
    (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2589537025006492;
    https://www.drugs.com/monograph/temozolomide.html)
