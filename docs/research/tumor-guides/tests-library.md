# Tests and Workup Library — Research Dossier

Research for the BrainHarbor "Tests and Workup" standalone page library.
Compiled 2026-08-30. Every substantive claim carries a source URL.

## Editorial ground rules applied while gathering

- **No prognosis figures.** Survival numbers were encountered repeatedly in the
  literature (especially around MGMT and IDH) and have been deliberately
  excluded from this dossier so they cannot leak into drafts.
- **Meaning, not verdict.** For every molecular marker below, this dossier
  records *what the word means* and *what the lab physically measures*. It does
  **not** record which result is "the good one." Several otherwise excellent
  sources do exactly that (see §12, Source conflicts) — those framings are
  flagged, not reproduced.
- **Licensing.** No AHFS or MedlinePlus monograph content. No NCI embedded
  images. Preference given to NCI/cancer.gov, ACS, Radiologyinfo.org, ACR,
  NCCN, WHO CNS5 / EANO / CAP guideline literature, and patient organisations
  (ABTA, National Brain Tumor Society).

# 1. MRI, with and without contrast

## 1.1 What it is

A brain MRI uses "a powerful magnetic field, radio waves and a computer to
produce pictures of the brain," and produces clearer, more detailed pictures of
the brain than other imaging methods. It does not use ionising radiation.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)

MRI is described by the American Cancer Society as "the best test for looking
for brain and spinal cord tumors," and is typically done as two scans — one
before contrast and one after a gadolinium injection.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

## 1.2 Why "with and without" is standard

The neuro-oncology field runs a standardised **Brain Tumor Imaging Protocol
(BTIP)**. Its minimum requirement is *parameter-matched* pre-contrast and
post-contrast isotropic 3D T1-weighted images — i.e. the "before" and "after"
scans are deliberately acquired with identical settings.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10751345/;
https://www.ajnr.org/content/36/10/E65)

Matching them allows a **T1-subtraction map**: the pre-contrast image is
digitally subtracted from the post-contrast image so that only the tissue that
actually took up contrast remains, which raises the contrast-to-noise ratio of
enhancing tissue and improves measurement of the enhancing part of the tumour.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10751345/)

Standardisation exists because "heterogeneity in MR scanner systems and pulse
sequence parameters can result in dramatic differences in perceived image
contrast," which makes scans hard to compare over time and between centres.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10751345/)

Plain-language translation for the page: the "without" scan is the baseline
picture of the brain's own signal; the "with" scan shows where the dye leaks
out of blood vessels. You need both, taken the same way, or the comparison
between today's scan and the next one is not trustworthy.

## 1.3 What the patient actually experiences, start to finish

- **Screening desk.** The facility runs a metal/implant screening before the
  patient is allowed near the magnet. Everything metallic must come off:
  jewellery, watches, hearing aids, clothing with metal fibres, coins, keys,
  hair accessories, and anything with a magnetic strip.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)
- **Clothing.** Loose comfortable clothing; jewellery left at home.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
- **IV.** If contrast is planned, an IV line is placed in the arm or hand.
  (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
- **Position.** The patient lies on a table that slides into a long tube; a coil
  (a frame that looks like a cage or helmet) is placed around the head. The
  patient is alone in a specially shielded room during the exam.
  (https://www.touchstoneimaging.com/what-should-i-expect-before-during-and-after-a-brain-mri-scan/)
- **Noise.** The scanner is loud throughout — banging, buzzing, thumping and
  clicking produced by rapidly switching magnetic gradients. Earplugs or
  headphones are supplied.
  (https://www.touchstoneimaging.com/what-should-i-expect-before-during-and-after-a-brain-mri-scan/;
  https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)
- **Stillness.** "High-quality images depend on your ability to remain perfectly
  still while the images are being recorded."
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
- **Contact.** The technologist watches continuously, can talk through an
  intercom, and gives the patient a call button.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)
- **Contrast injection.** Given partway through, through the IV. Possible brief
  warmth or a metallic taste that fades quickly.
  (https://www.touchstoneimaging.com/what-should-i-expect-before-during-and-after-a-brain-mri-scan/)
- **Afterwards.** No recovery period for a standard MRI; normal activity can
  usually resume immediately.
  (https://www.touchstoneimaging.com/what-should-i-expect-before-during-and-after-a-brain-mri-scan/)

## 1.4 How long

- Brain MRI without contrast: roughly **20–30 minutes** of scanning.
- Brain MRI with contrast: roughly **45–60 minutes**, including the injection
  and the extra post-contrast sequences.
- Total appointment including check-in and preparation: roughly **45–90
  minutes**.
  (https://www.touchstoneimaging.com/what-should-i-expect-before-during-and-after-a-brain-mri-scan/)

## 1.5 Claustrophobia and what can be done

- Reported rates vary widely by study: some series report ~15% of patients with
  severe claustrophobic reactions, ~13% reporting panic symptoms and ~1% unable
  to complete the scan; other work reports an overall ~10% claustrophobic event
  rate; one multi-centre service evaluation found incomplete examinations due to
  claustrophobia in **0.76%** of scans.
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41004835/;
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1078817422000190)
- Interventions actually used in MRI departments: **mild oral sedation**
  (usually a benzodiazepine), **supportive instruction/coaching from the
  radiographer**, **music**, and **virtual reality**.
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S107881742300175X)
- Radiologyinfo explicitly tells patients they may **request mild sedation** if
  they are claustrophobic or anxious — this is a thing you are allowed to ask
  for in advance, not an emergency measure.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
- Sedation is not free of downside: it carries risks including respiratory
  depression and prolonged recovery and is not suitable for every patient.
  (https://craftbodyscan.com/blog/claustrophobia-and-mri-your-complete-guide-to-comfortable-scanning-options/)
- Scanner design matters. In a comparative study, patients in open upright MRI
  reported less claustrophobia than in closed systems (18.4% vs 58.3%), fewer
  premature terminations (5.3% vs 31.0%) and less sedative use (5.3% vs 46.9%).
  A separate randomised controlled trial compared short-bore versus open MRI for
  claustrophobia reduction.
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S107881742400172X;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3161742/)
  **Caution for the page:** open/upright scanners are generally lower field
  strength and are not equivalent for brain-tumour protocol imaging — frame this
  as "ask whether your centre has options," not "ask for an open MRI."
- Anxiety-disrupted scans are a real system-level problem, with an estimated
  ~2 million MRI scans disrupted per year by anxiety, causing early termination
  and motion-degraded images.
  (https://craftbodyscan.com/blog/claustrophobia-and-mri-your-complete-guide-to-comfortable-scanning-options/)

## 1.6 Gadolinium contrast — the questions people actually ask

**"Is it like the CT dye? Will I be allergic?"**
Gadolinium does not contain iodine and "rarely causes an allergic reaction or
other problem," unlike x-ray/CT contrast.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)

**"What about my kidneys?"**
Patients should tell the technologist about kidney disease, kidney failure,
kidney transplant or liver disease before gadolinium is given, and gadolinium
may be withheld in severe kidney disease.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
The historical concern is **nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF)**. Current
evidence: for the lowest-risk "**group II**" agents (gadobenate dimeglumine,
gadoteridol, gadoterate meglumine, gadobutrol) the risk of NSF is very low —
zero events after 4,931 administrations in patients with eGFR <30, with 95% CI
upper bounds of 0.07% overall. ACR/NKF consensus deemed kidney-function
screening before group II agents optional.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590059520302181;
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33170103/;
https://www.acr.org/Clinical-Resources/Clinical-Tools-and-Reference/Contrast-Manual)

**"Does it stay in my brain?"**
Yes, traces do. "There is evidence that tiny traces of gadolinium may be
retained in different organs of the body, including the brain, after
contrast-enhanced MRI. While there are no known negative effects from this,
your doctor may take gadolinium retention into account when selecting a contrast
agent."
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
The FDA required a **class-wide warning and a patient Medication Guide** about
gadolinium retention, which patients are asked to read before receiving a
gadolinium agent. Gadolinium can be found in the body, including the brain, for
months to years. Retention has **not** been directly linked to adverse health
effects in patients with normal kidney function, and FDA concluded the benefit
of all approved agents continues to outweigh potential risks.
(https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/news-events/fda-safety-communications-labeling-changes/fda-warns-gadolinium-based-contrast-agents)
Chemistry matters: **linear** agents retain more gadolinium in the brain, and
for longer, than **macrocyclic** agents, because linear open-chain ligands are
less stable and more prone to releasing gadolinium ions. FDA encourages
providers to consider retention characteristics particularly for children,
pregnant patients, patients with inflammatory conditions, and **patients who
will require many doses over their lifetime** — which describes a brain-tumour
patient on lifelong surveillance exactly.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6158336/;
https://www.sentinelinitiative.org/news-events/fda-safety-communications-labeling-changes/fda-warns-gadolinium-based-contrast-agents)

**"I'm pregnant / might be."**
There is no known fetal risk from MRI itself, and MRI is usually safer for the
fetus than x-ray or CT; most MRI centres avoid contrast agents in pregnancy out
of an abundance of caution.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)

## 1.7 Implants, devices and screening

Items that must be disclosed: pacemakers and implantable cardioverter
defibrillators, ferromagnetic aneurysm clips (typically a contraindication),
neurostimulation systems, cochlear implants, implanted or external medication
pumps, and metallic fragments such as bullets, shrapnel or metal near the eyes.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)

Important and under-communicated: **many modern devices are MRI-compatible.**
"Some items, including newer cardiac pacemakers, ICDs, neurostimulation systems,
cochlear implants, and medication pumps, are acceptable for MRI" — provided the
technologist knows the **exact device specifications**. The practical patient
action is to bring the device card / model number.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/safety-mr)

Other stated risks and limitations: implanted medical devices may malfunction;
scanners have weight limits; implants and metal degrade image quality; MRI costs
more and takes longer than the alternatives.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)

## 1.8 Results — who and how long

"A radiologist, a doctor trained to supervise and interpret radiology exams,
will analyze the images" and sends a report to the ordering provider.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
For brain tumours, the scan is commonly re-reviewed at a **multidisciplinary
tumour board**, where neuro-oncology, neurosurgery, radiation oncology,
neuropathology, neuroradiology and neuropsychology review imaging, tissue and
biomarker results together. Board review improves interpretation compared with
reading the written report alone, and in one series was associated with a change
in treatment in 40.5% of cases discussed.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8875699/;
https://braintumor.org/news/brain-tumor-board-101-answers-to-frequently-asked-questions/)

## 1.9 Which treatments require it first

- **Surgery:** a pre-operative MRI is standard before craniotomy.
  (https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
- **Neuronavigation ("stealth") scan:** a *separate*, thin-slice
  (~1.5 mm) contrast MRI, often with 7–10 adhesive fiducial markers stuck on the
  scalp, done as close to surgery as possible — commonly the day before —
  because the markers fall off. Data is transferred by DICOM to the navigation
  workstation in theatre.
  (http://www.neurosurgeryresident.net/Op.%20Operative%20Techniques/030.%20Neuronavigation/Op30.%20Neuronavigation.pdf;
  https://www.ovid.com/jnls/neur/fulltext/02223311-200250030-00003~neuronavigation-concept-techniques-and-applications)
  This is a genuinely confusing patient moment: "I just had an MRI, why another
  one?" Worth an explicit paragraph.
- **Early post-operative MRI:** used to measure how much enhancing tumour
  remains and to set the baseline for all future comparisons. Traditionally
  within 72 hours; more recent evidence supports a **48-hour** window, because
  surgically induced (reactive) enhancement becomes more common after 48 hours —
  thin linear enhancement rose from 50.8% within 48 h to 69.1% beyond 48 h, and
  scans with no enhancement fell from 22.4% to 8.6%.
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9955136/;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8392564/;
  https://thejns.org/focus/view/journals/neurosurg-focus/37/6/article-pE3.xml)
- **Radiotherapy planning** and **surveillance** — see §10.

## 1.10 Which tumour types

All of them. MRI is the primary imaging test for gliomas, meningiomas, pituitary
tumours, vestibular schwannomas, brain metastases and paediatric tumours.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

**Spine MRI is part of staging for some tumours.** For posterior fossa tumours
such as medulloblastoma, a **pre-operative** spine MRI is preferable, because
surgically induced blood/inflammation afterwards is hard to distinguish from
tumour; protocols require MRI of the entire spine with and without gadolinium
either ≤7 days before surgery or within 28 days after.
(https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/14/NCT01063114/Prot_SAP_000.pdf;
https://www.ajnr.org/content/21/9/1757)

## 1.11 Questions patients should ask

- Is this scan with contrast, without, or both — and why?
- Which gadolinium agent do you use here (linear or macrocyclic)?
- I have [device]. Here is the model card — is it MRI-safe, and does the setting
  need changing?
- I get very anxious in enclosed spaces. What can you offer — coaching, music,
  a mild sedative prescribed in advance, a different scanner?
- If I get a sedative, can I drive home?
- How long will the scan take, and how long will I need to hold still at a time?
- When will the result be ready, who will call me, and will it go to a tumour
  board before I hear?
- Can I get a copy of my images on a disc/portal for a second opinion?

# 2. CT scan

## 2.1 What it is and when it is used instead

CT "makes a series of detailed pictures of areas inside the body."
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
For brain tumours, CT is "not quite as good as MRI" but is used when MRI is
unavailable or when imaging is needed immediately; CT also gives better bone
detail, and CT angiography can be better than MR angiography in some vascular
situations.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

ABTA's framing is the one most patients recognise: "A CT is often used to detect
the presence of a tumor, and an MRI is used to gather more detailed information
about the tumor's size, location, and possible type."
(https://www.abta.org/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-diagnosis/)

This is the emergency-room story most readers lived through: seizure or sudden
headache → CT in the ER → "there's something there" → MRI later for detail.

## 2.2 What the patient experiences

- Lying on a table that moves through a ring-shaped scanner; the exam is
  painless, though there may be discomfort from holding still and from IV
  placement.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/headct)
- If iodinated contrast is used: a pin prick for the IV, then a feeling of
  warmth or flushing, a metallic taste, and sometimes an urge to urinate — all
  short-lived.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/headct)

## 2.3 How long

The CT exam is usually completed **within 30 minutes**; the portion needing the
contrast injection usually lasts less than a minute. The actual head scan itself
is a matter of seconds to a couple of minutes.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/headct)

## 2.4 Preparation, risks, discomforts

- If iodinated contrast is used, the patient is screened for chronic or acute
  kidney disease.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/headct)
- Serious allergic reaction to iodinated contrast is extremely rare, and
  radiology departments are equipped to treat it.
  (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/headct)
- CT uses ionising radiation. A head CT with contrast has been quoted at about
  **4 mSv**, roughly 16 months of natural background radiation.
  (https://www.webmd.com/cancer/radiation-doses-ct-scans) — *see §12, this
  figure varies between sources and should be given as a range or omitted.*

## 2.5 Where CT still leads

- Emergency assessment (bleeding, acute hydrocephalus, herniation).
- Bone detail — skull base, bone invasion, surgical approach planning.
  (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
- Radiotherapy planning (CT simulation) and PET/CT hybrid imaging.
- When MRI is contraindicated (incompatible device) or impossible (patient
  cannot tolerate the scan).
- Immediate post-operative check for bleeding.

## 2.6 Questions to ask

- Why CT rather than MRI today?
- Will I still need an MRI?
- Is contrast being used, and have you checked my kidney function?
- How much radiation is this, and how many of these will I have?

# 3. Advanced imaging for surgical planning

These are add-on sequences and separate scans. Most are ordered by the
neurosurgeon or the radiation oncologist to answer a specific question, not
routinely.

## 3.1 Functional MRI (fMRI)

**What it is.** fMRI identifies the brain regions that handle "speech, thought,
sensation, or movement" while the patient performs specific tasks during the
scan.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
It is a safe, non-invasive technique used for pre-operative assessment before
brain-tumour surgery.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844025008448;
https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/10.1148/rycan.230078)

**What the patient does.** This is the key experiential difference — the patient
has *jobs* inside the scanner:
- Motor tasks: **finger tapping**, tongue movement, foot tapping, chosen to
  target different parts of the motor strip; finger tapping is typical for
  tumours near the hand-knob area.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757221/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8160093/)
- Language tasks: **sentence completion**, **silent word generation**, simple
  word reading, or **verb generation**. The American Society of Functional
  Neuroradiology publishes recommended paradigm algorithms for pre-surgical
  language assessment.
  (https://www.ajnr.org/content/38/10/E65;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757221/)
- Tasks are usually practised beforehand, and patients who struggle with a
  complex task are switched to a simpler one, which yields more reliable
  results.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757221/)
- Some protocols add a **breath-hold** run to check blood-vessel reactivity.
  (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373788317_Detection_and_Mitigation_of_Neurovascular_Uncoupling_in_Brain_Gliomas)

**Limits patients should know.** fMRI can be misleading near a tumour because of
**neurovascular uncoupling** — the tumour disturbs the normal link between nerve
activity and local blood flow, so a working brain region can fail to light up.
Breath-hold cerebrovascular reactivity mapping is used to detect this risk.
(https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373788317_Detection_and_Mitigation_of_Neurovascular_Uncoupling_in_Brain_Gliomas)
fMRI is a planning map, not the final word: intraoperative direct cortical
stimulation during awake surgery remains the reference standard for mapping.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4757221/)
It also requires cooperation — sedation defeats the purpose.

## 3.2 DTI / tractography

**What it is.** Diffusion tensor imaging maps "major pathways (tracts) of white
matter (nerve fibers)" to guide surgical planning.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
Diffusion MRI tractography is a non-invasive technique that visualises and
quantifies white-matter tracts and is extensively used in pre-operative planning
for brain tumours, epilepsy and functional neurosurgery.
(https://journals.lww.com/investigativeradiology/fulltext/2024/01000/advancements_in_diffusion_mri_tractography_for.2.aspx)

**What it adds.** Pre-operative DTI shows one of five patterns of white-matter
tract involvement — **non-affected, displaced, edematous, infiltrated,
disrupted** — and these are used to plan the surgical corridor and how far to
resect.
(https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s43055-021-00490-5;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8050646/)

**Patient experience.** Nothing extra to do — it is additional scan time on the
MRI table (no tasks, no separate injection). Chief cost is more minutes of lying
still.

## 3.3 MR spectroscopy (MRS)

**What it is.** "An MRS is used to diagnose tumors, based on their chemical
make-up."
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
It measures biochemical changes in an area of the brain and displays them as a
graph-like spectrum, helping suggest tumour type and growth rate.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

**What it measures.** MRS detects metabolites present at high enough
concentration: **choline (Cho)** (cell-membrane turnover), **creatine (Cr)**
(energy reference), **N-acetyl aspartate (NAA)** — found essentially only in
neurons and used as a marker of healthy neurons — plus lactate, myo-inositol,
glutamine/glutamate, lipids and some amino acids.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8272438/;
https://www.asfnr.org/what-is-magnetic-resonance-spectroscopy)

**A specialised use worth naming.** **2-hydroxyglutarate (2HG)** accumulates in
IDH-mutant tumours and can be detected by MRS as a non-invasive marker of that
mutation, with reasonable accuracy; a meta-analysis using individual patient
data assessed 2HG-MRS for predicting IDH-mutant glioma.
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/20/12/1573/5055433;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477829/)
Technically hard: 2HG overlaps spectrally with background metabolites, so most
studies used spectral-editing methods with long echo times rather than the
routine short-TE approach — meaning it is not available everywhere.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4731570/)

**Patient experience.** Extra minutes inside the same scanner; no tasks.

## 3.4 Perfusion imaging

**What it is.** Perfusion MRI shows "blood going through different parts of the
brain," and tumours typically show a higher blood supply than normal tissue.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
Clinically the two main flavours are **dynamic susceptibility contrast (DSC)**
and **dynamic contrast enhancement (DCE)**.
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/15820/html)
Perfusion-weighted imaging aids grading, differentiation and pre-treatment
planning.
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17505814/)

**Patient experience.** Requires a contrast injection, often given as a rapid
bolus by power injector, and the patient must hold especially still during a
fast series of images.

**Limit worth stating plainly.** Perfusion is one of the main tools used to try
to separate treatment change from tumour growth (§10), but "MRI-based perfusion
and spectroscopy or brain FDG-PET scans cannot reliably distinguish tumor
progression from pseudoprogression."
(https://ascopost.com/issues/august-15-2012/understanding-and-managing-pseudoprogression-in-glioblastoma-patients/)

## 3.5 PET, including amino-acid PET

**FDG-PET.** A PET scan is "a procedure to find malignant tumor cells in the
body" using radioactive glucose.
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
It shows metabolic activity rather than anatomy, highlighting areas using more
energy, and is used to help tell tumour from scar tissue after treatment; it
works better for high-grade tumours.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
Limitation: normal brain uses a lot of glucose, so tumour can be hard to see
against background.

**Amino-acid PET.** The most widely used amino-acid tracers are **18F-FET**,
**11C-methionine (11C-MET)** and **18F-FDOPA**.
(https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/64/5/693)
Joint practice guidelines/procedure standards for glioma PET with radiolabelled
amino acids and FDG were developed by EANM, SNMMI, EANO and the PET-RANO working
group.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6351513/;
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30519867/)
**PET RANO 1.0** (gliomas) and **PET RANO BM 1.0** (brain metastases) criteria
now standardise PET-based monitoring with amino-acid tracers, and the RANO/EANO
recommendations for clinical use of PET in gliomas were updated in 2025.
(https://jnm.snmjournals.org/content/64/5/693;
https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(25)00193-7/fulltext)
Typical clinical questions it is ordered for: where to biopsy, tumour extent
beyond contrast enhancement, and treatment change vs recurrence.
(https://www.ajnr.org/content/44/3/236;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213158216302571)
Availability is the real constraint — amino-acid PET is concentrated in academic
centres, especially in the US.

**SSTR / DOTATATE PET for meningioma.** Nearly all meningiomas express
somatostatin receptor 1/2, so SSTR ligands such as **68Ga-DOTATATE** are used to
plan management. There is a joint EANM/SNMMI/EANO/PET-task-force practice
guideline for SSTR-ligand imaging and therapy of meningiomas, and consensus
radiation treatment-planning guidelines using 68Ga-DOTATATE PET/CT for resected
meningiomas. DOTATATE PET can detect residual or additional disease not visible
on MRI, particularly at the skull base and in recurrence.
(https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(24)03726-X/abstract;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8365988/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0360301622003224)

**SPECT** also appears in NCI's list of tests as "a procedure to find malignant
tumor cells in the brain" using an injected radioactive substance.
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)

**PET patient experience (generic).** IV tracer injection; a quiet uptake wait
(commonly ~20–60 minutes depending on tracer) in a dim room, ideally without
talking or moving; then scanning on a PET or PET/CT scanner. Fasting may be
required for FDG; amino-acid tracers have their own prep. Radiation exposure
comes from the tracer plus any CT component.
(General procedural framing: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6351513/)

## 3.6 MRA / MRV and angiography

MR angiography and MR venography visualise arteries and veins before an
operation.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
Catheter angiography (and pre-operative embolisation) may be used for very
vascular tumours such as some meningiomas.

## 3.7 Questions to ask about advanced imaging

- What specific question is this extra scan answering?
- Will it change the operation, or the radiation plan?
- Do I have to do anything during the scan (tasks, holding my breath)?
- Does it need another injection of contrast or a radioactive tracer?
- Is this available here, or do I need to travel for it?
- Is it covered by my insurance?

# 4. Biopsy and what the lab does

## 4.1 Types of biopsy

**Stereotactic (needle) biopsy.** "Uses a computer and a 3-dimensional scanning
device to find the tumor and guide the needle."
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
Performed under general or local anaesthesia with imaging guidance; the surgeon
drills a small hole in the skull and inserts a hollow needle into the tumour to
remove small tissue samples.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
NCCN patient material describes it as "a thin, hollow needle is inserted into
the brain to remove a sliver of the tumor."
(https://www.nccn.org/patients/guidelines/content/PDF/brain-gliomas-patient.pdf)
It can be **frame-based** (a rigid frame fixed to the head) or **frameless**
(navigation-guided). A meta-analysis found no significant difference in
diagnostic yield between the two systems, and navigation-guided biopsy is as
effective as frame-based without additional complications.
(https://surgicalneurologyint.com/surgicalint-articles/frame-based-versus-frameless-stereotactic-brain-biopsies-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis/;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10219353/)
Frameless biopsy can be done under local anaesthesia; in one 80-patient series
diagnosis was confirmed in 76 cases (95.0%).
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560739/)

**Open biopsy / craniotomy.** Involves a craniotomy where "a part of the skull is
removed" to obtain tissue.
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
When removal of the tumour is feasible, tissue is taken during that operation
rather than as a separate procedure.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

## 4.2 Diagnostic yield and risks

- Diagnostic yield for stereotactic biopsy is reported between **88.9% and
  99.7%**, with 100% in some frameless series.
  (https://ruralneuropractice.com/diagnostic-yield-and-complication-of-frameless-stereotactic-brain-biopsy/;
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560739/)
- Bleeding is the main risk, and most of it is silent. One series reported
  asymptomatic haemorrhage in 17.5% of patients with **no** symptomatic
  haemorrhage; another reported post-operative intracerebral haematoma in 2.4%,
  not symptomatic. Rates vary widely between series and definitions.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560739/;
  https://ruralneuropractice.com/diagnostic-yield-and-complication-of-frameless-stereotactic-brain-biopsy/;
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32874721/;
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31900737/)
- New neurological deficit or death occurred in about 1 in 90 (1.1%) in one
  series.
  (https://ruralneuropractice.com/diagnostic-yield-and-complication-of-frameless-stereotactic-brain-biopsy/)
- Practical framing for readers: a small, real risk of bleeding, a small risk of
  a new neurological problem, and a small chance the sample does not give an
  answer and has to be repeated.

## 4.3 What the patient experiences

- Pre-op: navigation MRI/CT (often with scalp fiducials, §1.9), fasting per ASA
  rules (§6), consent conversation.
- If frame-based: the frame is fixed to the skull with pins under local
  anaesthetic — pressure and a sharp sting, then the odd sensation of the frame,
  then a scan wearing the frame.
- Anaesthesia: general, or local plus sedation depending on approach.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560739/)
- A small shaved patch, a small incision, a small hole in the skull ("burr
  hole"), and needle passes to collect several tissue cores.
  (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
- Post-op: neuro observations, usually a scan to check for bleeding, often an
  overnight stay.
- The wound is small; the headache is usually modest; the hard part is the wait.

## 4.4 Frozen section — what happens while you are still asleep

- With open surgery, "small samples of the tumor are looked at right away by the
  pathologist while the patient is still in the operating room," and a
  preliminary diagnosis may be available the same day.
  (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
- Timing: 90% of frozen-section blocks are turned around within 20 minutes of
  the pathologist receiving the sample; mean reported turnaround is 26 ± 5.6
  minutes (range 20–62). Frozen diagnosis is made in ~20–23 minutes versus at
  least 2–3 working days for final histopathology.
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11125713/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12185993/)
- Accuracy: frozen-section diagnosis agreed with the final paraffin diagnosis in
  504/558 (90.3%) of cases and disagreed in 54/558 (9.7%); agreement is lower
  for low-grade tumours than high-grade.
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4322495/;
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25593754/)
- **This is the single most important thing to explain**: the frozen section
  answers "is there enough diagnostic tissue, and roughly what is this?" so the
  surgeon can act during the operation. It is *not* the diagnosis. It is right
  about 9 times in 10, and it deliberately trades precision for speed.

## 4.5 Why the full pathology takes days to weeks — what is physically happening

This is the wait almost nobody explains. The physical steps:

1. **Fixation.** The tissue goes into neutral buffered formalin. Depending on
   sample size, tissue is fixed for **6 to 72 hours** at room temperature before
   processing; the fixative must penetrate the whole tissue and then the
   chemical reactions must reach equilibrium. Standardised biomarker assay
   protocols specify 6–72 hours of formalin fixation.
   (https://cellcarta.com/science-hub/optimal-ffpe-tissue-fixation-processing-and-embedding-a-comprehensive-guide/)
2. **Processing.** Dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration — a
   multi-stage automated run, usually overnight.
   (https://www.leicabiosystems.com/knowledge-pathway/an-introduction-to-specimen-processing/;
   https://cellcarta.com/science-hub/optimal-ffpe-tissue-fixation-processing-and-embedding-a-comprehensive-guide/)
3. **Embedding and sectioning.** The tissue is set in a wax block and sliced
   into ribbons a few microns thick, mounted on slides.
   (https://www.leicabiosystems.com/knowledge-pathway/an-introduction-to-specimen-processing/)
4. **Staining and microscopy.** H&E first, then the neuropathologist reads it.
5. **Immunohistochemistry.** Antibody stains on the paraffin sections
   (IHC of FFPE tissue is "a gold standard in oncology for tumor
   characterization"); each panel is another round of slides, typically another
   day or more.
   (https://cellcarta.com/science-hub/optimal-ffpe-tissue-fixation-processing-and-embedding-a-comprehensive-guide/;
   https://www.abcam.com/en-us/technical-resources/protocols/ihc-with-samples-in-paraffin)
6. **Molecular tests.** DNA/RNA extraction, then sequencing / FISH / PCR /
   methylation array — often sent to a different laboratory, often batched.
   (See §5.5 for times.)
7. **Integration and sign-out.** The neuropathologist combines microscope
   findings with the molecular results into one integrated diagnosis — WHO CNS5
   requires this "histomolecular" integration.
   (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810)
8. **Tumour board.** Often the report is discussed at a multidisciplinary board
   before the plan is finalised.
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8875699/)

The honest summary: the multi-step nature of fixation (6–72 h), processing,
embedding, sectioning, and then IHC accounts for the extended turnaround of
final pathology reports.
(https://cellcarta.com/science-hub/optimal-ffpe-tissue-fixation-processing-and-embedding-a-comprehensive-guide/)

**Reframe for readers:** nothing is sitting idle. The delay is not indifference,
it is chemistry plus queueing plus the fact that the answer arrives in layers —
frozen section (minutes), H&E and IHC (days), molecular (a week or several), and
methylation profiling (a couple of weeks). It is normal to get a partial answer
first and a fuller one later, and for the *name* of the diagnosis to change when
the molecular results land (§5).

## 4.6 Second opinion on pathology

Discordance between an outside pathology diagnosis and expert re-review is not
rare. In one comprehensive cancer centre's experience, neuropathology had the
**highest discordance rate (10.9%)** of any surgical pathology subspecialty;
overall for reviewed outside cases discordance was 14.2% (2.2% major, 12.0%
minor).
(https://ar.iiarjournals.org/content/38/5/2989)
In paediatric brain tumours, major disagreements occurred in 12% of cases, with
71% of all major disagreements occurring in paediatric brain tumours, and novel
molecular tests contributed to the diagnosis in 55% of those.
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28675683/)
One centre reports 11–18% of patients receive a change in diagnosis after
transferring from another facility.
(https://www.roswellpark.org/cancer/brain/diagnosis/second-opinion)
NBTS encourages securing a second opinion where possible.
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)
Practical point: slides and blocks can be sent out for expert neuropathology
review; patients can ask for this.
(https://pathology.jhu.edu/brain-tumor/second-opinions)

## 4.7 When biopsy is not possible

NCCN has expanded its CNS guidelines to recommend **CSF-based molecular tumour
profiling** for high-grade glioma / glioblastoma when tissue biopsy is not
feasible — regardless of the reason (tumour location, patient health, or patient
refusal).
(https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/nccn-expands-csf-molecular-profiling-recommendation-to-inoperable-high-grade-glioma;
https://www.targetedonc.com/view/nccn-now-backs-csf-genomic-testing-in-gliomas-when-biopsy-isn-t-possible)

## 4.8 Questions to ask

- Is this a biopsy only, or an attempt to remove the tumour?
- Frame-based or frameless? Awake or asleep?
- What happens if the frozen section doesn't give enough information?
- Which tests will be run on my tissue, and where are they sent?
- When should I expect the first result, and when the complete one?
- Who will call me, and can I have the appointment booked now so I'm not waiting
  by the phone?
- Can extra tissue be banked for future testing or research?
- Would you send my slides for a second neuropathology opinion?

# 5. Molecular and genetic testing (and the pathology report)

## 5.1 Why this exists at all

Under the WHO 2021 classification (**WHO CNS5**), molecular characteristics
became **essential diagnostic criteria** for many CNS tumour types, and an
integrated "histomolecular" diagnosis is required — the diagnosis depends on
integrating what the tumour looks like under the microscope with its underlying
molecular alterations.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/;
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810)

Concretely: **these tests change the name of the diagnosis**, not just the
footnotes. That is the single most useful thing to tell a patient who is
watching their diagnosis get renamed between the first conversation and the
final report.

The three major diagnostic entities of adult diffuse glioma under CNS5 are:
1. **Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant**
2. **Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted**
3. **Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype**
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)

Two naming consequences worth spelling out:
- IDH-mutant astrocytomas are graded **within** the tumour type as CNS WHO grade
  2, 3 or 4 — and an IDH-mutant tumour with grade 4 features is **no longer
  called "glioblastoma"**; that name is now reserved for IDH-wildtype tumours.
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html;
  https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-023-04250-5)
- **Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A and/or CDKN2B assigns grade 4 regardless of
  what the tumour looks like under the microscope.**
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)

## 5.2 What each term means, and what is physically measured

*Descriptive only. What the word means and how it is measured. What a specific
result means for a specific person is a conversation with their own team.*

| Term | What it is | What the lab measures | Typical method |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IDH1 / IDH2** | Isocitrate dehydrogenase, an enzyme in normal cell metabolism. A change (mutation) in the gene for it is one of the first things checked in a diffuse glioma. | Whether the tumour cells carry a mutation in the IDH1 or IDH2 gene. IDH testing **must** be performed on all diffuse gliomas. | IHC for the common IDH1 p.R132H protein change; sequencing (NGS or Sanger) for other/rarer variants. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810; https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cap-guidelines/current-cap-guidelines/diagnostic-testing-for-diffuse-gliomas) |
| **1p/19q co-deletion** | Loss of one whole copy of the short arm of chromosome 1 *and* one whole copy of the long arm of chromosome 19, together. | Whether **whole-arm** loss of both 1p and 19q is present; partial losses do not count. Required to confirm an oligodendroglioma diagnosis. | FISH, or sequencing/copy-number analysis; whole-arm confirmation requires sequencing or in-situ hybridisation assays. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/; https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **ATRX** | A gene involved in packaging DNA. "ATRX loss" means the tumour cells no longer make the normal protein. | Presence/absence of nuclear ATRX protein, or a mutation in the gene. Used to help separate astrocytoma from oligodendroglioma. | Immunohistochemistry (loss of staining) or sequencing. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **TERT promoter** | The switch region controlling the gene for telomerase, an enzyme that maintains the ends of chromosomes. | Whether a mutation is present in that switch region. Found in oligodendrogliomas and in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. | Targeted sequencing / ddPCR / NGS. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **CDKN2A / CDKN2B** | Two neighbouring genes that make brake proteins for cell division. "Homozygous deletion" means both copies are gone. | Copy number — whether both copies are deleted. | NGS copy-number analysis, methylation-array-derived CNV, FISH, or MLPA. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810; https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| **EGFR amplification** | Extra copies of the epidermal growth factor receptor gene. Common in IDH-wildtype glioblastoma. | Gene copy number. | FISH or NGS copy-number. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **+7 / −10** | Gain of a whole copy of chromosome 7 together with loss of a copy of chromosome 10. | Whole-chromosome copy number. One of the molecular criteria that can define a glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, even in a grade 2–3-looking tumour. | NGS/CNV or methylation array CNV. (https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cap-guidelines/current-cap-guidelines/diagnostic-testing-for-diffuse-gliomas; https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **H3 K27M** (H3 p.K28M) | A specific change in a histone gene — histones are the spools DNA winds around. Defines **diffuse midline glioma** (a paediatric-type tumour that also occurs in adults). | Presence of the specific amino-acid change. | Mutation-specific IHC, or sequencing. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **H3 G34** | A different histone change; defines **diffuse hemispheric glioma, H3 G34-mutant**. | Presence of the specific change. | IHC/sequencing. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **BRAF** | A gene in a growth-signalling pathway. Two different alterations matter: the **V600E** point mutation, and the **KIAA1549::BRAF fusion** (two genes joined together) seen in pilocytic astrocytoma. | Either the point mutation, or the fusion. | Mutation-specific IHC/sequencing for V600E; RNA-based NGS or FISH for fusions. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **MGMT promoter methylation** | MGMT is a DNA-repair protein that removes methyl groups from the O6 position of guanine, counteracting alkylating chemotherapy. Its "promoter" is the on/off switch for the gene; methylation is a chemical tag that quietens the switch. | The **percentage of CpG sites in the promoter that carry the methyl tag** — it is a quantitative measurement, not simply yes/no. | Pyrosequencing (quantitative, per-CpG) or methylation-specific PCR; these are more informative than IHC for the MGMT protein. (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9072488/; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3311573/; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3891253/) |
| **Ki-67 / MIB-1 proliferation index** | Ki-67 is a protein present in cells that are actively cycling and absent in resting cells. MIB-1 is the antibody used to stain for it. | The **percentage of tumour cell nuclei that stain positive** — the "labelling index." | Immunohistochemistry, counted manually or by image analysis. (https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF02893360.pdf; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10644968/) |
| **TP53** | A gene often described as a guardian of cell division. | Mutation status, or abnormal p53 protein accumulation. | Sequencing, or p53 IHC as a surrogate. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |
| **Methylation profiling** | A genome-wide read of the chemical tags on the DNA, producing a pattern that is compared with a large reference library of known tumour types. | The methylation pattern at ~850,000 sites, matched against reference cohorts; the same data also yields a genome-wide copy-number profile. | 850k methylation array — described as the "sole method for DNA methylation-based classification." (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810; https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2025.1720458/full) |
| **NGS panel** | "Next-generation sequencing" — reading many genes at once. | DNA panels detect single-nucleotide variants, small insertions/deletions and copy-number changes across many genes simultaneously; RNA panels are best for finding gene fusions where the partner gene is unknown. | Targeted or comprehensive sequencing panel. (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810) |

**Caveat to carry onto the Ki-67 page section:** there is no standardised method
for counting stained nuclei or choosing a representative area, which produces
poor interobserver reproducibility and high variability in the index; there is
considerable overlap of values between grades and proposed cut-offs vary
substantially between reports. Ki-67 cannot be used as a diagnostic factor alone
and should be used with established histological criteria. This is a good,
honest, non-prognostic thing to tell readers: **the number has real measurement
noise, so do not treat a small difference as meaningful.**
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10644968/;
https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF02893360.pdf;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0344033810002050)

## 5.3 Which tests are supposed to be done

CAP guideline (diffuse gliomas):
- **IDH mutational testing must be performed on all diffuse gliomas.**
- For IDH-mutant tumours: 1p/19q co-deletion, ATRX, TERT promoter, CDKN2A/B.
- **MGMT promoter methylation testing should be performed on all glioblastoma,
  IDH-wildtype.**
- Testing should identify whole chromosome 7 gain / chromosome 10 loss, EGFR
  amplification and TERT promoter mutation in grade 2–3 tumours to establish a
  molecular glioblastoma diagnosis.
(https://www.cap.org/protocols-and-guidelines/cap-guidelines/current-cap-guidelines/diagnostic-testing-for-diffuse-gliomas)

NCCN: for the most common adult-type diffuse gliomas the required workup
includes IDH mutation screening, and for oligodendroglioma 1p/19q co-deletion
must be proven.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9098981/)

EANO guideline emphasises that "incorrect diagnoses can be rectified in up to
25% of cases" through advanced molecular testing — i.e. this is not optional
extra detail.
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810)

## 5.4 What is in the pathology report (absorbs WI-444)

Reports contain information about the patient, the tissue specimen, and the
final diagnosis, and are broken into sections — patient identifiers and dates, a
**gross description** (what the pathologist saw with the naked eye: colour,
shape, feel, size of the tissue), a **microscopic description**, ancillary test
results, and the final integrated diagnosis.
(https://www.oncolink.org/cancer-treatment/procedures-diagnostic-tests/blood-tests-tumor-diagnostic-tests/understanding-a-pathology-report;
https://documents.cap.org/documents/how-to-read-pathology-report.pdf;
https://www.cap.org/member-resources/patient-education/how-to-read-your-pathology-report)

For brain tumours specifically the report carries: the site of the operative
procedure (e.g. brain, frontal lobe), the tumour type (e.g. glioblastoma), the
grade, markers tested by immunohistochemistry (e.g. GFAP), and molecular genetic
testing results (e.g. EGFR, IDH1, MGMT).
(https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4300589)

"This report has information about your tumor that is used to plan your
treatment," and is one of the most important documents guiding neuro-oncology
treatment decisions.
(https://www.cap.org/member-resources/patient-education/how-to-read-your-pathology-report;
https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4300589)

Reader-facing structure that follows from this: *what the top line says* /
*what the grade line says* / *what the stain list says* / *what the molecular
section says* / *what "addendum" and "amended report" mean* / *why the name may
have changed*.

## 5.5 How long molecular results take

- Standard outsourced NGS: approximately **3 weeks**.
- Array-based methylation testing with sample transport and batch processing:
  approximately **3 weeks** in current clinical implementations; some sources
  quote 3–7 days for the analysis itself with final reports in 1–2 weeks.
  (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03562-5;
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2025.1720458/full)
- Faster is arriving but is not yet standard: nanopore workflows have achieved
  intraoperative methylation classification in a 30-minute window with
  comprehensive profiling within 24 hours; rapid amplicon nanopore sequencing
  has reached 105 minutes point-of-care; one point-of-care implementation
  achieved a median biomarker turnaround of **3 business days** with 83% of
  cases under 5 business days.
  (https://www.insideprecisionmedicine.com/topics/precision-medicine/nanopore-sequencing-comprehensively-profiles-brain-tumor-dna-in-30-minutes/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11600771/;
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8947443/;
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03562-5)

## 5.6 Questions to ask

- Which molecular tests are being run on my tumour, and which are still pending?
- Is my report final, or is it an addendum/amended report?
- Has my diagnosis changed since the first result? Why?
- Does the report follow the WHO 2021 (CNS5) naming?
- Is any of my testing being sent out, and to which lab?
- Do any of these results open up a clinical trial or a targeted drug?
- Can I have a printed copy of my pathology report?
- Would you go through the report with me line by line?

# 6. Pre-surgery clearance

## 6.1 What it is

A package of checks done before an operation to confirm the patient can safely
receive anaesthesia and surgery: history and examination, blood tests, sometimes
an ECG or chest imaging, an anaesthesia consultation, and a plan for which
medicines to stop and when.

For highly invasive surgery such as craniotomy, the pre-anaesthesia evaluation
is recommended **before the day of the procedure**, and the choice and timing of
tests should be individualised based on the medical record, patient interview,
physical examination and the type/invasiveness of the planned procedure.
(https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8964/;
https://accessanesthesiology.mhmedical.com/content.aspx?sectionid=61588129&bookid=974)

## 6.2 What tests, and why "routine" is not automatic

- Test selection is driven by clinical characteristics — cardiocirculatory
  disease, respiratory disease, and the type of surgery.
- **ECG** is indicated in patients with known cardiovascular risk factors, and is
  usually indicated with a cardiac history or above roughly age 50–60.
- **Chest x-ray** is indicated only with active respiratory symptoms or
  undiagnosed cardiorespiratory disease — it is not automatic.
(https://accessanesthesiology.mhmedical.com/content.aspx?sectionid=61588129&bookid=974;
https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8964/)
- Blood and urine tests mainly assess "how well the liver, kidneys, and some
  other organs are working" before surgery.
  (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
- Routine testing in minimal-risk patients is a known area of over-testing.
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3722913/;
  https://www.anthem.com/medpolicies/abcbs/active/gl_pw_d056675.html)

Neurosurgery-specific additions in practice: a **group and save / type and
screen** and coagulation studies, and, for pituitary and skull-base tumours,
endocrine bloods and formal visual fields (§9).

## 6.3 The anaesthesia consultation

The pre-anaesthesia history and physical includes review of records, patient
interview and examination, with baseline assessment of **airway, heart, lungs
and vital signs**.
(https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8964/)
On the day, patients meet the anaesthesiologist to "address any patient concerns
and review any allergies or sensitivities," and the neurosurgeon for a final
discussion of the surgical plan, potential risks and complications.
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

## 6.4 Fasting

ASA fasting guidance: **2 hours** for clear fluids, **6 hours** after a light
meal, **8 hours** after a full meal high in calories or fat.
(https://www.guidelinecentral.com/guideline/8964/)
The clear-fluids rule is worth stating loudly — many patients needlessly go
without water all night.

## 6.5 Blood thinners and antiplatelet medicines

From the 2024 ACC/AHA perioperative guideline:
- If antiplatelet drugs must be stopped: **prasugrel 7 days**, **clopidogrel 5
  days**, **ticagrelor 3 days**, **aspirin 4–5 days** before surgery.
- Aspirin depends on why it is being taken. For **secondary prevention** (chronic
  coronary disease, prior MI, prior stroke) it may be reasonable to continue if
  the cardiac risk outweighs bleeding risk. For **primary prevention** (and not
  carotid surgery) it should be discontinued.
- **Bridging** of oral anticoagulants should be used selectively, only in
  patients at highest thrombotic risk, and is not recommended in most cases.
- Restarting: DOACs 24–72 hours after surgery; warfarin within 24 hours.
(https://www.jacc.org/doi/10.1016/j.jacc.2024.06.013;
https://www.ccjm.org/content/92/4/213;
https://www.the-hospitalist.org/hospitalist/article/39231/cardiology/the-2024-acc-aha-perioperative-cardiac-guidelines-whats-new/;
https://journal.chestnet.org/article/S0012-3692(22)01359-9/fulltext)

**Editorial framing required:** these day-counts are for context so readers
recognise the conversation — they are individualised decisions. The page must
say plainly: *do not stop a blood thinner on your own; ask who is in charge of
that decision (usually the prescriber plus the surgeon).*

Also worth flagging for brain-tumour readers specifically: many are on
**dexamethasone** and **anti-seizure medicines**, which are generally
**continued**, and patients should continue regular medications unless told
otherwise.
(https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)

## 6.6 Other day-before / day-of practicalities

- Follow the hospital's pre-op instructions for the 24–48 hours before surgery;
  they vary by health system.
- On arrival: change into a gown, remove jewellery and contact lenses, IV line
  placed, pre-operative labs if needed, meet anaesthetist, meet surgeon.
- Bring **advance directives** (living will, healthcare proxy) — NBTS
  specifically recommends this.
(https://braintumor.org/news/what-to-expect-before-and-after-brain-surgery/)

## 6.7 Questions to ask

- Which of my medicines do I stop, and on which date exactly?
- Who decides about my blood thinner — you or my cardiologist?
- Do I keep taking my steroid and my seizure medicine?
- When do I stop food, and when do I stop clear fluids?
- Do I need an ECG or a chest x-ray, and why?
- What time do I arrive, and who can stay with me?
- What happens if a test comes back abnormal — does surgery get postponed?

# 7. Neurological and neuropsychological assessment

## 7.1 The bedside neurological exam

"A series of questions and tests to check the brain, spinal cord, and nerve
function."
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq)
It tests "reflexes, muscle strength, vision, eye and mouth movement,
coordination, balance, and alertness," and is performed by a neurologist or
neurosurgeon.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

Patient experience: no needles, no machines, 10–20 minutes, repeated often. The
useful reframe — this is repeated at every visit precisely so that *change* can
be detected; it is not because the doctor forgot they already did it.

## 7.2 Formal neuropsychological assessment

**What it tests.** Standard batteries cover **attention, executive function,
memory (verbal and non-verbal), language, visuomotor speed and visuospatial
ability**; some batteries use 26 independent measures across eight domains
including mood.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213158222002844;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9338148/)

**When.** Typically before surgery and again afterwards. Reported timings vary:
approximately 1 week post-op in some protocols, 2 days pre-op and 3–4 months
post-op in others, and baseline plus ~1 month post-op before starting adjuvant
treatment in others.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772529425017266;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213158222002844)

**Why before surgery.** Pre-operative neuropsychological testing outperformed
tumour location in predicting post-surgical cognitive function — baseline
cognitive status is an important predictor of outcome, which is why the
"before" test is not a formality.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213158222002844;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12427565/)

**What patients experience.** Several hours with a psychologist or technician:
word lists, drawings, puzzles, timed tasks, questionnaires about mood. It is
tiring and many patients find it confronting because it makes deficits visible.
Breaks are allowed. There is no pass or fail.

**What the results are used for.** Surgical planning (especially near language
or motor areas), rehabilitation referral, return-to-work and driving
discussions, and tracking change over treatment. Perioperative assessment
reveals dynamic changes in neurocognitive function following brain-tumour
surgery.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2772529425017266)

**Reassurance available without quoting prognosis:** across whole samples, 85%
of test scores showed no decline after surgery; and in one series of early
surgery in asymptomatic patients, 87.2% had stable or improved neuropsychological
outcomes at 3 months. Memory showed the most improvement, visuomotor speed the
most worsening.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2213158222002844;
https://thejns.org/view/journals/j-neurosurg/135/3/article-p871.xml)
*(These are cognitive-outcome figures, not survival figures — permitted, but
consider whether numbers are needed at all at 6th-grade level.)*

## 7.3 Questions to ask

- What will this testing be used for?
- How long will it take, and can I split it over two visits?
- Will I get a written report, and will it go to my employer or insurer only
  with my permission?
- Can this help with driving, work or disability decisions?
- Will it be repeated? When?

# 8. EEG and seizure workup

## 8.1 What it is

An EEG records the electrical signals brain cells use to communicate. A
technician places small metal discs (electrodes) on the scalp, or a cap with
electrodes held on with removable adhesive. The recording is painless.
(https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/9656-electroencephalogram-eeg;
https://www.webmd.com/epilepsy/electroencephalogram-eeg)

## 8.2 Types and duration

- **Routine EEG: 20–40 minutes.**
- **Prolonged EEG: 1–2 hours**, with some types running for days.
- **HD-EEG: 45 minutes to 2 hours.**
- **Sleep EEG**, sometimes combined with a sleep study, if a sleep-related
  disorder linked to epilepsy is suspected.
- **Video EEG monitoring**: in hospital over a few days, filming the patient
  while recording brain activity, to capture what happens to the body during a
  seizure.
(https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/9656-electroencephalogram-eeg;
https://www.cureepilepsy.org/understanding-epilepsy/diagnosing-epilepsy/eeg/)

## 8.3 What happens during the test

The technician may ask the patient to open or close their eyes, **breathe deeply
in and out (hyperventilate)**, or **look at a flashing light (photic
stimulation)**. These activation procedures are deliberate attempts to bring out
abnormal activity.
(https://www.webmd.com/epilepsy/electroencephalogram-eeg;
https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/9656-electroencephalogram-eeg)

Practical notes for the page: hair should be clean and free of product;
electrode paste has to be washed out afterwards; patients may be asked to sleep
less the night before ("sleep-deprived EEG") to increase the yield; a normal EEG
does **not** rule out epilepsy, which is a frequent source of confusion.

## 8.4 Where it fits in brain-tumour care

- Working up a first seizure (often the event that led to diagnosis).
- Distinguishing seizures from other episodes (faints, medication effects,
  stroke-like events).
- Investigating unexplained confusion or reduced consciousness (non-convulsive
  status epilepticus).
- Monitoring in intensive care after surgery.
- Duration of monitoring matters: work exists on how long EEG monitoring is
  needed to ensure a low risk of seizure recurrence in hospitalised patients.
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12783230/)

Most relevant tumour types: supratentorial gliomas (especially low-grade),
meningiomas over the convexity, and metastases in the cortex.

## 8.5 Questions to ask

- Do I need to stay awake the night before?
- Should I take my seizure medicine as normal that morning?
- Will this change my medicine or my driving status?
- How long until the neurologist reads it?
- If this EEG is normal, does that mean I did not have a seizure?

# 9. Specialty tests by location

## 9.1 Visual field testing — pituitary and skull-base tumours

**Why.** Static automated perimetry assesses compression of the anterior visual
pathway by a pituitary adenoma and monitors for tumour progression by tracking
afferent visual function.
(https://eyewiki.org/Pituitary_Adenoma)
Automated static perimetry is the gold standard for visual field evaluation; the
Humphrey Field Analyzer is one of the most used devices.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK585112/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3306097/)

**Protocol.** Standard achromatic perimetry using the central 30-2 program with
a Goldmann size III target is a typical protocol; algorithms testing the central
24 degrees perform as well as those testing 30 degrees while shortening the
test.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3306097/;
https://eyewiki.org/Pituitary_Adenoma;
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/aos.15731)

**What the patient experiences.** The patient puts their chin on a rest and
stares at a central fixation point inside a large white bowl. The eye not being
tested is **patched** and the room lights are dimmed. Dim lights appear in
different positions and the patient presses a button each time one is seen with
their side vision, while keeping their gaze on the centre. The machine adjusts
brightness to find the threshold. Testing is done **one eye at a time** and takes
several minutes per eye.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK585112/;
https://eyerounds.org/tutorials/VF-testing/;
https://glaucoma.org/articles/what-is-a-visual-field-test)

**Discomfort.** The test is boring, requires sustained concentration, and is
tiring — fatigue during the test is a recognised problem and shows up as false
negatives. Patients should know it is normal to feel like you are "failing"
because you cannot see many of the lights: the machine deliberately presents
lights near and below the threshold of vision.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK585112/)

**When it is repeated.** Before surgery as a baseline, after surgery, and during
follow-up of a residual or watched tumour.

## 9.2 Endocrine blood tests — pituitary and skull-base tumours

**Screening for hormone excess.** Measure **prolactin** and **IGF-1** in all
patients with an incidentally discovered pituitary adenoma; screen for
hypercortisolism if clinically warranted.
(https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-025-01134-8;
https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/pituitary-incidentaloma)

**Screening for hormone deficiency (hypopituitarism).** Baseline screening in
all patients with sellar lesions: **free T4, TSH, morning cortisol, ACTH, LH,
FSH, IGF-1**, plus **testosterone** in men and **oestradiol** in women as
needed.
(https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-025-01134-8;
https://www.cns.org/guidelines/management-nonfunctioning-pituitary-adenomas/3-pretreatment-endocrine-evaluation)

**Practical detail patients need.** Several of these are **timed** tests —
cortisol and ACTH are drawn early in the morning, and a morning cortisol above
10 µg/dL suggests an intact hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Prolactin can
be raised by stress, needles, and by stalk compression, so a single high value
is not self-explanatory. Some patients go on to **dynamic testing** (stimulation
or suppression tests) which take several hours with repeated blood draws through
a cannula.
(https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/1157189-workup;
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41574-025-01134-8)

Post-operative endocrine follow-up is standard after pituitary surgery, per
Pituitary Society survey work on perioperative endocrine management.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8294287/)

## 9.3 Audiometry — vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)

**Why.** Patients with vestibular schwannoma typically complete **pure-tone and
speech audiometry** and auditory brainstem responses (ABR).
(https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/acoustic-neuroma-an-overview-11407)
Pure-tone audiometry is used to screen patients with asymmetrical hearing loss;
several formal screening protocols exist (AAO-HNS, Rule3000, Seattle and
others), with good pooled sensitivity but low specificity.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9689241/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1672293020301185)
Audiologic screening in diagnosis and management of vestibular schwannoma is
covered by a CNS guideline.
(https://www.cns.org/guidelines/treatment-adults-vestibular-schwannoma/2-audiologic-screening-in-diagnosis-management-of-)

**What is measured.** Two numbers dominate the conversation: the **pure-tone
average (PTA)** and the **word recognition score (WRS)**. Vestibular schwannoma
may show an increased pure-tone average and decreased speech discrimination.
Thresholds of better than 50 dB PTA and better than 50% word recognition are
used in certain management considerations (the "50/50" rule).
(https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/acoustic-neuroma-an-overview-11407)

**What the patient experiences.** In a sound booth with headphones or inserts:
press a button or raise a hand when you hear a tone; repeat back words spoken at
different volumes; sometimes a pressure test of the eardrum (tympanometry).
Painless, roughly 30–60 minutes. ABR adds scalp electrodes and clicks played
into the ear while the patient rests quietly. Vestibular tests (caloric testing,
VEMP) may be added; caloric testing involves warm and cool air or water in the
ear canal and can cause temporary dizziness or nausea.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9444786/;
https://www.audiologyonline.com/articles/acoustic-neuroma-an-overview-11407)

**When repeated.** At diagnosis and at each surveillance interval alongside MRI,
because hearing change can drive management decisions independently of tumour
size.

## 9.4 Questions to ask

- Which specialist is doing this test, and will they send the result to my
  neurosurgeon?
- Is this the baseline, and how often will it be repeated?
- Does this test need to happen before surgery?
- (Pituitary) Do I need to have this blood test first thing in the morning, and
  fasting?
- (Pituitary) Will I need hormone replacement, and who manages that?
- (Acoustic neuroma) If my hearing changes, does that change the plan?

# 10. Follow-up scans and reading the results

## 10.1 Why surveillance exists and what the rhythm is

Surveillance imaging exists to detect change early enough to act on it, and to
provide a baseline for comparison. The exact rhythm is tumour- and
treatment-specific.

**Meningioma (NCCN-derived):**
- WHO grade 1 and 2: brain MRI at **3, 6 and 12 months**, then every **6–12
  months for 5 years**, then every **1–3 years** as clinically indicated.
- WHO grade 3: brain MRI every **2–4 months for the first 3 years**, then every
  **3–6 months**.
- More frequent imaging may be needed for tumours treated for recurrence or with
  systemic therapy.
(https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/12/3/478/7914655)

**High-grade glioma:** clinical practice commonly uses roughly 2–3 monthly MRI
after chemoradiation, but this is convention rather than a strong evidence base.
One modelling study derived optimal intervals for IDH-wildtype glioblastoma of
every ~7.4 weeks until 120 weeks after the end of standard treatment, then an
inflection period, then every ~27.6 weeks — explicitly a **research-derived**
schedule, not official guidance.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8099465/)
The NCCN CNS guideline is the operative reference for practice.
(https://www.nccn.org/guidelines/guidelines-detail?category=1&id=1425)

**Post-operative baseline:** the early post-operative MRI (within 48–72 hours,
§1.9) is the reference point against which everything later is compared.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9955136/)
Under RANO 2.0, the **post-radiation scan** is adopted as the baseline for
response assessment.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12479667/)

## 10.2 RANO response criteria in plain terms

**RANO 2.0** was introduced in 2023 and is designed to apply across glioma
subtypes.
(https://ascopubs.org/doi/abs/10.1200/JCO.23.01059;
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/1/2/7286265)

Four categories, defined by how much the measured tumour changed:
- **Complete response (CR):** no measurable tumour.
- **Partial response (PR):** ≤ −65% volume change, or ≤ −50% change in SPD (sum
  of products of perpendicular diameters).
- **Stable disease (SD):** between −65% and +40% by volume, or between −50% and
  +25% by SPD.
- **Progressive disease (PD):** ≥ +40% by volume, or ≥ +25% by SPD.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12479667/;
https://kjronline.org/DOIx.php?id=10.3348%2Fkjr.2024.0016)

Key changes in RANO 2.0: adoption of the standardised Brain Tumor Imaging
Protocol, use of the post-radiation scan as baseline, formalised handling of
"preliminary progressive disease" requiring a confirmation scan, and **removal
of T2/FLAIR measurements from the assessment of enhancing tumour**.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12479667/;
https://www.ajnr.org/content/45/12/1846)

**Two things readers need to hear:**
1. RANO is a **research/clinical-trial measuring stick**. Individual care is not
   dictated by it, and a radiology report saying "stable disease" is using a
   defined band, not saying "nothing changed at all."
2. "Stable" covers a wide band — including a tumour that shrank a lot but not
   quite enough for partial response, and one that grew somewhat but not enough
   for progression.

## 10.3 Pseudoprogression

**What it is.** After chemoradiation, a scan can show increased contrast
enhancement that later subsides **without any change in treatment** — that is,
it looked like growth but was not.

**How common.** 20–30% of patients show this on their first post-radiation MRI;
reported incidence across series varies between **12% and 64%**.
(https://ascopost.com/issues/august-15-2012/understanding-and-managing-pseudoprogression-in-glioblastoma-patients/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10412732/)

**When.** Most prevalent within the **first 12 weeks** after completing
radiotherapy; commonly in the first 3 months, occasionally persisting up to six
months; rarely seen after six months.
(https://ascopost.com/issues/august-15-2012/understanding-and-managing-pseudoprogression-in-glioblastoma-patients/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10412732/)
Delayed pseudoprogression has also been described with tumour-treating fields.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10979818/)

**How it is handled.** Because the incidence is high in the 12 weeks after
radiotherapy, RANO 2.0 requires either continuation of treatment with a **repeat
MRI to confirm progression** during that window, or histopathological evidence
of unequivocal recurrent tumour, before progression is declared. After that
window confirmation scans are not mandatory, nor for recurrent-tumour treatment
— except for therapies with a high likelihood of pseudoprogression, such as
certain immunotherapies, oncolytic viruses or immunotoxins, where mandatory
confirmation is recommended.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12479667/;
https://kjronline.org/DOIx.php?id=10.3348%2Fkjr.2024.0476)

Other clinical rules used: progression can be determined if the majority of new
enhancement is **outside the radiation field** (e.g. beyond the high-dose
region / 80% isodose line), or if there is pathological confirmation; and if
doubt exists, continued follow-up imaging is recommended because time eventually
gives clarity.
(https://ascopost.com/issues/august-15-2012/understanding-and-managing-pseudoprogression-in-glioblastoma-patients/)

**Be honest about the limits of the fancy scans.** "MRI-based perfusion and
spectroscopy or brain FDG-PET scans cannot reliably distinguish tumor
progression from pseudoprogression," and despite advances in perfusion MRI and
PET no imaging technique is yet validated for detecting pseudoprogression or
incorporated into the latest response criteria.
(https://ascopost.com/issues/august-15-2012/understanding-and-managing-pseudoprogression-in-glioblastoma-patients/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7893814/)
Perfusion MRI is nevertheless routinely used to help with the judgement.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4755513/;
https://ajronline.org/doi/10.2214/AJR.11.7417)

## 10.4 Radiation necrosis

**What it is.** Late damage to normal brain tissue in the treated area, which on
a scan can look like a growing, enhancing mass.

**How common / when.** After stereotactic radiosurgery for brain metastases,
radiation necrosis developed in 45 of 238 lesions (19%) in one series and in 61
of 388 patients (15.7%) in another. Median time to diagnosis was **7 months**
(range 1–25 months); it most commonly occurs within **2 years** of SRS, though
late radiation necrosis occurring or recurring **more than 5 years** after SRS
is described. Risk rises with dose.
(https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(07)01476-9/fulltext;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8507553/;
https://thejns.org/view/journals/j-neurosurg/142/4/article-p1117.xml)
There are International Stereotactic Radiosurgery Society recommendations
informing management of symptomatic brain radiation necrosis after SRS.
(https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(23)07647-2/fulltext)

**How it is confirmed.** Imaging (including perfusion and PET), sometimes with
biopsy for definitive confirmation; in one small biopsy series imaging diagnosis
matched biopsy in all cases assessed.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8507553/)

## 10.5 Scan anxiety ("scanxiety")

**How common.** Scanxiety affects over 70% of cancer patients undergoing routine
oncologic imaging, with clinically significant scanxiety in 71.2% of patients in
one prospective longitudinal study. Rates tend to fall as time passes after
surgery.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0899707125002347;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659363/)

**When it peaks.** It is "most prevalent in the scan-to-results waiting period."
Consequences include poorer quality of life and somatic symptoms.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10000102/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749208123001511)

**Who is more affected.** Risk factors include low coping self-efficacy,
pre-existing anxiety, low well-being, lower education level, less time since
diagnosis, and greater baseline anxiety.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10000102/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659363/)

**What helps — and the honest state of the evidence.** Pre-scan counselling,
simplified scheduling and online real-time communication portals have shown
initial promise in small studies but have not been rigorously tested across
diverse populations. Trials that changed the surveillance regimen itself did not
reduce scanxiety, and no intervention has yet been identified as reliably
effective. Scanxiety lacks recognition and standardised interventions — a
significant gap in current practice.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659363/;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10000102/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1546084324001603)

**Practical page content that is honest and still useful:** name it; say it is
near-universal; say the worst part is the gap between scan and result; and
concentrate the advice on the one thing patients can actually control — **the
scheduling gap**. Ask for the scan and the results appointment to be booked
close together; ask who will call and when; decide in advance whether you want
to read the portal report before the appointment.

## 10.6 Questions to ask

- What are we comparing this scan to?
- Is it possible that what we're seeing is treatment effect rather than tumour?
- If we're not sure, what do we do — repeat the scan, add perfusion or PET, or
  biopsy?
- When is the next scan, and can I book the results appointment at the same time?
- Will my report appear in the patient portal before you have spoken to me, and
  can that be changed?
- Is this scan being discussed at a tumour board?

# 11. Lumbar puncture and CSF testing

## 11.1 What the patient experiences

The patient lies "on your side... with your knees up near your chest" (or sits
leaning forward), the lower back is cleaned and numbed with local anaesthetic,
and a needle "is then inserted between the bones of the spine to withdraw some
of the fluid."
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

Typical timeline: 20–45 minutes including positioning and consent; the needle is
in for a few minutes. Sensations: stinging from the local anaesthetic, pressure,
and sometimes a brief electric-shock sensation down a leg. Some centres use
fluoroscopic or ultrasound guidance if landmarks are difficult.

## 11.2 Imaging first

Imaging typically precedes the procedure to prevent a dangerous pressure drop.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
Nuance from the general medicine literature: neuroimaging before LP was once
thought necessary to rule out raised intracranial pressure risking herniation,
but the frequency of a causative lesion is low and herniation risk is minimal in
patients without clinical features of brain shift; routine pre-LP CT delays
treatment. **In a patient with a known brain tumour, however, imaging review
before LP is standard practice** — this is exactly the population where a mass
lesion is present by definition.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK604145/;
https://www.ccjm.org/content/ccjom/84/2/111.full.pdf)

## 11.3 Risks and discomforts

The main one is **post-dural puncture headache (PDPH)** — a positional headache
that is relieved by lying flat and returns on sitting or standing, typically
appearing 12–72 hours after the procedure, sometimes with nausea.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7749614/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5873702/)

Incidence figures vary widely across studies — reported between **1% and 40%**,
with around 30% quoted in some diagnostic-LP populations.
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7749614/;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5873702/)

**Needle type matters and is worth telling patients about.** Rates were
significantly lower with **atraumatic** needles, and headaches were shorter. In
one randomised comparison, PDPH occurred in 18% with a 22G Sprotte (atraumatic)
versus 39% with a 22G Quincke (cutting) needle.
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1529943015002211;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7749614/;
https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03126045)
This gives readers a concrete, legitimate question to ask: *"Will you be using
an atraumatic (pencil-point) needle?"*

Other risks: back pain at the site, bleeding (more of a concern on
anticoagulants), and rarely infection.

## 11.4 Where it applies

**Leptomeningeal metastasis.** EANO–ESMO guidance: clinical, imaging **and CSF
cytology** assessments are **all mandatory** to classify leptomeningeal
metastasis. One **repeat** lumbar puncture with optimised analysis conditions
should be carried out in patients with suspected LM and initially negative or
equivocal CSF studies — i.e. a negative first tap does not close the question.
(https://www.esmoopen.com/article/S2059-7029(23)00859-1/fulltext;
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37863528/;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0923753419421492)

**Medulloblastoma staging.** Initial treatment is assigned by metastatic (M)
stage determined by **both** CSF cytology and MRI. Chang staging: M0 no
metastasis; **M1 = tumour cells present in the CSF**; M2 nodular seeding in the
cerebellar/cerebral subarachnoid space or third/lateral ventricle; M3 metastasis
in the spinal subarachnoid space; M4 metastasis outside the cerebrospinal axis.
CSF staging should be performed **14 days post-operatively** by lumbar puncture,
and specimens should contain at least 10 nucleated cells.
(https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2012/245385;
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959804922000041;
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4995146/)
Approximately 30% of children with medulloblastoma have CSF dissemination on MRI
or cytology.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8063474/)
The 14-day rule is a genuinely useful patient-facing fact — it explains why the
LP is *not* done at the same time as surgery.

**CNS germ cell tumours.** Lumbar CSF is obtained for **tumour markers
(alpha-fetoprotein, AFP, and beta-hCG)** and for cytopathological review.
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/child-cns-germ-cell-treatment-pdq)
In primary intracranial germ cell tumours, raised β-hCG is found more often in
CSF than in plasma before treatment, and at relapse CSF levels become detectable
before serum values rise; hCG does not cross the blood-brain barrier.
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4788851/;
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8622720/)
These markers can establish the diagnosis without a biopsy in some cases — which
is why the LP is not "just another test" for this group.

**Other:** CSF cytology matters for CNS lymphoma and ependymoma; the ACS notes
CSF testing is particularly important for ependymomas and lymphomas.
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)

**CSF molecular profiling.** NCCN now recommends CSF-based molecular tumour
profiling in high-grade glioma / glioblastoma when tissue biopsy is not
feasible.
(https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/nccn-expands-csf-molecular-profiling-recommendation-to-inoperable-high-grade-glioma;
https://clpmag.com/disease-states/cancer/nccn-updates-guidelines-fluid-based-testing-brain-tumors/)

## 11.5 Questions to ask

- Why do I need this, and what will you do differently depending on the result?
- Have you looked at my brain scan before doing this?
- Will you use an atraumatic (pencil-point) needle?
- What do I do if I get a headache afterwards, and who do I call?
- If the first test is negative, will it need repeating?
- (Medulloblastoma) Why are we waiting two weeks after surgery?

# 12. Source conflicts, cautions and outdated material

1. **"Good marker / bad marker" framing is everywhere in otherwise excellent
   sources.** The American Cancer Society page explicitly describes IDH1/IDH2
   mutations and MGMT promoter methylation as associated with a "better outlook."
   (https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/detection-diagnosis-staging/how-diagnosed.html)
   The EANO guideline separates tumours into "prognostically distinct groups."
   (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/25/10/1731/7189810)
   This dossier deliberately did not carry that framing forward. Any draft
   written from these sources will drift toward it unless the prompt/style guide
   forbids it explicitly.

2. **MGMT is the sharpest edge.** MGMT methylation status is genuinely used to
   *choose treatment*, particularly in older adults, not only to predict
   outcome.
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3891253/;
   https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/9/1457/6263562)
   A page that says nothing about how the result is used risks being unhelpful;
   a page that explains it risks stating which result is "favourable."
   **Recommended line:** describe what MGMT is and what is measured (percentage
   of methylated CpG sites), state that the care team uses the result as one
   input when deciding whether and how to use certain chemotherapy, and route
   "what does *my* result mean" to the reader's own team. Do not characterise
   either result.

3. **Radiation dose figures for head CT disagree.** A commonly cited consumer
   figure for head CT with contrast is 4 mSv / ~16 months of background
   radiation (https://www.webmd.com/cancer/radiation-doses-ct-scans), but other
   references put a routine non-contrast head CT closer to ~2 mSv. Give a range
   or a qualitative comparison rather than a single number.

4. **Post-operative MRI timing has shifted.** The traditional teaching is
   "within 72 hours"; more recent evidence supports 48 hours because
   surgically-induced enhancement increases beyond 48 hours.
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9955136/;
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31431787/)
   Write it as "usually within the first day or two after surgery."

5. **NSF guidance is materially out of date in older patient material.** Many
   older pages still describe mandatory kidney-function screening before any
   gadolinium. Current ACR/NKF consensus makes screening optional before group
   II agents.
   (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590059520302181;
   https://www.acr.org/Clinical-Resources/Clinical-Tools-and-Reference/Contrast-Manual)
   Radiologyinfo's own page still uses the older, more cautious framing.
   (https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info/mri-brain)
   Safest reader-facing line: "Tell them about any kidney problems — they will
   decide whether extra checks are needed."

6. **Claustrophobia rates vary by an order of magnitude** between studies
   (0.76% incomplete exams up to ~15% severe reactions), depending on definition
   and scanner. Do not quote a single number.
   (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1078817422000190;
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41004835/)

7. **Open/upright MRI comparisons come from a mixed evidence base** and are not
   equivalent for tumour-protocol brain imaging. The comparative comfort data is
   real but should not become "ask for an open MRI."
   (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S107881742400172X)

8. **Biopsy haemorrhage rates vary enormously by definition.** One review
   reported intraoperative bleeding in 12.3% and post-operative intracerebral
   haemorrhage in 19.1% (largely radiographic/asymptomatic), while other series
   report 2.4% asymptomatic haematoma and no symptomatic haemorrhage. The
   clinically meaningful figure is *symptomatic* haemorrhage, which is low.
   Quote qualitatively.
   (https://ruralneuropractice.com/diagnostic-yield-and-complication-of-frameless-stereotactic-brain-biopsy/;
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12560739/;
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31900737/)

9. **Molecular turnaround times are changing fast.** The ~3-week figure is the
   current mainstream reality; nanopore/rapid workflows (30-minute
   intraoperative classification, 3-business-day medians) are real but are not
   yet what most patients will experience. Write "usually one to three weeks,
   sometimes longer" and note that a few centres are much faster.
   (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03562-5;
   https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11600771/)

10. **"Oligoastrocytoma" is obsolete.** Older ABTA and other patient PDFs still
    carry the combined "Oligodendroglioma & Oligoastrocytoma" title; WHO CNS5
    does not recognise oligoastrocytoma as a diagnosis. Do not reuse that
    terminology.
    (https://www.abta.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Oligodendroglioma_Oligoastrocytoma_2020_web_en.pdf;
    https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)

11. **Grading notation changed.** WHO CNS5 uses Arabic numerals and grades
    *within* tumour type ("CNS WHO grade 3"), not the older Roman-numeral
    across-the-board convention. Reports written before 2021, and many web
    sources, use the old style — readers holding two reports may see both.
    (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)

12. **Scanxiety interventions: the evidence is weak and the review literature
    says so plainly.** Avoid promising that any specific technique works. State
    that it is common, name the waiting period as the worst part, and stick to
    logistics advice.
    (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659363/;
    https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10000102/)

13. **Access blocked during research:** The Brain Tumour Charity diagnosis page,
    ABTA diagnosis page, NCCN patient guidelines PDF, Johns Hopkins "Understanding
    My Report", and the RSNA fMRI review all returned HTTP 403 to automated
    fetching. Their content was obtained via search summaries and cited
    accordingly; a human should open these directly before final drafting,
    especially the NCCN patient guideline, which is the best available
    plain-language model for a "questions to ask" section.
