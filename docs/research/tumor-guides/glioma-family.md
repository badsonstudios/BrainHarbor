## 0.1 What a glioma is, at the cell level

Gliomas are tumours that start in the **glial cells** of the brain and spinal
cord — the support cells that surround, feed, insulate, and protect neurons
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Gliomas are the most common
primary tumour arising inside the central nervous system
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).

Three glial cell types give rise to the named glioma families
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/):

| Cell | Normal job | Tumour family |
|---|---|---|
| **Astrocyte** (star-shaped) | Structural support, blood–brain barrier maintenance, nutrient supply, regulating the chemical environment around neurons | Astrocytoma (including glioblastoma) |
| **Oligodendrocyte** | Makes myelin, the fatty insulation around nerve fibres in the CNS | Oligodendroglioma |
| **Ependymal cell** | Lines the ventricles and the central canal of the spinal cord; handles cerebrospinal fluid | Ependymoma |

NCI describes the same split in patient-facing terms: astrocytic tumours arise
from "star-shaped astrocytes that support nerve cell health"; oligodendroglial
tumours arise from oligodendrocytes, "another supportive cell type," and both
belong to the glial family whose tumours are called gliomas
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**Important nuance to keep, not lose in simplification:** the *name* reflects
which normal cell the tumour resembles, not proof of the exact cell it grew from.
For oligodendroglioma specifically, the cell of origin "likely stems from
neuroprogenitor cells with glial precursor characteristics rather than mature
oligodendrocytes" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Same idea
applies across the family — these are named by resemblance.

**Diffuse vs circumscribed.** This is the single most useful structural
distinction for a patient and it is usually buried. *Diffuse* gliomas send
individual cells out through normal brain tissue beyond anything visible on a
scan; *circumscribed* gliomas have "more well-delineated borders separating them
from the surrounding brain parenchyma" and generally a more favourable natural
history, with attempted gross total resection as the treatment of choice
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/). Grade 1 gliomas are "benign, well
delineated and can be cured by surgery, but from grade 2 and above, the tumors
are diffuse and because of that, incurable"
(https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade).

## 0.2 The WHO CNS5 (2021) architecture — how the family tree is actually shaped

The 5th edition WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System
("WHO CNS5", 2021) reorganised gliomas into groups by *biology*, not just
appearance (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

**Glioma groupings under CNS5:**

1. **Adult-type diffuse gliomas** — three entities only
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/):
   - Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant (CNS WHO grade 2, 3, or 4)
   - Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted (grade 2 or 3)
   - Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (grade 4)
2. **Pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas** — four entities, generally
   MAPK-pathway driven, grade 1
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/):
   diffuse astrocytoma MYB- or MYBL1-altered; angiocentric glioma; polymorphous
   low-grade neuroepithelial tumour of the young (PLNTY); diffuse low-grade
   glioma, MAPK pathway-altered.
3. **Pediatric-type diffuse high-grade gliomas** — diffuse midline glioma H3
   K27-altered; diffuse hemispheric glioma H3 G34-mutant; diffuse pediatric-type
   high-grade glioma H3-wildtype and IDH-wildtype; infant-type hemispheric glioma
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
   https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
4. **Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas** — pilocytic astrocytoma, pleomorphic
   xanthoastrocytoma, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, chordoid glioma,
   astroblastoma, and the new entity high-grade astrocytoma with piloid features
   (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).
5. Plus ependymal tumours, glioneuronal/neuronal tumours, etc.
   (https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumorwhoclassification.html)

**CRITICAL and almost universally misexplained:** "adult-type" and
"pediatric-type" describe the tumour's **molecular biology, not the patient's
age**. Adults can be diagnosed with pediatric-type gliomas and children with
adult-type gliomas. In one series of 596 adult patients, 20 had pediatric-type
gliomas — diffuse astrocytoma MYB/MYBL1-altered, diffuse midline glioma H3
K27-altered, and diffuse pediatric-type high-grade glioma
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10196618/). The split exists because
these tumour groups have "distinct clinical, genetic and epigenetic features"
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

**Other CNS5 mechanics that matter to a reader holding paperwork:**

- **Arabic numerals replaced Roman numerals.** Grades are written 1, 2, 3, 4 —
  not I, II, III, IV (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **Grading is now done *within* tumour type.** "Grading is now done within tumor
  types as part of the integrated diagnosis," which means grade 3 in one tumour
  type does not carry the same meaning as grade 3 in another
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). This is why the
  correct phrase is "**CNS WHO grade**," not just "WHO grade"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **Integrated diagnosis.** The diagnosis is built by combining what the tumour
  looks like under the microscope with what the molecular tests show
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). CNS5 recommends a
  "layered" report, and explicitly acknowledges cases where histology and
  genetics diverge (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **NOS and NEC.** "NOS" (not otherwise specified) on a report means the required
  molecular testing was not available. "NEC" (not elsewhere classified) means
  testing was done but the results did not fit a defined entity
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). A patient seeing NOS/NEC
  should know it is a statement about the *testing*, not about how bad the
  tumour is.

## 0.3 Retired-name crosswalk — the single highest-value block on the site

Readers arrive with pathology reports and printouts from before 2021. Every page
needs this table or a page-specific slice of it.

| Old term they may be holding | Status under WHO CNS5 (2021) | What it maps to now |
|---|---|---|
| **Anaplastic astrocytoma** | Retired. The designation "WHO grade 3" replaced "anaplastic astrocytoma" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/); explicitly listed as a retired term (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) | Usually *Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 3* — **or**, if IDH-wildtype with the right molecular features, *Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, grade 4* |
| **Anaplastic oligodendroglioma** | Retired (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) | *Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 3* |
| **Diffuse astrocytoma** (as a standalone diagnosis) | No longer a diagnosis in its own right; NBTS states diffuse astrocytoma and anaplastic astrocytoma are "no longer diagnoses per the WHO classifications" (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/) | *Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 2* (or an IDH-wildtype entity) |
| **Oligoastrocytoma / mixed glioma** | Eliminated. "Hybrid entities eliminated: Oligoastrocytoma no longer appears in classification; molecular testing determines true classification" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/); listed as a retired term (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) | Molecular testing sorts it into *either* astrocytoma IDH-mutant *or* oligodendroglioma IDH-mutant 1p/19q-codeleted |
| **Secondary glioblastoma** / **glioblastoma, IDH-mutant** | Retired. NBTS states that in 2021 the diagnostic criteria for glioblastoma changed and previous terminology — secondary glioblastoma and IDH-mutant glioblastoma — was retired (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/) | *Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4.* Rationale: "even high-grade IDH-mutant astrocytomas are less aggressive than their IDH-wildtype counterparts, so they are no longer called glioblastomas" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) |
| **Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)** | Superseded name. Cancer Research UK notes glioblastoma was "previously called glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)" (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/types/glioma-adults) | *Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4*. "GBM" is still in wide colloquial use |
| **DIPG (diffuse intrinsic pontine glioma)** | Descriptive/anatomic term; the molecular entity is broader | Most are *Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered, CNS WHO grade 4* (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| Grades written **I, II, III, IV** | Roman numerals replaced by Arabic (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) | 1, 2, 3, 4 |

**The headline consequence, stated plainly:** under CNS5, a tumour with an IDH
mutation is **never** called glioblastoma. Glioblastoma is by definition
IDH-wildtype (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/,
https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). A patient whose 2016 report
said "secondary glioblastoma, IDH-mutant" has not been re-diagnosed with a
different disease; the disease has been *renamed* — and the rename reflects that
this tumour behaves differently from IDH-wildtype glioblastoma
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

**The reverse consequence, equally important:** a tumour that looks
low-grade or grade 3 under the microscope can now be **called glioblastoma on
molecular grounds alone**. The 2021 classification "introduced molecular criteria
that allow histologically lower-grade IDH-wildtype astrocytic tumors to be
diagnosed as glioblastoma (so-called 'molecular' glioblastoma)"
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). A patient can therefore be
told "grade 4 / glioblastoma" when nobody saw necrosis on their slides. That
needs explaining or it reads as a contradiction or an error.

## 0.4 Grade vs stage — and why brain tumours are graded

Brain tumours are given a **grade**, not a stage
(https://www.moffitt.org/cancers/brain-cancer/diagnosis/stages/). NCI states
flatly: "there is no standard staging system for adult brain and spinal cord
tumors" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

The reason is biological. Staging elsewhere in oncology describes how far a
cancer has spread through the body. Primary brain tumours very rarely leave the
CNS — malignant gliomas "remain localized to the central nervous system and do
not typically metastasize outside of the brain," with extracranial spread
historically estimated around 0.4% of glioblastoma cases and reported up to about
2% in more recent series (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10073898/).
Tumours "may migrate within the brain, but it is very rare for primary brain
tumors to spread outside of the brain, or away from the central nervous system"
(https://www.moffitt.org/cancers/brain-cancer/diagnosis/stages/).

What grade actually describes: how abnormal the cells look and how fast they
divide. NCI's patient-level description — grade 1: cells "look more like normal
cells under a microscope and grow and spread more slowly"; grade 2: slower than
3–4 but "may spread into nearby tissue and may recur"; grade 3: abnormal-looking,
faster; grade 4: cells "do not look like normal cells under a microscope and grow
and spread very quickly"
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**Caveat to attach every time:** under CNS5 grade is assigned within tumour type
and now includes molecular criteria, so grade alone is no longer the whole
picture (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

## 0.5 What causes gliomas — and what does not

### What is actually established

- **Most gliomas have no identifiable cause.** "Most gliomas occur without
  predisposing conditions" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). NCI:
  "the cause of most adult brain and spinal cord tumors is not known"
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). And:
  "Few definitive observations have been made about environmental or occupational
  causes of primary CNS tumors"
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Ionising radiation to the head** is the one well-established environmental
  risk factor. "Only previous exposure to ionizing radiation and particular
  inherited genetic syndromes are accepted risk factors for glioma; the vast
  majority of cases are thought to occur spontaneously"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6240585/). Macmillan: "People who had
  radiotherapy to the head as children have a slightly higher risk of developing
  a brain tumour later in life"
  (https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).
  Scale matters: Cancer Research UK estimates "less than 1 out of every 100 brain
  tumours (less than 1%) diagnosed in the UK are caused by ionising radiation"
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
- **Age.** "Brain tumours can start at any age. But as we get older our risk of
  developing most cancers, including brain tumours, increases"
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
- **Inherited syndromes** account for a small minority — about 5% of primary
  brain tumours have known hereditary factors
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/nursing-and-health-professions/turcot-syndrome).
- **Vinyl chloride** exposure: NCI lists it, hedged — "Being exposed to vinyl
  chloride may increase the risk of glioma"
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Overweight/obesity:** Cancer Research UK attributes "about 2 out of 100 brain
  tumours (2%)" in the UK to being overweight or obese
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
  Note this is a population-attributable estimate, not a statement about any
  individual, and it covers all brain tumour types, not gliomas specifically.
- **Family history:** "Your risk is higher than other people in the general
  population if you have a close relative who has had a brain tumour"
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
  This is a relative increase on a small baseline, and most such families have no
  identified syndrome.

### The self-blame list — cover each explicitly

Patients arrive convinced they caused this. Each of these deserves its own named
sub-heading so it is findable.

**Cell phones / mobile phones.** NCI's bottom line: "the evidence to date
suggests that cell phone use does not cause brain or other kinds of cancer in
humans"
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet).
Mechanism: phones emit radiofrequency radiation in the **non-ionising** range —
"the energy is too low to damage DNA"
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet).
Evidence: the Danish cohort of >358,000 subscribers found "no association ...
between cell phone use and the incidence of glioma, meningioma, or acoustic
neuroma"; the UK Million Women Study found self-reported cell phone use "was not
associated with an increased risk of glioma, meningioma, or non-central nervous
system tumors"; COSMOS (264,574 participants) found "the cumulative amount of
mobile phone call-time was not associated with the risk of developing glioma,
meningioma, or acoustic neuroma"
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet).
Population check: adult glioma incidence rates stayed stable in the US, Nordic
countries and Australia across decades in which mobile phone use exploded
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3297541/). *Honest caveat to include:*
IARC classified radiofrequency fields from mobile phones as "possibly
carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B) in 2011 based on limited evidence
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet);
some case-control analyses in the heaviest-use groups have suggested a small
increase that investigators judged inconclusive
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet).
Macmillan states plainly: "Mobile phones, power lines and viruses have all been
suggested as possible causes of brain tumours. But research has not found any
clear evidence linking these things to brain tumours"
(https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).

**Head injury.** Macmillan: "Hurting your head or brain in an accident (head
injury) has not been shown to cause brain tumours"
(https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).
*Honest caveat:* this is an area of live research — a 2023 UCL mouse study
proposed a mechanism by which injury-related inflammation could interact with
pre-existing mutations, and reported an association in a patient cohort
(https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2023/feb/head-injuries-could-be-risk-factor-developing-brain-cancer).
Framing for patients: the reason so many people remember a head injury before
diagnosis is partly that a bang on the head is memorable and often prompts the
scan that finds a tumour that was already there. Do not overstate the new work;
it has not changed guidance from the major patient organisations.

**Stress.** No causal link established. Cancer Research UK addresses stress and
cancer generally: research "has not shown a consistent link between stress and
cancer"
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/causes-of-cancer/cancer-myths-questions/can-stress-cause-cancer).
Duke's Tisch Brain Tumor Center states the consensus directly: stress does not
cause brain tumours
(https://tischbraintumorcenter.duke.edu/blog/can-stress-cause-brain-tumors).

**Diet.** No specific food, sweetener, or diet is an established cause of
gliomas; the authoritative sources listing brain tumour risk factors do not
include diet
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes,
https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).
Say this positively and without hedging into implying guilt.

**Power lines and viruses.** Not linked
(https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).

**Not contagious. Not caused by anything the patient or family did.** Anchor this
to the "cause of most adult brain and spinal cord tumors is not known" statement
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

### Inherited syndromes where the link is genuine

About 5% of primary brain tumours have known hereditary factors
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/nursing-and-health-professions/turcot-syndrome).

- **Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1)** — associated with gliomas including optic
  pathway gliomas and pilocytic astrocytomas; NF1 loss is a MAPK-pathway
  alteration (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
  https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
  NF1 is also among the MAPK alterations found in high-grade astrocytoma with
  piloid features (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).
- **Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2)** — listed as a CNS tumour predisposition
  syndrome (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
  **Precision point:** NF2 is much more strongly associated with schwannomas,
  meningiomas and ependymomas than with diffuse gliomas — do not imply it is a
  glioma syndrome in the same way NF1 is.
- **Li-Fraumeni syndrome (germline TP53)** — associated with high-grade gliomas
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/); listed among syndromes
  predisposing to glioblastoma
  (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z).
- **Turcot syndrome / Lynch syndrome / constitutional mismatch repair deficiency
  (CMMRD)** — mismatch-repair genes MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2. Lynch and Turcot
  type 1 are autosomal dominant; CMMRD is autosomal recessive
  (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z). High-grade gliomas occur
  in this context (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z).
- **Tuberous sclerosis** — subependymal giant cell astrocytoma
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).
- **von Hippel-Lindau** and **Gorlin syndrome** are listed as brain-tumour
  predisposition syndromes but are not glioma syndromes per se — VHL causes
  haemangioblastomas, Gorlin medulloblastoma
  (https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes,
  https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**The key patient message:** for the great majority of people, a glioma is not
inherited and does not put children or siblings at meaningfully raised risk. Only
about 5% of primary brain tumours have known hereditary factors
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/nursing-and-health-professions/turcot-syndrome).
The molecular changes found in the tumour (IDH, 1p/19q, TERT, EGFR, and so on)
are **somatic** — they happened in the tumour cells only and are not passed on.
This distinction is not made clearly anywhere on the consumer web and is a real
gap BrainHarbor can fill.

## 0.6 What a glioma does to the brain — the mechanism of symptoms

This is the "what is it actually doing to my brain" section. Six mechanisms,
which combine.

**1. Mass effect (pushing).** "Tumor can damage neural tissue or displace it by
compression, leading to focal symptoms. Tumors and peritumoral edema form a
space-occupying lesion that causes mass effect on nearby structures and elevates
ICP" (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6). The skull is
a closed box; anything that takes up room pushes on something else.

**2. Vasogenic oedema (swelling).** The tumour disrupts the blood–brain barrier;
"the disruption of the blood–brain barrier by the tumor leads to vasogenic edema
that is probably one of the main causes of clinical impairment"
(https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6). Mechanism: a
deficiency in the blood–brain barrier with "consequent extravasation of water,
electrolytes, and plasma proteins from altered tumor microvessels"
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2588896/). "The resultant expansion of
the cerebral interstitial space contributes to the elevated intracranial pressure
observed with brain tumors" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2588896/).
**Patient-critical implication:** a lot of symptoms come from the *swelling*, not
the tumour itself — which is why steroids can improve someone dramatically within
a day or two without the tumour having shrunk at all
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/). Raised ICP "is more
commonly seen in high-grade gliomas complicated by blood–brain barrier disruption
and vasogenic oedema"
(https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6).

**3. Infiltration (growing through).** Diffuse gliomas migrate through normal
brain rather than forming a discrete ball. They "tend to grow along blood vessels
or white matter fibers, which serve as a route for cancer cell migration," with
the corpus callosum, internal capsule, optic radiation, anterior commissure,
fornix and subependymal regions as favoured routes
(https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/14/10/2507). Tumour cells "invade beyond regions
visible as abnormal areas on MRI"
(https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full).
**This is the single most important fact for explaining why "they got it all"
is not the same thing as cured.**

**4. Raised intracranial pressure (ICP).** "Symptoms of acutely increased
intracranial pressure include typically headache, nausea, vomiting, and
eventually coma. Frequent symptoms are also visual disturbance (gaze paresis,
reduced vision) and dizziness"
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/B9780128023952000043).
NCI's patient-level list of the classic pattern includes "morning headaches or
headaches relieved by vomiting," frequent nausea and vomiting, and unusual
sleepiness (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
Explain *why* it is worse in the morning: lying flat overnight raises ICP.

**5. Seizures.** Seizures happen because tumour and the irritated brain around it
disturb the normal electrical balance of nearby cortex. Seizures are common in
glioma and are frequently the presenting event
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Specific, well-evidenced
mechanism in IDH-mutant tumours: mutant IDH converts alpha-ketoglutarate to
**D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2-HG)**, which is structurally similar to the excitatory
neurotransmitter glutamate and may increase neuronal firing by mimicking
glutamate at the NMDA receptor
(https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000003911); D-2-HG also
"promote[s] neuronal spiking by changing the metabolic profile of surrounding
neurons and activating the mTOR signaling pathway ... in the human peritumoral
epileptic cortex"
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134). IDH
mutation is an independent risk factor for epilepsy in glioma and is associated
with worse post-operative seizure control and more resistance to anti-seizure
medication
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134). This
explains, honestly and without hype, why slower-growing IDH-mutant tumours are
*more* likely to present with seizures than fast IDH-wildtype ones.

**6. Hydrocephalus.** If a tumour blocks the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, fluid
backs up and pressure rises. Obstruction "most commonly occurs at the foramina
[of] Monro, the aqueduct of Sylvius, the fourth ventricle, and foramen magnum,
but most tumors with a significant size can obstruct at any point of CSF
pathways" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560875/). Posterior fossa and
tectal-region tumours are the classic culprits
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560875/). Acute hydrocephalus symptoms:
"altered mental status, nausea/vomiting, headache and up-gaze palsy (Parinaud's
syndrome)" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560875/). Management may
include ventriculoperitoneal shunting
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).

### Why symptoms map to location

The rule to teach: **the symptom tells you where, the scan tells you what.** A
glioma disrupts whatever job the brain does at that spot.

- **Frontal lobe** — personality and behaviour change, planning and
  decision-making, motivation, and (dominant side) speech production. In
  frontal-lobe astrocytoma "changes in behavior and personality can be present
  months to years before diagnosis"
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/).
  Frontal is the commonest oligodendroglioma site
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Temporal lobe** — seizures, memory, language comprehension.
- **Parietal lobe** — sensation, spatial awareness, reading/writing/calculation.
- **Occipital lobe** — vision and visual field loss.
- **Cerebellum** — balance, coordination, gait; and posterior fossa position
  makes hydrocephalus more likely
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560875/).
- **Brainstem** — cranial nerve problems, double vision, facial weakness,
  swallowing, and, near the aqueduct, hydrocephalus
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560875/).
- **Spinal cord** — NCI: "Back pain or pain that spreads from the back towards
  the arms or legs," bowel/urinary change, "weakness or numbness in the arms or
  legs," and walking difficulty
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

Presenting symptom set overall: "new-onset headaches, seizures, cognitive
deficits, or focal neurological deficits," plus nausea and vomiting
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). NCI's patient list adds vision,
hearing and speech problems, loss of appetite, personality or mood changes,
balance problems, weakness, and unusual sleepiness
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**Incidental discovery is real:** "approximately 3% to 10% of low-grade gliomas
are found incidentally upon work-up for other conditions"
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Some readers found theirs after
an unrelated scan and feel unmoored by having no symptoms; name that experience.

## 0.7 Molecular marker glossary (write once, reuse on all six pages)

| Marker | What it is | What it means diagnostically |
|---|---|---|
| **IDH1 / IDH2** | Genes for isocitrate dehydrogenase, a metabolic enzyme. Mutation is an early, initiating event (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/) | The first fork in the road for adult diffuse glioma. IDH-mutant → astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma. IDH-wildtype (with the right extra findings) → glioblastoma (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). Mutant IDH produces the oncometabolite **2-hydroxyglutarate**, which causes widespread DNA hypermethylation (the G-CIMP phenotype) and impairs cell differentiation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). NCI notes "the presence of an IDH1 or IDH2 variant is a strong prognostic factor" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq) |
| **1p/19q co-deletion** | Loss of the short arm of chromosome 1 and the long arm of chromosome 19, together, through a translocation event (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq) | **Defines oligodendroglioma** when combined with IDH mutation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9208578/). Also predicts benefit from chemotherapy — "a powerful prognostic factor and may predict for response to chemotherapy" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq) |
| **ATRX** | Chromatin remodelling gene; loss shown by immunostaining | Loss points to **astrocytoma**; ATRX is typically **retained** in oligodendroglioma (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). Near-universal loss (95%) in H3 G34-mutant diffuse hemispheric glioma (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| **TP53** | Tumour suppressor | Frequently mutated alongside ATRX in IDH-mutant astrocytoma (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| **TERT promoter mutation** | Keeps telomerase switched on so cells avoid ageing out | One of three molecular criteria that can make an IDH-wildtype astrocytic tumour a glioblastoma (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). Also present in most oligodendrogliomas — so **TERT alone does not mean glioblastoma**; it must be read alongside IDH (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| **CDKN2A/B homozygous deletion** | Loss of both copies of tumour-suppressor genes controlling the cell cycle | In IDH-mutant **astrocytoma**, homozygous deletion is "diagnostic of a CNS WHO grade 4 tumor" even without necrosis or microvascular proliferation (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). In oligodendroglioma it "may indicate aggressive grade 3 behavior" (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |
| **EGFR amplification** | Extra copies of the epidermal growth factor receptor gene | One of the three molecular criteria for glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) |
| **+7/−10** | Gain of the whole of chromosome 7 with loss of the whole of chromosome 10 | Third molecular criterion for glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) |
| **MGMT promoter methylation** | Methylation switches off a DNA-repair gene, so the tumour is less able to undo damage from alkylating chemotherapy | Predicts benefit from temozolomide; "benefit from TMZ chemotherapy was largely limited to patients with glioblastoma possessing a methylated MGMT promoter" and unmethylated status "might be a predictor for lack of efficacy" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/). NCI calls it "an independent prognostic factor for improved survival in newly diagnosed glioblastoma" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). It is a **treatment-guiding** marker, not a diagnosis-defining one |
| **H3 K27M / H3 K27-altered** | Histone H3 alteration (lysine-to-methionine at position 27) that inhibits the PRC2 complex; also EZHIP overexpression or EGFR mutation qualifies (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/, https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) | Defines **diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered, CNS WHO grade 4** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) |
| **H3 G34 (G34R/V)** | Histone H3F3A missense mutation, glycine→arginine or valine at position 34 (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) | Defines **diffuse hemispheric glioma, H3 G34-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4** (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/) |
| **BRAF (V600E mutation, KIAA1549-BRAF fusion)** | MAPK-pathway activation | Characteristic of pilocytic astrocytoma and pediatric low-grade gliomas; "RAF alterations occur in approximately 80% of pilocytic astrocytomas" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). Directly targetable by drugs (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html) |
| **CIC / FUBP1** | Genes on 19q and 1p | Alterations characteristic of oligodendroglioma, alongside 1p/19q co-deletion (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/) |
| **DNA methylation profiling** | Reads the tumour's epigenetic "fingerprint" | Increasingly used as a reliable complementary classifier across glioma types, including reliably classifying oligodendrogliomas (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) |

**How molecular findings override the microscope — say this explicitly:**
CNS5 acknowledges situations where histology and genetics diverge, and the
integrated diagnosis resolves them
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). Two concrete examples to
use on the pages:
1. Slides look like a grade 2 or 3 astrocytoma, but the tumour is IDH-wildtype
   with TERT promoter mutation / EGFR amplification / +7−10 → diagnosis is
   **glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, grade 4**
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/,
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
2. Slides show no necrosis and no microvascular proliferation, but the IDH-mutant
   astrocytoma has homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion → **grade 4**
   (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

## 0.8 Treatment modality glossary (write once, reuse)

- **Maximal safe resection.** Surgery to remove as much tumour as can be removed
  without causing unacceptable neurological harm; "maximal safe resection remains
  the mainstay" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Surgery also
  supplies the tissue needed for the molecular tests that determine the
  diagnosis, and can immediately relieve pressure
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Awake craniotomy with brain mapping.** The patient is woken during surgery so
  the team can stimulate and test speech, movement and cognition and stay out of
  critical areas; used to resect lower-grade gliomas in locations previously
  deemed inoperable "without severe permanent neurological sequelae," with
  improved quality of life in terms of seizure control
  (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2023.1089139/full).
- **5-ALA fluorescence guidance.** A drink that makes high-grade tumour tissue
  fluoresce under a special light; gives "statistically significant improvement in
  the extent of resection of high-grade gliomas over conventional stereotactic
  navigation" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Supratotal resection.** Removing beyond the abnormality visible on MRI,
  because tumour cells extend past it
  (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full).
- **Radiation therapy.** Standard high-grade dosing is 60 Gy in 30 fractions;
  50–54 Gy is used for low-grade tumours
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). For
  oligodendroglioma, dose ranges 45–65 Gy with no significant survival benefit
  demonstrated between the lower and higher ends
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Temozolomide (TMZ).** Oral alkylating chemotherapy, generally better
  tolerated than PCV (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **PCV.** Procarbazine + lomustine (CCNU) + vincristine, usually limited to six
  cycles because of adverse events including leukopenia and thrombocytopenia
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Tumour Treating Fields (TTFields / Optune).** A wearable device delivering
  alternating electric fields through scalp arrays that disrupt cell division;
  started 4–7 weeks after radiotherapy during maintenance TMZ in selected
  patients (supratentorial glioblastoma, KPS ≥60), with mainly skin irritation as
  toxicity, endorsed by NCCN and ASTRO 2025 guidelines
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/).
- **Bevacizumab.** Anti-VEGF antibody; reduces neovascularisation and vascular
  permeability, "helping to stabilize the blood-brain barrier"; used in recurrent
  disease, palliation, and radiation necrosis, and to reduce peritumoral oedema
  and steroid requirements (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). In
  newly diagnosed glioblastoma, "bevacizumab did not improve OS in either trial"
  (RTOG 0825, AVAglio), though progression-free survival improved modestly
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Vorasidenib (Voranigo).** Oral dual IDH1/IDH2 inhibitor. FDA granted
  traditional approval on 6 August 2024 for adults and children aged ≥12 with
  **grade 2** astrocytoma or oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1/IDH2
  mutation, **following surgery**
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/). Based on the phase 3
  INDIGO trial in patients with residual or recurrent grade 2 IDH-mutant glioma
  whose only treatment had been surgery
  (https://servier.us/blog/serviers-voranigo-vorasidenib-tablets-receives-fda-approval-as-first-targeted-therapy-for-grade-2-idh-mutant-glioma/).
  Described by Dana-Farber as the first FDA approval for glioma patients in
  decades
  (https://www.dana-farber.org/newsroom/news-releases/2024/fda-approves-new-therapy-for-glioma-patients-for-first-time-in-decades).
- **Dordaviprone (ONC201).** Brain-penetrant DRD2 antagonist / ClpP agonist; FDA
  approved for recurrent H3 K27M-mutant diffuse midline glioma
  (https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.23.01134). Note there is genuine
  professional debate about the strength of the evidence behind this accelerated
  approval
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/advance-article/doi/10.1093/neuonc/noag117/8690955).
- **Steroids (dexamethasone).** Treat vasogenic oedema, not the tumour;
  anti-oedema effects are "rapid, with symptom relief observed within 24–48 h,
  making it the treatment of choice for managing intracranial pressure (ICP) and
  neurological symptoms" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/).
  Usually combined with an anti-ulcer agent
  (https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/283252-medication).
- **Anti-seizure medication.** Levetiracetam is commonly chosen "because it lacks
  the effects on the P450 system seen with phenytoin and carbamazepine, which can
  interfere with antineoplastic therapy"
  (https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/283252-medication). **Routine
  preventive anti-seizure medication in patients who have never had a seizure is
  not recommended** — SNO/EANO guidance recommends against routine prophylaxis in
  newly diagnosed brain tumours
  (https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/info/hp/cancer/if-hp-cancer-guide-cns012-seizures.pdf).
- **Clinical trials.** NCI's PDQ and the patient organisations both frame trial
  participation as a standard option to discuss, not a last resort
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).

## 0.9 Prognosis vocabulary — meaning only, no numbers

BrainHarbor publishes no survival figures. It should still teach the vocabulary,
because patients will meet these words in appointments and on other sites, and
because understanding what the words *mean* is what defuses them.

Definitions, in NCI's own terms
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/prognosis):

- **Overall survival** — the percentage of people with a specific cancer who have
  not died from any cause during a set period.
- **Cancer-specific survival** — the percentage who have not died *from the
  cancer* during a set period, commonly 5 years.
- **Relative survival** — a comparison: the percentage of people with the cancer
  surviving a given period compared with people who do not have that cancer.
- **Disease-free / progression-free survival** — the percentage with no sign of
  cancer, or no growth, after treatment.
- **Median survival** — the halfway point. If median survival is 12 months, then
  12 months after diagnosis half of that group were still alive
  (https://cancer.ca/en/cancer-information/what-is-cancer/prognosis-and-survival).
  Explicitly teach: a median is **not** a deadline, and half the group by
  definition did better than it.

Why a population number does not predict one person, sourced:

- "Because statistics are based on large groups of people, they cannot be used to
  predict exactly what will happen to you"
  (https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/prognosis).
- "Everyone is different. Treatments and how people respond to treatment can
  differ greatly" (https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/prognosis).
- The numbers are old by the time they are published: "it takes years to see the
  benefit of new treatments," so published statistics may not reflect the
  treatments available now
  (https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/prognosis).
- "A prognosis is an educated guess. Your doctor cannot be certain how it will go
  for you" (https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/diagnosis-staging/prognosis).
- "Researchers can study cancer statistics for trends but cannot use that data to
  say exactly what will happen to you or anyone else who has cancer"
  (https://cancer.ca/en/cancer-information/what-is-cancer/prognosis-and-survival).

Additional honest points worth making, all sourced above:
- Published statistics for glioma types are drawn from patients diagnosed under
  the *old* classification, and CNS5 moved tumours between categories — so the
  group a number describes may not be the group a reader is in
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/).
- Prognostic factors listed by clinicians include age, comorbidities, grade and
  location, presence of hydrocephalus, response to adjuvant therapy, and extent
  of resection (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/) — i.e. it is a
  combination specific to one person, not a single number.

## 0.10 Epidemiology (context only, non-prognostic)

- Gliomas accounted for 22.9% of all primary brain and CNS tumours in US
  registry data for 2017–2021
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290).
- Glioblastoma is the most commonly occurring malignant brain/CNS
  histopathology — 13.9% of all tumours and 51.5% of all malignant tumours
  (2017–2021), incidence 3.28 per 100,000 (2018–2022 data)
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290,
  https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/Supplement_4/iv1/8285946).
- Diffuse astrocytic and oligodendroglial tumours as a group: incidence 4.37 per
  100,000 (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290).
- IDH-mutant astrocytomas: average annual incidence 1.21 per 100,000; typically
  under age 55, mean age of onset 38
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/).
- Oligodendroglioma: incidence about 0.2 per 100,000; slight male predominance;
  peak in the fourth–fifth decades with a secondary paediatric peak at ages 6–12
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- Glioblastoma median age at diagnosis 66
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).
- Gliomas overall: about 6 cases per 100,000 per year in the US
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).
- Gliomas are more common in men; meningiomas more common in women
  (https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors).

# PART 1 — GLIOMA (THE UMBRELLA PAGE)

## 1.1 What it is

A glioma is a tumour that starts in the glial (support) cells of the brain or
spinal cord (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). "Glioma" is a
**family name**, not a single disease — Cancer Research UK: gliomas are brain
tumours originating from glial cells, and "some types of gliomas grow faster than
others"
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/types/glioma-adults).

Frame the umbrella relationship explicitly, because this is where readers get
lost. Use a nesting model:

```
GLIOMA  (family: tumours of glial cells)
├── Adult-type diffuse gliomas       ← the three big adult diagnoses
│   ├── Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant                    grade 2, 3, 4
│   ├── Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant & 1p/19q-codeleted   grade 2, 3
│   └── Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype                 grade 4
├── Pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas       grade 1
├── Pediatric-type diffuse high-grade gliomas      grade 4
│   (incl. diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered)
├── Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas
│   (incl. pilocytic astrocytoma)                  grade 1 and up
└── Ependymal tumours (ependymoma)
```
(structure from https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/,
https://www.pathologyoutlines.com/topic/cnstumorwhoclassification.html)

And state the cross-cutting descriptors that are **not** diagnoses:
"low-grade glioma," "high-grade glioma," "diffuse glioma" — these are *groupings*
that describe behaviour, and each contains several actual diagnoses.

Location in general: adult-type diffuse gliomas are primarily supratentorial —
IDH-mutant astrocytomas "primarily supratentorial; rarely occur infratentorially"
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html); glioblastoma occurs
"primarily in cerebral hemispheres; also occurs in brainstem, cerebellum, and
spinal cord"
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).

## 1.2 How it starts

Use §0.5 in full. Umbrella-page specifics:
- Molecular initiating events differ by type: in IDH-mutant tumours "IDH mutation
  [is] the initiating event with subsequent TP53 and ATRX alterations
  (astrocytomas) or 1p/19q codeletion (oligodendrogliomas) followed by TERT
  mutations" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/).
- Mechanism of IDH mutation: elevates 2-hydroxyglutarate, "causing histone
  methylation and DNA hypermethylation," disrupting normal cellular metabolism
  and epigenetic regulation (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).
- Most pediatric-type low-grade gliomas and pilocytic astrocytomas are driven by
  MAPK-pathway alterations
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
  https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).

## 1.3 What it does to the brain

Use §0.6 in full. This is the natural home for the definitive version of the
mechanism explanation; other pages should link back here rather than repeat it.

## 1.4 How it is named and graded

Use §0.2, §0.3, §0.4, §0.7. The umbrella page carries the master retired-name
crosswalk.

Add: what a pathology report actually contains and in what order — tumour type,
CNS WHO grade, the molecular findings, and possibly NOS/NEC
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). Explain that the full
molecular result can take days to a couple of weeks after surgery, and that the
first, provisional diagnosis can change once it comes back — this is normal, not
an error.

## 1.5 Typical course

Set expectations at the family level:
- Grade 1, circumscribed gliomas can be cured by complete surgical removal
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade,
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).
- Grade 2 and above are diffuse "and because of that, incurable"
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade); diffuse
  gliomas are "universally incurable despite multimodality standard-of-care
  treatments" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9913857/). Handle this
  with care and pair it with the fact that "incurable" is not "untreatable" and
  is not a timeline.
- "Central nervous system (CNS) tumors often recur, sometimes many years after
  treatment" (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- Long-term MRI surveillance is the norm across the family.

## 1.6 Treatments (family level)

Surgery → radiation → chemotherapy is the general scaffold, but *which* parts
apply, in what order, and whether any apply at all, depends entirely on type,
grade and molecular markers (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). "The WHO
classification of gliomas is used to guide treatment"
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/) — this sentence justifies the
entire naming-and-grading section to a reader who wonders why the labels matter.

Supportive care runs alongside: anti-seizure medication, steroids, DVT
prophylaxis, and shunting for hydrocephalus where needed
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).

## 1.7 Questions patients most need answered (umbrella)

1. Is a glioma cancer? (Malignant vs benign; and why "benign" in the brain is not
   the same as harmless — NCI: benign brain tumours "grow and press on nearby
   areas of the brain"
   (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).)
2. Why doesn't my tumour have a stage? (§0.4)
3. My report says something different from what my doctor said / from what I read
   online. Why? (§0.3 — retired names)
4. Did I cause this? (§0.5)
5. Will my children get it? (§0.5 — 5%, somatic vs germline)
6. Can it spread to the rest of my body? (§0.4 — very rare)
7. What do all these letters on my report mean? (§0.7)
8. Why does it matter so much which exact type it is? (treatment is chosen by
   type + grade + molecular markers —
   https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/)
9. What is happening in my brain to cause these symptoms? (§0.6)
10. Why am I so tired / why has my personality changed? (§0.6 location mapping;
    frontal lobe changes can predate diagnosis by months to years —
    https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/)

# PART 2 — LOW-GRADE GLIOMA

## 2.1 What it is — and the terminology trap

**"Low-grade glioma" is a descriptive grouping, not a diagnosis.** It generally
means CNS WHO grade 1 and grade 2 gliomas
(https://www.moffitt.org/cancers/brain-cancer/diagnosis/stages/), but the WHO
"recommends avoiding the term 'low-grade glioma,' since it groups together almost
two dozen WHO grade I and grade II glial neoplasms"
(https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade). This page
therefore has to do two jobs: explain the term because everyone uses it, and
immediately push the reader toward their actual diagnosis.

**The single most important honest point on this page:** grade 1 and grade 2 are
not the same thing at all.

- **Grade 1** gliomas (e.g. pilocytic astrocytoma) are "benign, well delineated
  and can be cured by surgery"
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade). The
  pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas are also grade 1 with "generally good
  prognosis after resection"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **Grade 2** diffuse gliomas are diffuse and infiltrative: "from grade 2 and
  above, the tumors are diffuse and because of that, incurable"
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade). They are
  "locally invasive and frequently exhibit malignant degeneration into
  higher-grade gliomas"
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade). Patients
  "are often told by physicians prior to oncology referral that grade II glioma is
  curable and perhaps not even malignant," which is not correct
  (https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade).

Some clinicians use "**lower-grade glioma**" for grade 2–3 diffuse gliomas as a
group — this is the usage in the landmark TCGA analysis
(https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1402121). Flag the term so readers
who meet it are not confused.

**Which actual diagnoses live under "low-grade glioma":**
- Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 2
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)
- Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 2
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)
- Pilocytic astrocytoma and other circumscribed astrocytic gliomas
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/)
- Pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas: diffuse astrocytoma MYB/MYBL1-altered;
  angiocentric glioma; PLNTY; diffuse low-grade glioma, MAPK pathway-altered
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
  https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)

Location: predominantly supratentorial for the adult diffuse grade 2 tumours
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html); pilocytic astrocytomas
often cerebellum, optic pathway and other midline structures
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560614/).

## 2.2 How it starts

Same as §0.5 plus grade-2-specific mechanisms:
- Adult grade 2 diffuse gliomas begin with an IDH1/IDH2 mutation as the
  initiating event, followed by either TP53+ATRX (astrocytoma path) or 1p/19q
  co-deletion (oligodendroglioma path), then TERT
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/).
- Pediatric-type low-grade gliomas and pilocytic astrocytomas are MAPK-driven,
  most commonly via BRAF fusion or BRAF V600E, without IDH or histone mutations
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
  https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html). "More than 80% of pilocytic
  astrocytomas have gene alterations in some component of the MEK signaling
  pathway" (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).
- NF1 is the relevant hereditary link for optic pathway / low-grade gliomas
  (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html,
  https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).

## 2.3 What it does to the brain

Mechanistic profile of a low-grade glioma differs from a high-grade one and this
is worth its own explanation:

- **Seizures dominate.** Low-grade gliomas often present with seizures
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/ — IDH-mutant astrocytomas
  and the pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas both "present with
  seizures").
  `[CORRECTED AT WI-513 — this bullet previously quoted "most of these tumors do
  not cause neurologic deficits at the time of diagnosis" and attributed it to
  cancernetwork.com. That article contains ZERO occurrences of "seizure" and
  ZERO of "deficit"; the sentence is not in it. The seizure half is real and is
  now sourced to PMC9723092. The no-deficit half has no support in any fetched
  source and must not be published until one is found.]`
  IDH-mutant status is itself an independent risk factor for epileptogenesis via
  D-2-HG (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134,
  https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000003911).
- **Less oedema, less enhancement.** "Low-grade tumors usually do not enhance,
  while high-grade tumors have varying degrees of enhancement"
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/) — because the blood–brain
  barrier is less disrupted. For oligodendroglioma specifically, "peritumoral
  edema and hemorrhage is uncommon"
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). This explains why a person
  with a large-looking grade 2 tumour may feel largely fine.
- **Slow displacement, so the brain compensates.** Symptoms accrue slowly and can
  be attributed to stress or ageing for a long time.
- **Infiltration continues regardless.** Tumour cells extend beyond MRI-visible
  abnormality
  (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full).
- 3–10% of low-grade gliomas are found incidentally
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).

## 2.4 How it is named and graded

- Grade 2 criteria for IDH-mutant astrocytoma: "low-to-moderate cellularity,
  minimal/absent mitotic activity, no necrosis or microvascular proliferation"
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- Grade 2 oligodendroglioma: "branching capillary network, low mitotic activity,
  low Ki-67 index" (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- Pediatric-type diffuse low-grade gliomas are grade 1
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **Molecular override to flag prominently:** an IDH-**wildtype** tumour that
  looks grade 2 under the microscope is diagnosed as glioblastoma grade 4 if it
  has TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or +7/−10
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). This is the reason a
  "low-grade-looking" tumour can come back labelled grade 4, and it is a specific
  and terrifying experience that this page must address head-on.
- Also flag: homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion moves an IDH-mutant astrocytoma to
  grade 4 without any grade-4 histology
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

## 2.5 Typical course

**Active surveillance ("watch and wait") is a legitimate option for some people.**
"Initial observation (watchful waiting or surveillance) is a reasonable option for
low-risk patients" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/).
Explain what it involves — serial MRI at intervals, not doing nothing — and why
it can be the right choice (delaying radiation avoids its late cognitive costs).

**The high-risk/low-risk split.** RTOG 9802 defined low-risk as age <40 with
gross total resection, and high-risk as age ≥40 and/or subtotal resection or
biopsy only (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/). **Important
honesty flag:** those criteria were designed to select trial participants, not to
define who needs treatment — "there is no clinical justification for a strict age
cutoff, and other prognostic factors should be considered"
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/). Modern practice
increasingly weights molecular subtype and extent of resection alongside age.

**Transformation to higher grade.** This is the central fear on this page and it
should be stated accurately, without numbers where they would function as
prognosis. What is known: malignant transformation — histological and/or genomic
progression from grade 2 to grade 3/4 at recurrence — is a known behaviour of IDH-
mutant gliomas
(https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2022.40.16_suppl.2065). Risk factors
previously identified include gemistocytic morphology, a large proportion of
residual tumour, patient age, and recurrence after radiotherapy
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11306837/). Transformation is
associated with worse outcomes in astrocytomas, a trend not seen in
oligodendrogliomas
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475770/). Mechanism: continued
molecular evolution beyond the initiating IDH mutation, "a broad spectrum of
genetic changes that cannot be explained by a simple genetic event"
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/).

**Recurrence is expected in diffuse grade 2 disease** and can occur many years
later (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
By contrast, grade 1 circumscribed tumours can be cured by complete resection
(https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/); angiocentric glioma has an "indolent
course with rare recurrence post-resection"
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

## 2.6 Treatments

- **Surgery first, maximal safe resection**
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Awake craniotomy with direct
  electrical stimulation makes resection possible in eloquent areas previously
  deemed inoperable, without severe permanent deficits, and improves seizure
  control
  (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2023.1089139/full).
  Supratotal resection is used in some centres because tumour extends beyond MRI
  abnormality
  (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full).
- **Observation** for selected low-risk patients
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/).
- **Radiation then PCV for high-risk grade 2 disease.** NCI: "Radiation therapy
  followed by six cycles of vincristine (PCV) chemotherapy is a recommended
  option" based on RTOG 9802
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). RTOG 9802
  demonstrated a clear benefit of adding PCV to radiotherapy in high-risk
  low-grade glioma and was the first trial to show an overall survival increase
  from post-radiation chemotherapy in this setting
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7527157/). Low-grade radiation dosing
  is 50–54 Gy (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Vorasidenib** for residual or recurrent **grade 2** IDH-mutant astrocytoma or
  oligodendroglioma after surgery — FDA approved 6 August 2024 for patients ≥12
  years (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/). This is the most
  significant recent change on this page and is the reason a low-grade glioma
  page written before mid-2024 is out of date.
- **Targeted therapy for BRAF-altered pediatric-type low-grade glioma:**
  dabrafenib + trametinib is indicated in patients aged ≥1 year with BRAF V600E
  low-grade glioma requiring systemic therapy, and is "now considered the standard
  FDA-approved treatment for this group"; tovorafenib is approved for relapsed or
  refractory pediatric LGG with BRAF fusion/rearrangement or BRAF V600 mutation
  from 6 months of age (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).
  Selumetinib (MEK1/2 inhibitor) is used in pediatric LGG
  (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).
- **Radiation is generally avoided in children** because of long-term cognitive
  impairment, personality change, hypopituitarism, motor abnormalities and
  secondary tumour risk (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Seizure management** is a primary treatment goal here, not an afterthought —
  see §0.8.

## 2.7 Questions patients most need answered

1. "Low grade" — does that mean it's not cancer? (No; grade 2 diffuse gliomas are
   malignant and incurable, and patients are frequently told otherwise —
   https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade)
2. Why is my doctor suggesting we just watch it?
   (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/)
3. Isn't waiting dangerous?
4. Will it turn into a higher grade? What are the signs?
   (https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2022.40.16_suppl.2065)
5. Why did my report say grade 4 when it looked low-grade on the scan?
   (molecular glioblastoma / CDKN2A —
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/,
   https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)
6. Why am I having seizures if the tumour is small and slow?
   (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134)
7. Should I have radiation now or later?
8. What is vorasidenib and am I eligible?
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/)
9. Can I still work / drive? (driving law after seizures varies by jurisdiction —
   route to a clinician, do not state rules)
10. If they removed all of it, why do I still need scans?
    (https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full)

# PART 3 — HIGH-GRADE GLIOMA

## 3.1 What it is

"High-grade glioma" is again a **grouping**, meaning CNS WHO grade 3 and grade 4
gliomas (https://www.moffitt.org/cancers/brain-cancer/diagnosis/stages/). It is
not a diagnosis and it does not appear on a WHO CNS5 report as a standalone
label.

Diagnoses that sit under it:
- Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 3 or 4
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)
- Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 3 — note
  **there is no grade 4 oligodendroglioma**
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)
- Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, CNS WHO grade 4
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/)
- Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered, CNS WHO grade 4
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/)
- Diffuse hemispheric glioma, H3 G34-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/)
- Diffuse pediatric-type high-grade glioma, H3-wildtype and IDH-wildtype, grade 4
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)
- Infant-type hemispheric glioma (grade not yet assigned)
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html)
- High-grade astrocytoma with piloid features — a circumscribed astrocytic
  glioma, "an aggressive astrocytic neoplasm," most often posterior fossa, middle-
  aged adults, with MAPK alteration + ATRX mutation + homozygous CDKN2A/B
  deletion (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/)

**The critical framing point for this page:** two tumours can both be "grade 4"
and be biologically very different. "Even high-grade IDH-mutant astrocytomas are
less aggressive than their IDH-wildtype counterparts," which is precisely why
CNS5 stopped calling them glioblastomas
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). And because CNS5 grades
within tumour type, identical grades across different types do not indicate
equivalent behaviour (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

**Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered — where it sits.** Midline structures:
thalamus is the most affected site in adults, followed by brainstem and spinal
cord; in children the pons predominates (this is what "DIPG" describes)
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11640674/,
https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). It can also occur in adults
aged 18–40, more often male
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11640674/). Bilateral thalamic
involvement is common with EGFR-mutant subtype; spinal cord involvement is more
common in older patients (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
Adult tumours "exhibit less aggressive clinical behavior" than the paediatric
form (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11640674/).

## 3.2 How it starts

- Same baseline as §0.5 — most have no identifiable cause.
- Two distinct routes to a grade 4 astrocytic tumour: **de novo** IDH-wildtype
  glioblastoma (the great majority, older adults), versus IDH-mutant astrocytoma
  that has **transformed** upward from grade 2/3
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475770/). The old name for the second
  route was "secondary glioblastoma" — now retired
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).
- Histone-mutant tumours: H3 K27M substitution inhibits the PRC2 complex, with
  loss of H3K27 trimethylation detectable by immunohistochemistry
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). H3 G34 mutation "results in
  reduced binding of SETD2 and KDM2A to the tail of the histone H3 protein"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- Hereditary syndromes that genuinely raise high-grade glioma risk: NF1,
  Li-Fraumeni, Turcot/Lynch/CMMRD
  (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z).

## 3.3 What it does to the brain

Everything in §0.6, but with the emphasis inverted relative to low-grade disease:

- **Oedema is infiltrative here.** ~~Raised ICP "is more commonly seen in
  high-grade gliomas complicated by blood–brain barrier disruption and vasogenic
  oedema".~~ **CORRECTED 2026-09-07 (WI-515): that sentence is NOT in the cited
  Springer paper.** The paper was fetched in full; its only "more commonly seen"
  sentence is about hematologic malignancies. Do not state a comparative claim
  about how much oedema or raised pressure high-grade gliomas cause — nothing in
  the fetched set supports one. What the paper DOES say, verbatim: "peritumoral
  edema in high-grade gliomas is referred to as **infiltrative edema** because it
  represents vasogenic edema **in a zone of infiltrating tumor cells**"
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6). That is a
  statement about the NATURE of the oedema, not its quantity, and it is what
  `/tumors/high-grade-glioma` publishes.
- **Contrast enhancement on MRI** reflects that barrier breakdown
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).
- **Rapid infiltration along white matter tracts**, including across the corpus
  callosum (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/14/10/2507).
- **Deficits appear faster** and are more likely to be the presenting problem
  than in grade 2 disease.
- **Midline tumours** cause cranial nerve problems, ataxia, and hydrocephalus;
  reported symptoms in adult H3 K27-altered DMG include headache, dizziness,
  nausea, vomiting, blurred vision, raised intracranial pressure, limb motor and
  sensory disorders, and ataxia
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11640674/). Leptomeningeal
  dissemination and local recurrence occur in a minority of adult cases
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11640674/).

## 3.4 How it is named and graded

- **Grade 3 IDH-mutant astrocytoma:** "increased cellularity, brisk mitotic
  activity, may show microvascular proliferation"
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **Grade 4 IDH-mutant astrocytoma:** necrosis and/or microvascular proliferation
  — **or** homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion, which is by itself "diagnostic of a CNS
  WHO grade 4 tumor" (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **Grade 3 oligodendroglioma:** "microvascular proliferation, high cellularity,
  marked atypia, necrosis, brisk mitoses"
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, grade 4:** IDH-wildtype and H3-wildtype with one
  or more of microvascular proliferation, necrosis, TERT promoter mutation, EGFR
  amplification, or +7/−10
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **H3-defined entities:** diagnosis is made by the histone alteration regardless
  of how bland or aggressive the histology looks
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- Retired: "anaplastic astrocytoma," "anaplastic oligodendroglioma," and
  "anaplasia" as a grading word
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
  https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

## 3.5 Typical course

- High-grade gliomas recur; for glioblastoma, "despite appropriate management,
  90% of the patients will develop relapse or progression"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11540275/). (This is a recurrence
  figure, not a survival figure — but consider whether even this belongs on a
  patient page, or whether "recurrence is expected and is planned for" carries the
  same information with less harm.)
- Recurrence is predominantly **local**: in one series initial recurrences were
  central in 66.7%, in-field in 19.0%, out-field 9.5%, distant 19.0%
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3643853/).
- **Pseudoprogression** must be explained on this page. Roughly 10–30% of patients
  who receive radiation with concurrent temozolomide "will have transient
  worsening of their MRI scans due to treatment effects that improve with time"
  (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). Mechanism: transiently
  increased permeability of tumour vasculature from irradiation, enhanced by
  temozolomide (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). Timing:
  RANO defines it as occurring within 12 weeks of completing radiotherapy
  (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). It can produce genuine
  new symptoms from mass effect and oedema
  (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). RANO rules: within the
  first 12 weeks, progression can only be called if most new enhancement lies
  outside the radiation field or there is pathological confirmation
  (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). **This is one of the
  most distressing and least-explained experiences in the whole disease** and is a
  strong candidate for a dedicated BrainHarbor explainer.
- **Radiation necrosis** is a later treatment effect that can also mimic tumour
  growth; bevacizumab is used for it
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

## 3.6 Treatments

- **Surgery** — maximal safe resection, with 5-ALA fluorescence guidance giving
  significantly better extent of resection in high-grade glioma
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Chemoradiation (the Stupp regimen)** — "the Stupp protocol is the standard of
  care for high-grade gliomas," radiotherapy 60 Gy over six weeks with
  temozolomide (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/). Detail from NCI:
  60 Gy in 30 fractions; concurrent temozolomide 75 mg/m² daily during radiation;
  then adjuvant temozolomide 150–200 mg/m² on days 1–5 of 28-day cycles for six
  cycles (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Grade 3 IDH-mutant astrocytoma specifically:** the CATNON trial "established
  the role for adjuvant temozolomide in patients with IDH-mutant astrocytoma,
  grade 3" — radiotherapy followed by 12 cycles of adjuvant temozolomide, without
  concurrent temozolomide during radiation
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/28/5/1083/8489914). Benefit was
  limited to IDH-mutant tumours
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/28/5/1083/8489914). CATNON
  radiotherapy dose was 59.4 Gy in 33 fractions
  (https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(25)00614-X/abstract).
  **Note this is a genuinely different regimen from glioblastoma's** — same grade
  range, different drug schedule, because the biology differs.
- **Grade 3 oligodendroglioma:** see Part 5 — PCV, not the glioblastoma regimen
  (https://ascopost.com/issues/july-1-2012/new-standard-of-care-for-anaplastic-oligodendroglial-tumors-with-1p19q-codeletions/).
- **TTFields** for supratentorial glioblastoma (see §0.8)
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/).
- **Diffuse midline glioma H3 K27-altered:** dordaviprone (ONC201) is FDA
  approved for recurrent H3 K27M-mutant disease
  (https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.23.01134); flag the ongoing debate over
  the strength of the approval evidence
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/advance-article/doi/10.1093/neuonc/noag117/8690955).
- **Recurrent disease:** re-operation, angiogenesis inhibitors, tumour treating
  fields (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/); re-irradiation is used
  in selected patients (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11871646/).
- **Clinical trials** — a standard part of the conversation at diagnosis and at
  recurrence (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

## 3.7 Questions patients most need answered

1. What does grade 4 actually mean? Is grade 4 always glioblastoma? (No —
   astrocytoma IDH-mutant grade 4 and the H3-defined entities are also grade 4 —
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/)
2. My old report said "anaplastic astrocytoma." What is it called now? (§0.3)
3. My scan looks worse after radiation. Is the treatment failing?
   (pseudoprogression — https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150)
4. Why does my friend with the same grade get different chemo? (CATNON vs Stupp
   vs PCV — https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/28/5/1083/8489914)
5. What is MGMT and why does it decide my chemo?
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/)

# PART 4 — ASTROCYTOMA

## 4.1 What it is

An astrocytoma is a glioma that arises from — or resembles — **astrocytes**, the
star-shaped glial cells that support nerve cells, help maintain the blood–brain
barrier, and regulate the chemical environment around neurons
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). About 2 in
10 brain tumours are astrocytomas
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/types-of-brain-tumors.html).

**"Astrocytoma" is a family too.** Under CNS5 it spans at least three distinct
groups, and a reader needs to know which one they have:

1. **Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant** (adult-type diffuse glioma), CNS WHO grade 2, 3
   or 4 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). This is the entity
   most people mean when a neuro-oncologist says "astrocytoma" to an adult today.
   NBTS describes it as "an invasive tumor derived from astrocytes (star-shaped
   glial cells) with poorly defined boundaries," which can occur anywhere in the
   CNS but typically develops "near or within the frontal lobes of the brain"
   (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/).
   Primarily supratentorial; infratentorial occurrence is rare
   (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
2. **Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas** — pilocytic astrocytoma, pleomorphic
   xanthoastrocytoma, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, chordoid glioma,
   astroblastoma, high-grade astrocytoma with piloid features
   (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/). These have well-defined borders
   and a generally more favourable natural history, with gross total resection as
   treatment of choice (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).
3. **Pediatric-type diffuse astrocytomas** — e.g. diffuse astrocytoma,
   MYB- or MYBL1-altered, CNS WHO grade 1, associated with long-term epilepsy and
   with good prognosis after gross total resection
   (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

**Relationship to the umbrella terms — the sentence to write:** an astrocytoma is
one kind of glioma; whether it is called "low-grade" or "high-grade" depends on
its CNS WHO grade; and glioblastoma is, biologically, an astrocytic tumour —
"glioblastoma is the most common type of astrocytoma"
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/types-of-brain-tumors.html)
— but under CNS5 it is a separate named diagnosis and is never IDH-mutant
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/).

Pilocytic astrocytoma specifics: rare in adults, defined by MAPK-pathway
alterations including targetable BRAF mutations, and "less heterogenous and
non-diffuse, allowing for local treatments"
(https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/how-brain-tumours-are-diagnosed/brain-tumour-biology/different-types-brain-tumours/);
typically features KIAA1549-BRAF fusion or BRAF V600E
(https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).

## 4.2 How it starts

- §0.5 baseline: unknown in most cases; ionising radiation and inherited
  syndromes are the accepted risk factors
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6240585/).
- **Adult IDH-mutant astrocytoma:** IDH mutation is the initiating event,
  followed by TP53 and ATRX alterations, then TERT
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/). TP53 and ATRX mutations are
  frequently present; ATRX loss on immunostaining reflects the underlying mutation
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **Pilocytic and pediatric astrocytomas:** MAPK-pathway activation, most often
  BRAF fusion or V600E (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html).
- **Hereditary:** NF1 (pilocytic and optic pathway gliomas), Li-Fraumeni
  (high-grade), tuberous sclerosis (subependymal giant cell astrocytoma)
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
  https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z).
- Cover the self-blame list (cell phones, head injury, stress, diet) here too —
  do not make readers navigate to the umbrella page for reassurance.

## 4.3 What it does to the brain

- §0.6 mechanisms. Location bias matters: frontal lobe predominance means
  **behaviour and personality change is a leading and under-recognised
  presentation** — "changes in behavior and personality can be present months to
  years before diagnosis"
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/).
  This has real consequences: families often reinterpret years of "he became
  difficult" after diagnosis, and that reinterpretation deserves compassionate
  handling.
- Seizures are common at presentation; other symptoms include "speech or language
  difficulties, vision changes, and sensory changes"
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/).
- IDH-mutant status independently raises seizure risk through the D-2-HG
  mechanism (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134).
- Diffuse astrocytomas infiltrate; circumscribed astrocytic gliomas push rather
  than infiltrate, which is why the latter can be cured surgically
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).

## 4.4 How it is named and graded

**Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant** is graded 2, 3 or 4 *within the type*
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). Criteria
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html):

| CNS WHO grade | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 2 | Low-to-moderate cellularity, minimal or absent mitotic activity, no necrosis, no microvascular proliferation |
| 3 | Increased cellularity, brisk mitotic activity, may show microvascular proliferation |
| 4 | Necrosis and/or microvascular proliferation — **or** homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B, which is diagnostic of grade 4 on its own |

Defining and supporting markers: IDH1/IDH2 mutation (defining); ATRX loss and
TP53 mutation (supporting, and separate it from oligodendroglioma); 1p/19q **not**
co-deleted (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

Retired names to address on this page, prominently:
- "Diffuse astrocytoma" and "anaplastic astrocytoma" are no longer diagnoses
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/astrocytoma-idh-mutant/,
  https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- "Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant" / "secondary glioblastoma" is now **astrocytoma,
  IDH-mutant, CNS WHO grade 4**
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). This is the rename that most
  commonly lands on this page's readers, and it needs the reassurance attached:
  the change was made because these tumours behave less aggressively than
  IDH-wildtype glioblastoma
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- Conversely: an astrocytic tumour that is IDH-**wildtype** may be reclassified as
  glioblastoma even without grade-4 histology
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). Some people whose report
  once said "anaplastic astrocytoma" now have a glioblastoma diagnosis. Say so
  plainly; it is worse to let them discover it by accident.

Circumscribed astrocytic gliomas are graded by their own criteria; e.g. high-grade
astrocytoma with piloid features carries MAPK alteration + ATRX mutation +
homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).

## 4.5 Typical course

- Grade 2: observation may be appropriate for selected low-risk patients
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/); transformation to
  higher grade over time is a known behaviour
  (https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2022.40.16_suppl.2065); transformation is
  associated with worse outcomes in astrocytomas specifically, unlike in
  oligodendrogliomas (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475770/).
- Grade 3 and 4: treated up front; recurrence expected.
- Pilocytic astrocytoma and other circumscribed types: gross total resection is
  the treatment of choice and can be curative
  (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35562132/).
- Long-term MRI surveillance across the board; CNS tumours "often recur, sometimes
  many years after treatment"
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

## 4.6 Treatments

- **Surgery:** maximal safe resection; awake mapping for eloquent-area tumours
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
  https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2023.1089139/full).
- **Grade 2, high risk:** radiation followed by six cycles of PCV
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7527157/).
- **Grade 2, residual/recurrent, IDH-mutant:** vorasidenib after surgery, FDA
  approved August 2024 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/).
- **Grade 3 IDH-mutant:** radiotherapy followed by 12 cycles of adjuvant
  temozolomide (CATNON), without concurrent temozolomide during radiation
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/28/5/1083/8489914).
- **Grade 4 IDH-mutant:** managed like high-grade glioma with chemoradiation;
  note that it is treated as its own entity and not identical to glioblastoma
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).
- **Pilocytic / BRAF-altered:** surgery first; targeted MAPK-pathway inhibitors
  (dabrafenib + trametinib for BRAF V600E; tovorafenib for BRAF
  fusion/rearrangement or V600 in relapsed/refractory pediatric LGG; selumetinib)
  (https://tp.amegroups.org/article/view/122626/html). Radiation is generally
  avoided in children (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Why this differs from oligodendroglioma:** PCV's strongest evidence sits with
  1p/19q-codeleted tumours
  (https://ascopubs.org/doi/pdfdirect/10.14694/EdBook_AM.2013.33.114); the
  temozolomide-based CATNON pathway is the astrocytoma path for grade 3
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/28/5/1083/8489914).

# PART 5 — OLIGODENDROGLIOMA

## 5.1 What it is

An oligodendroglioma is a diffuse infiltrating glioma that grows from — or
resembles — **oligodendrocytes**, the cells that make myelin, the insulation
around nerve fibres (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/,
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). NCI
describes oligodendrocytes as cells "which help keep nerve cells healthy"
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**Full CNS5 name:** *Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted*, CNS WHO
grade 2 or 3 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). **There is no
grade 1 and no grade 4 oligodendroglioma**
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

**Location.** Cerebral hemispheric white matter, 80–90% supratentorial, frontal
lobes most common (50–65%), then temporal (47%) and parietal (7–20%); a
cortical–subcortical distribution with diffuse white matter infiltration; rarely
intraventricular or subependymal
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

**Frequency.** The third most common primary brain neoplasm after glioblastoma
and diffuse astrocytoma; about 5% of primary intracranial tumours; incidence 0.2
per 100,000; slight male predominance; peak in the fourth–fifth decades with a
secondary paediatric peak at ages 6–12
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Cancer Research UK calls it "a
rare type of glioma"
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/types/glioma-adults).

**Relationship to umbrella terms:** grade 2 oligodendroglioma is a low-grade
glioma; grade 3 oligodendroglioma is a high-grade glioma; both are adult-type
diffuse gliomas; neither is an astrocytoma
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/).

## 5.2 How it starts

- §0.5 baseline applies unchanged.
- Molecular sequence: IDH mutation first, then 1p/19q co-deletion, then TERT
  promoter mutation (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4791257/).
- "Nearly all OGs harbor mutations in the IDH1 or IDH2 genes"
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- 1p/19q co-deletion occurs through an unbalanced translocation event
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- CIC (on 19q) and FUBP1 (on 1p) alterations accompany the co-deletion
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/,
  https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/how-brain-tumours-are-diagnosed/brain-tumour-biology/different-types-brain-tumours/).
- IDH mutation produces 2-hydroxyglutarate, inducing the G-CIMP hypermethylation
  phenotype and impairing cellular differentiation
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- No inherited syndrome is characteristically associated with oligodendroglioma
  in the way NF1 is with pilocytic astrocytoma — say this, because it is
  reassuring and true.

## 5.3 What it does to the brain

The clinical signature here is distinctive and should be foregrounded:

- **Seizures are the hallmark.** "Seizures are reported in 35% to 91% of cases,
  making them a hallmark symptom" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/);
  patients often have a long history of seizures before diagnosis
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Mechanism: cortical and
  subcortical location puts the tumour in and next to seizure-generating cortex
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/), compounded by the IDH-mutant
  D-2-HG mechanism
  (https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/24/9/1423/6498134,
  https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000003911).
- **Little oedema.** "Peritumoral edema and hemorrhage is uncommon"
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/) — so raised-ICP symptoms are
  less prominent early than in glioblastoma.
- **Calcification.** Up to 90% show calcifications on CT, often in a gyriform
  pattern that is "highly suggestive of this tumor type"
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Worth explaining because
  patients see "calcification" on reports and assume it is something they did.
- **Frontal-lobe consequences:** cognitive and personality change
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- Other symptoms: persistent or progressive headache, focal deficits (weakness,
  sensory disturbance, aphasia), visual field deficits, and raised-ICP symptoms
  including nausea, vomiting and papilloedema
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Grade 3 tumours present with
  more rapid symptom onset and more severe deficits
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

## 5.4 How it is named and graded

**The definition is molecular and both parts are mandatory:** IDH1/IDH2 mutation
**AND** whole-arm 1p/19q co-deletion
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9208578/). NCI states that under the
2021 classification "oligodendrogliomas are defined by the presence of isocitrate
dehydrogenase IDH1 or IDH2 mutations and 1p/19q co-deletion"
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**This is a genuine override of the microscope.** A tumour that looks textbook
oligodendroglial under the microscope — "fried egg" cells with perinuclear halos
and a "chicken wire" branching capillary network
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/) — is **not** an oligodendroglioma
if 1p/19q is intact. Note the literature caveat that "not all 1p/19q
non-codeleted oligodendroglial tumors are astrocytic"
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5323103/), which is why the integrated
diagnosis, not appearance, decides.

Grading (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html):

| CNS WHO grade | Criteria |
|---|---|
| 2 | Branching capillary network, low mitotic activity, low Ki-67 index |
| 3 | Microvascular proliferation, high cellularity, marked atypia, necrosis, brisk mitoses |

Supporting markers:
- TERT promoter mutations are present in the majority but are **not diagnostic on
  their own** (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- **ATRX is typically retained** — unlike astrocytoma
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). This is the practical
  discriminator on a report.
- CDKN2A/B deletion "may indicate aggressive grade 3 behavior"
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html) — but note it does **not**
  create a grade 4 oligodendroglioma, because grade 4 does not exist for this
  entity (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).
- DNA methylation profiling reliably classifies these tumours
  (https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

Retired names: "anaplastic oligodendroglioma" → oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and
1p/19q-codeleted, CNS WHO grade 3
(https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). "Oligoastrocytoma" and
"mixed glioma" are gone; molecular testing assigns such cases to one side or the
other (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). This matters
practically — a US National Cancer Database analysis found substantial disparities
in whether 1p/19q testing was even performed
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8630738/), so some readers will
have older reports with no co-deletion result at all and may need to ask whether
their stored tissue can be retested.

## 5.5 Typical course

- Oligodendroglioma is "known for [its] relative chemosensitivity and indolent
  clinical course among diffuse gliomas of adult type"
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10014-024-00480-1).
- Recurrence and malignant transformation of residual or recurrent tumour occur
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/); but transformation is **not**
  associated with the same worsening of outcome seen in astrocytomas
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10475770/) — a genuinely different
  and useful point for this page.
- Timing of radiotherapy: "no difference in the overall survival between early or
  delayed postoperative RT," but early treatment gives longer time to progression
  and better seizure control (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
  This is a real, patient-facing trade-off worth explaining rather than hiding.
- Long-term surveillance and long-term seizure management are the shape of life
  with this diagnosis.

## 5.6 Treatments — and why PCV is the oligodendroglioma drug

**Surgery.** Complete or gross total resection is "the primary treatment of
choice"; debulking when complete resection is impossible improves neurological
symptoms and seizure control (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
Awake craniotomy with stimulation mapping, intraoperative MRI/ultrasound, and
5-ALA are used (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

**Radiation.** Focal/limited-field RT after surgery; fields include the resection
bed, residual enhancement and roughly 3 cm margins incorporating T2/FLAIR
hyperintensity; 45–65 Gy in 1.8–2 Gy fractions, with no significant survival
benefit demonstrated between the lower and higher ends of that range
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

**Why PCV, and why here specifically.** This is the most-asked and worst-answered
question about oligodendroglioma:

- The EORTC 26951 and RTOG 9402 trials in anaplastic oligodendroglial tumours
  "have shown that 1p/19q loss in these tumors is predictive of overall survival
  benefit of the addition of PCV chemotherapy to radiotherapy"
  (https://ascopubs.org/doi/pdfdirect/10.14694/EdBook_AM.2013.33.114).
- 1p/19q co-deletion is therefore both diagnostic and predictive — "a diagnostic
  marker and allows prognostication and prediction of the best drug response
  within IDH-mutant tumours"
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9208578/).
- Consequence: "the standard of post-surgical care for those tumors is considered
  to be initial chemoradiotherapy regardless of histopathological grade,
  particularly with PCV"
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10014-024-00480-1).
- **The plain-language version:** the same chromosome loss that makes it an
  oligodendroglioma also makes it unusually responsive to this chemotherapy. The
  label and the treatment are two consequences of one biological fact. That is
  why the 1p/19q test is worth waiting for.
- PCV is limited to about six cycles because of adverse events including
  leukopenia and thrombocytopenia
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). NCI's PDQ describes six cycles
  of PCV as adjuvant therapy for high-risk disease
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
- **Temozolomide vs PCV, honestly:** temozolomide is better tolerated and oral,
  but "recent evidence suggests PCV could lead to better overall survival compared
  to TMZ in 1p/19q co-deleted patients," and the CODEL study (NCT00887146) was
  designed to compare RT+PCV with RT+TMZ
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Temozolomide can also drive
  TMZ-resistant hypermutated tumours at recurrence
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Present this as a real,
  ongoing, discussable choice — not as a settled answer.
- **Vorasidenib** applies to grade 2 oligodendroglioma with residual or recurrent
  disease after surgery (FDA approval covers grade 2 astrocytoma **or**
  oligodendroglioma with a susceptible IDH1/IDH2 mutation)
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12416742/).
- **Bevacizumab** is limited to recurrent disease, palliative use, and radiation
  necrosis (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- **Seizure control is a treatment endpoint in its own right** for this tumour
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

# PART 6 — GLIOBLASTOMA

## 6.1 What it is

**Full CNS5 name:** *Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype*, CNS WHO grade 4
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/). NBTS: "an invasive tumor
with poorly defined boundaries that originates from astrocytes," classified as
high-grade because of its aggressive behaviour and brain infiltration
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).
Grade 4 is the highest grade, indicating cells appear most abnormal under the
microscope
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).

Formerly called glioblastoma multiforme (GBM)
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/types/glioma-adults);
"GBM" remains the everyday abbreviation.

**Frequency and demographics.** The most common primary malignant brain tumour;
13.9% of all brain and CNS tumours; incidence 3.27–3.28 per 100,000; median age at
diagnosis 66
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/,
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/Supplement_4/iv1/8285946).
51.5% of all malignant brain/CNS tumours
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290).
ABTA: "on average, more than 12,000 glioblastoma cases are diagnosed each year in
the United States"
(https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/brain-spinal-cord-tumors-adults/types-of-brain-tumors.html
— note this figure is quoted from ABTA via search; verify directly against
https://www.abta.org/tumor_types/glioblastoma-gbm/ before publishing).

**Location.** "Primarily in cerebral hemispheres; also occurs in brainstem,
cerebellum, and spinal cord"
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).

**Relationship to the other pages:** glioblastoma is a glioma; it is
astrocytic in origin; it is a high-grade glioma; it is an adult-type diffuse
glioma; and under CNS5 it is **always IDH-wildtype**
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/).

## 6.2 How it starts

- §0.5 baseline: cause unknown in the great majority
  (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). This
  section carries the heaviest emotional load on the whole site — people with a
  glioblastoma diagnosis search "what did I do" more than any other cohort. Give
  cell phones, head injury, stress and diet their own named answers here
  (https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet,
  https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors,
  https://tischbraintumorcenter.duke.edu/blog/can-stress-cause-brain-tumors).
- Hereditary syndromes associated with glioblastoma: NF1 (autosomal dominant),
  Li-Fraumeni (germline TP53, autosomal dominant), Turcot type 1 (MLH1, MSH2,
  MSH6, PMS2), Lynch (dominant) and constitutional mismatch repair deficiency
  (recessive) (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41525-025-00526-z). These are a
  small minority; overall about 5% of primary brain tumours have known hereditary
  factors
  (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/nursing-and-health-professions/turcot-syndrome).
- **The mutations that define glioblastoma are in the tumour, not in the person.**
  TERT, EGFR and +7/−10 are somatic changes
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). Make this explicit — it is
  the answer to "will my kids get this."

## 6.3 What it does to the brain

The most complete mechanism story of the six pages.

- **Infiltration along white matter tracts.** Glioblastomas "often spread along
  white matter tracts (eg, the corpus callosum, internal capsule, optic
  radiation, anterior commissure, fornix, and subependymal regions)" and "tend to
  grow along blood vessels or white matter fibers, which serve as a route for
  cancer cell migration" (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/14/10/2507).
- **"Butterfly glioma."** When a tumour crosses the corpus callosum into the
  other hemisphere it creates a bilateral symmetric appearance — hence "butterfly
  glioma"; the genu is involved in roughly 60%, body 30%, splenium 10%
  (https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/14/10/2507,
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-23794-6). Patients see this word on
  reports and it needs a plain explanation.
- **Microvascular proliferation and pseudopalisading necrosis — the engine.**
  Glioblastoma is "distinguished pathologically from lower grade tumors by
  necrosis and microvascular hyperplasia," with necrotic foci surrounded by
  "pseudopalisading" cells
  (https://academic.oup.com/jnen/article/65/6/529/2645248). Those pseudopalisades
  "are severely hypoxic, overexpress hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1), and secrete
  proangiogenic factors such as VEGF and IL-8," so "the microvascular hyperplasia
  in GBM that provides a new vasculature and promotes peripheral tumor expansion
  is tightly linked with the emergence of pseudopalisades"
  (https://academic.oup.com/jnen/article/65/6/529/2645248). **Plain-language
  version to build from:** the tumour outgrows its blood supply, cells suffocate
  and die, the starving cells around the dead zone send out chemical distress
  signals that force new leaky blood vessels to grow, and the tumour pushes
  outward toward them. That single explanation accounts for the necrosis on the
  scan, the ring of enhancement, the swelling, and why it keeps expanding.
- **Blood–brain barrier breakdown → contrast enhancement + oedema.** MRI shows
  "poorly circumscribed, marginal enhancement due to disruption of the
  blood-brain barrier," with central T1 hypointensity from necrosis and
  peripheral T2/FLAIR hyperintensity from oedema
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558954/). Those new vessels are leaky —
  hence vasogenic oedema
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2588896/) — which is a major cause of
  clinical impairment
  (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6) and is what
  steroids act on (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/).
- **Symptoms.** NBTS lists "one-sided weakness, difficulty with speech or
  language, and vision changes"; seizures affecting up to 50% of patients;
  headache, nausea and vomiting from increased intracranial pressure; and
  "behavioral and cognitive changes are also common, especially in older adults"
  (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).
- **Hydrocephalus** can occur and may require shunting
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6257011/).

## 6.4 How it is named and graded

**Definition (verbatim-level precision):** glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype, is a
diffuse astrocytic glioma that is IDH-wildtype and H3-wildtype and has one or more
of: microvascular proliferation, necrosis, TERT promoter mutation, EGFR
amplification, or +7/−10 chromosome copy-number changes — CNS WHO grade 4
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

Two consequences to spell out for readers:

1. **"Molecular glioblastoma."** A tumour with none of the classic grade-4
   appearances under the microscope is still glioblastoma if it is IDH-wildtype
   and carries TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or +7/−10
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10216527/,
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). "All tumors lacking IDH
   mutations that have concomitant gain of chromosome 7 and loss of chromosome
   10, EGFR amplification, or TERT promoter mutations are called glioblastoma and
   are given a WHO grade of 4"
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). People will be told
   "grade 4" after being told their tumour "looked lower grade." Explain it or
   they will assume someone made a mistake.
2. **Glioblastoma is never IDH-mutant now.** "Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant" and
   "secondary glioblastoma" are retired; those tumours are now astrocytoma,
   IDH-mutant, grade 4
   (https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/,
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/). Someone whose 2015 report
   said "secondary glioblastoma" has a different label today — and the label
   change was made because those tumours behave less aggressively
   (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

**MGMT is not part of the diagnosis** — it is a separate test that guides
treatment (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/,
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). Readers
routinely confuse the two.

**Also flag:** pediatric-type high-grade gliomas that once would have been called
glioblastoma are now separate entities — diffuse midline glioma H3 K27-altered,
diffuse hemispheric glioma H3 G34-mutant, diffuse pediatric-type high-grade
glioma H3/IDH-wildtype, infant-type hemispheric glioma
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html). Note that infant-type
hemispheric glioma shows better outcomes than its grade-4 status and
glioblastoma-like morphology would suggest, and may respond to targeted therapy
against NTRK/ROS1/ALK/MET fusions
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/).

## 6.5 Typical course

- Treatment-then-surveillance, with recurrence expected
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11540275/).
- Recurrence is mostly local, at or near the original site
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3643853/).
- **Pseudoprogression** — see §3.5; 10–30% of patients treated with radiation plus
  concurrent temozolomide show transient MRI worsening that improves with time,
  typically within 12 weeks of finishing radiotherapy, and it can cause real
  symptoms (https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/4/2/120/2418150). This belongs on
  the glioblastoma page in full, not by link.
- **Radiation necrosis** as a later mimic; bevacizumab is one management option
  (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).
- Extracranial spread is very rare
  (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10073898/).

## 6.6 Treatments

**Standard of care, newly diagnosed.** NCI: "The standard treatment for patients
with newly diagnosed glioblastoma is surgery followed by concurrent radiation
therapy and daily temozolomide, and then followed by six cycles of temozolomide"
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). Regimen detail:
60 Gy in 30 fractions; concurrent TMZ 75 mg/m² daily during radiation; adjuvant
TMZ 150–200 mg/m² days 1–5 of 28-day cycles × 6
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). This is the
"Stupp protocol" (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/).

**Surgery.** Maximal safe resection; 5-ALA fluorescence guidance significantly
improves extent of resection in high-grade glioma
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Surgery also establishes the
diagnosis and can safely reduce intracranial pressure
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). **Explain
honestly why complete removal is not possible:** cells extend beyond the
MRI-visible tumour
(https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.874826/full,
https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6694/14/10/2507).

**MGMT-guided decisions.** Benefit from temozolomide "was largely limited to
patients with glioblastoma possessing a methylated MGMT promoter," and
unmethylated status "might be a predictor for lack of efficacy for TMZ"
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/).

**Tumour Treating Fields.** Started 4–7 weeks after radiotherapy during
maintenance temozolomide, in selected patients with supratentorial glioblastoma
and KPS ≥60; main toxicity is skin irritation; endorsed by NCCN and ASTRO 2025
guidelines (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/).

**Older or frail patients.** Hypofractionated radiotherapy (shorter courses)
"appears to be a safe, well-tolerated alternative for elderly or frail patients
with GBM" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/); a phase III trial
(NCT05439278) is planned to compare 60 Gy over six weeks with 40 Gy over three
weeks in patients ≥70 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12467656/).

**Lomustine + temozolomide** in MGMT-methylated glioblastoma was tested in
CeTeG/NOA-09 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7005933/) — mention as
an option some centres use, not as universal standard.

**Carmustine wafers** placed in the resection cavity give a modest overall
survival benefit (HR 0.73)
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).

**Bevacizumab** did not improve overall survival in newly diagnosed glioblastoma
in RTOG 0825 or AVAglio, though progression-free survival improved modestly
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). Useful for
oedema and steroid-sparing and in recurrence
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). **This is a good honest example
of "helps the scan, not the outcome" for the anti-hype framing.**

**Dose-dense temozolomide** (RTOG 0525) showed "no statistically significant
difference between standard and dose-dense temozolomide ... for median OS"
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq) — i.e. more is
not better.

**Recurrence:** re-operation, angiogenesis inhibitors, TTFields
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/); fractionated stereotactic
re-irradiation in selected patients
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11871646/).

**Supportive care:** dexamethasone for oedema, with rapid symptomatic relief in
24–48 hours (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12406498/); anti-seizure
medication, usually levetiracetam
(https://emedicine.medscape.com/article/283252-medication); DVT prophylaxis
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK441874/) — worth naming, since
thromboembolic events are a recognised complication in glioma
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/).

**Clinical trials** — NBTS explicitly directs patients to ask their provider
about trials and biomarker testing
(https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/).

# PART 7 — SOURCE CONFLICTS, OUTDATED MATERIAL, AND EDITORIAL CAUTIONS

## 7.1 NCI's patient-facing PDQ page is pre-CNS5

This is the biggest single finding for editorial policy. The NCI **patient**
version of "Adult Central Nervous System Tumors Treatment (PDQ)" still uses:
Roman numeral grades (I–IV); "anaplastic astrocytoma" as a live diagnosis; "mixed
gliomas ... called oligoastrocytomas"; and grade-based treatment sections with no
IDH/1p/19q framing
(https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq). All four
of those are retired or superseded under WHO CNS5
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9723092/,
https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html).

**Editorial rule:** use NCI patient PDQ for supportive-care and general framing
language, but **never** for naming or grading. For naming/grading, use CNS5-derived
literature and the CNS5-updated patient organisations (NBTS, Cancer Research UK).

The NCI **health-professional** version is more current on molecular markers
(IDH, 1p/19q, MGMT, BRAF) but still describes the WHO grading scale in Roman
numerals I–IV and does not cover ATRX, TERT, CDKN2A/B, EGFR, +7/−10 or H3 K27 in
the section retrieved (https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/hp/adult-brain-treatment-pdq).
Treat it as authoritative for treatment regimens and trial evidence, not for
classification.

## 7.2 StatPearls oligodendroglioma chapter is internally inconsistent

The chapter states the WHO 2021 grading as "Grade II: Low-grade, IDH-mutant with
1p/19q codeletion / Grade III: Anaplastic OG," using both Roman numerals and the
retired word "anaplastic"
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/), while elsewhere correctly
describing the molecular definition. Its clinical, imaging, and treatment content
is high quality and heavily used above; its **naming and grading content should
not be reproduced**. Use https://cco.amegroups.org/article/view/110414/html for
oligodendroglioma grading instead.

## 7.3 "Low-grade glioma" — WHO advises against the term, everyone uses it anyway

WHO "recommends avoiding the term 'low-grade glioma'"
(https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/grade-ii-gliomasnot-so-low-grade), yet it is
the term patients are told, the term trials use (RTOG 9802), and the term people
search for. BrainHarbor should keep the page (search intent is real) but lead
with the caveat and route readers to their actual diagnosis. Also note the
competing term "lower-grade glioma" for grade 2–3
(https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa1402121).

## 7.4 Head injury — genuinely unsettled

Macmillan states head injury "has not been shown to cause brain tumours"
(https://www.macmillan.org.uk/cancer-information-and-support/brain-tumour/brain-tumour-causes-and-risk-factors),
while a 2023 UCL study proposed a mechanism and reported an association
(https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2023/feb/head-injuries-could-be-risk-factor-developing-brain-cancer),
and a hospital-based case-control study in Afghanistan reported an association
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12136956/). Cancer Research UK's risks
and causes page does not list head injury at all
(https://www.cancerresearchuk.org/about-cancer/brain-tumours/risks-causes).
**Recommended framing:** head injury is not an established cause; a possible link
is being researched; a bang on the head often *leads to the scan that finds* a
tumour that was already there. Do not present the new work as settled, and do not
suppress it.

## 7.5 Cell phones — the IARC classification vs. the evidence

NCI's conclusion ("does not cause brain or other kinds of cancer in humans") sits
alongside IARC's 2011 Group 2B "possibly carcinogenic" classification and one
Interphone analysis showing a small statistically significant glioma increase in
heavy users that the investigators judged inconclusive — all on the same NCI page
(https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/radiation/cell-phones-fact-sheet).
A meta-analysis found ever-use not significantly associated with glioma but noted
possible risk in long-term users
(https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1878875018308428).
**Recommended framing:** state the NCI conclusion plainly, mention that IARC's
2011 classification is often quoted, explain what Group 2B actually means (limited
evidence, not established harm), and cite the stable incidence trend
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3297541/). Do not pretend the
disagreement doesn't exist — patients have already found it.

## 7.6 RTOG 9802 "high risk" criteria are widely over-applied

Age ≥40 and/or subtotal resection are used clinically as if they define who needs
treatment, but they were trial *eligibility* criteria; "there is no clinical
justification for a strict age cutoff"
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6587541/). Do not present the age-40
line as a rule.

## 7.7 PCV vs temozolomide in oligodendroglioma is not settled

Recent evidence favours PCV over TMZ in 1p/19q co-deleted patients, and CODEL was
designed to answer the question directly
(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK559184/). Present as an open, discussable
choice.

## 7.8 Dordaviprone approval is contested

FDA approved for recurrent H3 K27M-mutant diffuse midline glioma
(https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.23.01134), but a published
Point/Counterpoint asks whether it was "a landmark approval or a premature step"
(https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/advance-article/doi/10.1093/neuonc/noag117/8690955).
If BrainHarbor mentions it, mention the debate.

## 7.9 Older organisation pages using retired names

ABTA's downloadable PDF is titled "Glioblastoma & Anaplastic Astrocytoma"
(https://www.abta.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/glioblastoma-anaplastic-astrocytoma.pdf)
— a 2018 asset using a retired term. Many hospital and charity pages are in the
same state. When BrainHarbor cites patient organisations, prefer their newer
CNS5-aligned type pages (e.g.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/glioblastoma-idh-wildtype/)
over PDFs and legacy pages.
