# Research pack: Meningioma · Brain metastases · "We don't have a name for it yet"

**Prepared for:** BrainHarbor (brainharbor.org) content build
**Date:** 2026-08-30
**Audience of the finished pages:** newly-diagnosed patients and caregivers, possibly cognitively impaired; published at 6th-grade reading level.

## How to read this document

This is **source material at clinical precision**, not draft copy. Every substantive claim carries a URL. The simplification pass happens downstream — do not lose the precision that is captured here.

**Constraints applied while gathering:**

- **No prognosis figures.** Survival statistics, median survival and five-year survival rates were deliberately *excluded* even where the sources supplied them. Where a source volunteered them, that is noted so a later editor doesn't accidentally re-import them.
- **Recurrence figures are a separate judgement call.** Recurrence risk is clinically load-bearing (it is the entire reason grade 2 meningioma gets radiation) but some of it is expressed as "at 5 years," which sits close to the no-prognosis rule. Numeric recurrence data is quarantined into clearly marked boxes labelled **[NUMBERS — EDITORIAL DECISION NEEDED]**. Publishable qualitative framing is given alongside each.
- **Licensing:** no AHFS or MedlinePlus drug monographs consulted; no NCI images used. Sources are NCI/cancer.gov, WHO CNS5 and its official update process (cIMPACT-NOW), EANO/ESMO/ASCO/SNO/ASTRO guidelines, CBTRUS, ABTA, National Brain Tumor Society, and peer-reviewed reviews.
- **WHO CNS5 (2021) naming and grading throughout**, with retired terminology flagged.

# PART 0 — Cross-cutting material used by all three pages

## 0.1 What a tumour actually does to the brain (mechanism of symptoms)

This is the single most reusable block of content. It should be written once and referenced from all three pages.

### The skull is a closed box (Monro–Kellie)

The cranial vault is an enclosed space. A growing tumour, plus the swelling around it, has nowhere to expand into, so pressure inside the head rises. Rising intracranial pressure is what produces headache, vomiting, and — at the extreme — neurological damage.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

### Mass effect

Tumour cells physically displace healthy brain tissue next to them. That displacement — not the tumour "eating" the brain — is what causes most focal symptoms.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

**Editorial note for the 6th-grade rewrite:** "mass effect" is a phrase patients see on their own radiology report and cannot decode. It means *the tumour is taking up room and pushing on things*. Worth an explicit glossary entry — this is the kind of term readers arrive holding.

### Vasogenic oedema (swelling)

Tumours secrete vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which increases blood–brain barrier permeability; tumour-associated vessels also have reduced or abnormal endothelial tight junctions, partly from VEGF and matrix metalloproteinases. Blood plasma leaks out of the vessels into the brain tissue around the tumour. This is *vasogenic* oedema.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44201-022-00013-6
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

**The clinically important consequence:** the swelling can be larger than the tumour, and a lot of a patient's symptoms come from the swelling rather than the lump. That is why steroids can produce dramatic improvement within 24 hours without the tumour changing size at all. (See §0.2.)

### Raised intracranial pressure (ICP)

Tumour growth plus oedema raises ICP, which is responsible for a spectrum from headache and vomiting through seizures to neurological damage.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

Classic red-flag pattern worth teaching readers: headaches that are worse in the morning, with nausea/vomiting. NCI lists "morning headache or headache that goes away after vomiting" among the signs of adult brain tumours.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

### Hydrocephalus

Interstitial (hydrocephalic) oedema occurs when the balance between cerebrospinal fluid production and clearance is disrupted, CSF volume rises, and fluid overflows into the periventricular extracellular space.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

Posterior fossa tumours in particular present with progressive headache, ataxia, nausea and vomiting because of mass effect, obstruction of CSF flow, hydrocephalus and raised ICP.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6486495/

Two mechanisms worth distinguishing for readers:
- **Obstructive** — the tumour blocks the drain (e.g. intraventricular meningioma, posterior fossa lesion).
- **Communicating** — the drainage surfaces themselves stop absorbing, which is the mechanism in leptomeningeal disease (see §B.4).

Intraventricular meningiomas specifically present with hydrocephalus, headaches, memory problems and walking difficulty.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

### Seizures

Focal mass effect on the cortex produces generalised or partial seizures.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

Key patient-facing point, well supported: **anti-seizure medication is not given "just in case."** In patients with newly diagnosed brain tumours who have *not* had a seizure, clinicians should not prescribe antiepileptic drugs to reduce seizure risk (Level A). Once a verified seizure has occurred, brain-tumour-related epilepsy is considered to have developed and an antiepileptic drug is started.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10894758/

The same "no primary prophylaxis" recommendation appears in the brain-metastasis guidelines.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JOP.19.00058

This answers a very common and otherwise baffling question: *"Why won't they give me seizure medicine when I have a brain tumour?"*

### Focal deficits

The deficit maps to the function of the compressed area, not to the tumour's size. A small tumour in the wrong place outranks a large tumour in a quiet place. This is the single most important conceptual idea for the meningioma page and is developed in §A.4.

## 0.2 Steroids (dexamethasone) — mechanism, effect, cost

**What they do:** dexamethasone reduces peritumoural vasogenic oedema, reduces raised intracranial pressure and the frequency of plateau waves, decreases CSF production, and decreases tumour cerebral blood flow. It appears to act partly by impeding vascular endothelial growth/permeability factor.
https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/498465_3

**How fast:** neurological improvement and reduction in cerebral oedema typically within 8–48 hours, usually 12–24 hours.
https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/498465_3

**Typical doses:** practice guidance suggests 4–8 mg/day for mild symptoms and 16 mg/day for moderate-to-severe symptoms attributed to mass effect; much higher doses are occasionally used for imminent herniation or to stabilise before urgent surgery.
https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/498465_3

**Why they get tapered:** side effects are significant — high blood sugar/diabetes, high blood pressure, impaired wound healing, insomnia — so treatment is tapered and stopped as soon as the oedema has resolved.
https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/498465_3

**Guideline position:** use steroids only in symptomatic patients.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

For leptomeningeal disease: "the lowest dose of steroids should be used for the shortest time possible."
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Extra nuance worth capturing for the brain-mets page:** surgery is sometimes favoured in patients who are candidates for immunotherapy precisely because it lets steroids be tapered off (steroids blunt immunotherapy).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Patient-facing framing that is honest and reassuring:** "The steroid is not treating the tumour. It is treating the swelling around the tumour. That is why you can feel much better within a day while the tumour itself has not changed."

## 0.3 WHO CNS5 (2021) terminology — what changed, and what old paperwork will say

Readers arrive holding reports written under the 2016 or even 2007 classification. Every page needs a "your paperwork may say something different" block.

| Old term readers may hold | Current (WHO CNS5, 2021) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Grade I / II / III (Roman numerals) | Grade 1 / 2 / 3 (Arabic numerals) | https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214 |
| "WHO grade" of the tumour entity | Grading is now done *within* a tumour type — the same tumour type can be different grades in different people | https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214 |
| Haemangiopericytoma / "solitary fibrous tumour-haemangiopericytoma" | **Solitary fibrous tumour** — "haemangiopericytoma" is retired | https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/radiol.221885 |
| Neurofibromatosis type 2 (NF2) | **NF2-related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN)** — renamed 2022 because neurofibromas do not occur in this condition | https://www.ctf.org/news/revised-diagnostic-criteria-for-nf2-swn-announcement/ · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35674741/ |
| "Glioblastoma, IDH-mutant" | Eliminated; those tumours are now *Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant*, graded 2–4 | https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214 |

**Why Arabic numerals:** partly to align with grading outside the CNS, and explicitly because Roman numerals invite typographical errors (II mistaken for III, III for IV) with serious clinical consequences.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/early/2022/06/16/ajnr.A7462

**Two suffixes readers may see on a report and panic about:**
- **NOS** — "not otherwise specified": the molecular information is *missing* (not done, not available, or the test failed).
- **NEC** — "not elsewhere classified": the testing *was* done successfully, but the results don't line up into a recognised WHO diagnosis.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214

This distinction matters emotionally: NOS does *not* mean "we don't know what it is and it's terrible", it usually means "the molecular test hasn't been run."

**Layered/integrated diagnosis:** CNS5 reports are structured in layers — integrated diagnosis at the top, then histopathological classification, then CNS WHO grade, then molecular findings. A patient's report will therefore look like several different answers stacked up. It isn't contradiction; it's a format.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/23/8/1231/6311214

# PART A — MENINGIOMA

## A.1 The core reassurance nobody explains: it starts *outside* the brain

Meningiomas arise from **meningothelial arachnoid cap cells** attached to the dura mater — that is, from the coverings of the brain, not from brain tissue.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

The meninges are three membranes — dura mater (outermost, tough, adherent to the inside of the skull), arachnoid mater (middle, thin), and pia mater (innermost, adherent to the brain surface). Their job is to protect the brain and spinal cord from mechanical trauma, support blood vessels, and form a continuous cavity through which cerebrospinal fluid flows.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK539882/
https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/articles/22266-meninges

Meningiomas are therefore **extra-axial** tumours — they arise outside the brain substance itself. They are the most common primary intracranial extra-axial tumour.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240

NCI states it plainly: "A meningeal tumor, also called a meningioma, forms in the meninges (thin layers of tissue that cover the brain and spinal cord)."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**Why this matters for the reader, precisely:** the tumour typically grows *inward* and compresses brain tissue, nerves and blood vessels, rather than infiltrating through them.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

That is a genuinely different physical situation from a diffuse glioma, and it is the reason a meningioma can often be lifted off the brain surface surgically. It is also the honest limit of the reassurance: **compression from the outside still causes real symptoms, and skull-base meningiomas can wrap around nerves and arteries.** Do not oversell "it's not in your brain" into "it's harmless."

**Imaging signature that supports the "outside the brain" story** (useful because patients read their own MRI report):
- Well-defined, lobulated extra-axial mass with broad-based dural attachment.
- **CSF cleft sign** — a rim of cerebrospinal fluid between the mass and the brain, characteristic of an extra-axial location. Literally a visible gap showing the tumour sits outside the brain.
- **Dural tail** — present in about 72% of cases, but not specific to meningioma.
- Avid, homogeneous enhancement after contrast; hypo- to iso-intense on T1, variable on T2.
- **Hyperostosis** (thickening of adjacent bone) on CT in roughly 25–49%.
- Calcification, best seen on non-contrast CT and susceptibility-weighted MRI.
https://insightsimaging.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s13244-019-0697-7
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

## A.2 How common, and who gets them

- Meningioma is the most common **predominantly non-malignant** brain/CNS histopathology in the US: **41.7% of all** brain and other CNS tumours and **56.8% of all non-malignant** tumours (CBTRUS, 2017–2021 data).
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/meningioma/
- In CBTRUS 2018–2022 data, meningioma is the most common tumour type in adults aged 40+, at 23.02 per 100,000; tumours of the meninges as a grouping have the highest incidence rate of any major histopathology grouping (10.99 per 100,000).
https://cbtrus.org/cbtrus-fact-sheet/
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/Supplement_4/iv1/8285946
- Reviews commonly quote ~37.6% of all primary intracranial tumours in adults — a slightly different denominator (intracranial only vs. all brain-and-other-CNS). Both figures are correct in their own frame; see §D.2.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240
- Peak incidence age 40–70; only ~2.5% of cases are paediatric. Median age at diagnosis reported as 67.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/meningioma/
- Women are affected roughly twice as often as men overall, and about 3.15 times as often during peak reproductive years.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
(Mayfield gives 2.9× more common in women.)
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

## A.3 Grade — what it means for a meningioma specifically

WHO CNS5 stratifies meningioma into three grades (1–3) based on histopathological criteria **and molecular profile**. Roman numerals are gone: what used to be written "Grade II (atypical)" is now "Grade 2."
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240

Meningioma is treated in CNS5 as a **single tumour type** with a large number of histological subtypes (sources cite "at least 16 neuropathologic subtypes"), rather than as many separate tumours. Grade is now assigned within the type.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240

### Grade 1
- The large majority of meningiomas (~85–95% in one review; ~80% in Mayfield's patient material).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm
- Slow-growing. NCI: "A grade I meningioma is the most common type of meningeal tumor and is a slow-growing tumor."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
- Grade 1 subtypes include meningothelial, fibrous, transitional, psammomatous, angiomatous, microcystic, secretory, lymphoplasmacyte-rich, and metaplastic.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

### Grade 2 ("atypical") — the grade that changes the plan
- Roughly 5–10% of meningiomas.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- Criteria, any one of which suffices:
  1. **Mitotic count** in the grade-2 range. Historically stated as 4–19 mitoses per 10 high-power fields; CNS5/cIMPACT-NOW now expresses this per unit area: **≥2.5 mitoses/mm²** for grade 2 (and ≥12.5/mm² for grade 3), assessed over 1.6 mm² of tissue in consecutive highest-activity fields.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776
  2. **Three of five atypia features**: necrosis, high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, prominent nucleoli, sheeting architecture, hypercellularity.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
  3. **Brain invasion** as a stand-alone criterion.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10339387/
  4. **Chordoid** or **clear cell** histological subtype.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

**"Brain invasion" needs careful handling for readers.** It sounds catastrophic and is not what patients imagine. The definition requires tumour cells to breach the pia mater and infiltrate into brain parenchyma — merely indenting the brain does not count, and extension along Virchow–Robin (perivascular) spaces does *not* qualify because the pia stays intact.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10339387/

Note also that its prognostic weight is genuinely contested (see §D.1).

### Grade 3 ("anaplastic" / malignant)
- Roughly 1–5% (Mayfield's patient material says ~2%).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm
- Criteria: **≥12.5 mitoses/mm²** (historically ≥20 per 10 HPF), or frank anaplasia.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- **Molecular criteria that override the microscope** (a genuine CNS5 innovation): a **TERT promoter mutation** or **homozygous CDKN2A/B deletion** makes the tumour grade 3 regardless of how it looks histologically.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240 · https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776

### What CNS5 *stopped* doing — a change readers with older reports will hit
- **Papillary and rhabdoid meningiomas are no longer automatically grade 3.** They can be grade 1, 2 or 3 and should not be graded on histology alone.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240
- CNS5 no longer uses other "atypical" features to push other morphological subtypes to grade 2.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240

So a person holding a 2015 report saying "papillary meningioma, WHO grade III" may be re-graded under the current system. This is exactly the kind of thing to flag.

### The most current refinement: cIMPACT-NOW update 8
cIMPACT-NOW is the official interim update process for the WHO CNS classification. Update 8 (2024/2025) clarified meningioma grading further:
- Standardised mitotic counting to per-mm² thresholds (above).
- Reaffirmed the strict pia-breach definition of brain invasion; molecular testing is now considered **essential** before assigning grade in "brain-invasive otherwise benign" (BIOB) meningiomas.
- **Chromosome 1p loss:** meningiomas with complete or segmental 1p deletion together with 22q monosomy or NF2 variants should be assigned **grade 2** even if histologically benign — a shift away from purely morphology-based grading.
- **H3K27me3 loss** correlates with recurrence risk in grade 2 tumours and gives prognostic information but does *not* currently change grade.
- **TERT promoter and CDKN2A/B testing** is recommended for grade 2 and 3 meningiomas; either alteration means grade 3.
- Molecular testing is specifically recommended for: borderline grade 1–2 morphology, BIOB meningiomas, grade 1 tumours with unexpectedly rapid growth, chordoid/clear cell/rhabdoid/papillary histology, and paediatric/young-adult cases.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776

**Other markers a patient may see on a report:** Ki-67 / MIB-1 proliferation index (higher = higher recurrence risk); progesterone receptor status.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320886/

## A.4 Location drives symptoms far more than size

This is the organising idea of the page. Meningioma symptoms are non-specific and vary by location; many are asymptomatic entirely.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

| Location | What it sits near | Typical symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| **Convexity** (surface of the brain, under the skull vault) | Motor/sensory/speech cortex | Seizures, headache, limb weakness, difficulty speaking, visual field loss; often silent until large | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm · https://www.mountsinai.org/care/neurosurgery/services/meningiomas/types |
| **Parasagittal / falx** (from the dura between the two hemispheres) | Leg motor strip, sagittal sinus, frontal lobes | Leg weakness, personality change, apathy/flattened affect, headache, vision problems, unsteadiness, tremor, memory problems | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm · https://www.mountsinai.org/care/neurosurgery/services/meningiomas/types |
| **Sphenoid wing** (behind the eyes; ~20% of meningiomas) | Optic nerve, cavernous sinus, cranial nerves III/IV/V/VI, temporal lobe | Vision loss, double vision, facial numbness, proptosis (bulging eye), seizures | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Olfactory groove** (floor of the front of the skull) | Olfactory nerves, frontal lobes, optic apparatus | **Loss of smell (anosmia)** — often unnoticed for years; subtle personality change, memory difficulty, euphoria, poor concentration, urinary incontinence, visual impairment | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm · https://www.mountsinai.org/care/neurosurgery/services/meningiomas/types |
| **Suprasellar / tuberculum sellae** | Optic chiasm, pituitary | Visual field defects | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Posterior fossa / petroclival / CPA** | Cranial nerves V, VII, VIII, IX, X | Facial pain or numbness, facial weakness, hearing loss, voice change, swallowing difficulty | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Foramen magnum** | Brainstem, upper cord | Headache, gait difficulty, brainstem compression | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Intraventricular** | CSF pathways | Hydrocephalus, headache, memory problems, walking difficulty | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Intraorbital** | Eye and orbit | Proptosis, vision loss | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |
| **Spinal** | Spinal cord, nerve roots | Back pain (characteristically worse at night), sensory loss, leg weakness/paralysis | https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm |

**"Skull base" as an umbrella term:** skull-base meningiomas grow in the bones forming the floor of the skull and the bony ridge behind the eyes, and are **harder to remove surgically than convexity meningiomas** — a difference in the plan, not in the tumour's grade.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

**The publishable point:** a 1 cm meningioma pressing on the optic nerve will take your sight; a 4 cm meningioma on the frontal convexity may cause nothing at all. Size on the report is not the headline. Location is.

## A.5 Why so many are found by accident

Meningiomas are slow-growing, so "they are often found incidentally in brain images such as MRI or CT when testing is done for other reasons."
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/meningioma/

Population data on how ordinary this is:
- In the Rotterdam population MRI study (Vernooij et al., NEJM 2007), incidental findings on brain MRI in the general population were common; among findings other than infarcts, benign primary tumours — **mainly meningiomas — were present in 1.6%**, and cerebral aneurysms in 1.8%. Prevalence of asymptomatic meningiomas increased with age.
https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa070972
- A meta-analysis across 36 studies and 37,697 individuals found a **pooled prevalence of incidental meningioma of 0.52%** (95% CI 0.34–0.78), higher in older people, women, and populations outside Asia.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00701-021-04919-8
- One review reports a pathological (autopsy-detected) prevalence on the order of 1 in 100 in the US.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

**Framing to use:** meningiomas are common enough that a meaningful number of people are walking around with one they will never know about. Being scanned for a headache, a car accident, or dizziness and finding one is not bad luck revealing a hidden disaster — it is the expected consequence of imaging a population in which these are common.

## A.6 Observation ("watch and wait") is a real plan, not a brush-off

### Guideline standing
NCI lists **active surveillance** as a treatment for grade 1 meningiomas with no signs or symptoms, and defines it as "closely watching a patient's condition but not giving any treatment unless there are changes in test results."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

The RANO working group and the EANO meningioma task force recommend observation for small tumours until symptoms develop, growth is sustained, or there is concern about encroachment on sensitive structures.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10180371/

A series following 675 patients with untreated meningiomas on serial MRI supported observation early on for small asymptomatic meningiomas, especially those under 2 cm in initial diameter.
https://thejns.org/focus/view/journals/neurosurg-focus/31/6/2011.9.focus11220.xml

### What the natural history actually shows
- Absolute annual growth rate of incidental meningiomas: **0.03–2.62 cm³/year, mean 0.796 cm³/year**; the majority (66%) grow less than 1 cm³/year.
- Tumour doubling time: **1.27 to 143.5 years, mean 21.6 years.**
- Three growth patterns: no growth, linear growth, exponential growth.
- Growth frequently **plateaus** — serial volumetry showed growth plateauing at about 1.5 years.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10180371/

- Most benign meningiomas grow at about **5.82% by volume per year.**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

### Features that predict *slower* growth (useful, concrete reassurance)
- Older age at discovery (younger patients had higher annual growth rates and shorter doubling times).
- **Calcification** on CT — calcified meningiomas grew more slowly.
- **Hypointense or isointense T2 signal** on MRI — lower growth rate.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10180371/

These three are things a patient can literally look for in their own report, and are far more useful than a generic "we'll keep an eye on it."

### Scan schedule
Typical practice: repeat scan at about six months, then annually; intervals may be 3, 6 or 12 months at first, stretching to yearly or every other year if there is no progression.
https://www.aaroncohen-gadol.com/en/patients/meningioma/treatment/observation
https://nyulangone.org/conditions/meningioma/treatments/watchful-waiting-for-meningioma

Mayfield: "Regular follow-up MRI or CT scans (1 to 2 years) are an important part of long-term care."
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

There is also a published prognostic model for personalising monitoring regimes in incidental asymptomatic meningioma (IMPACT), i.e. this is an active area of formalisation rather than guesswork.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/22/2/278/5585790
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8768908/

### The emotional cost — say it out loud
Waiting is not free. Patients on follow-up frequently experience stress and anxiety around each scan; one study found the watch-and-wait strategy associated with a **4.26-fold higher risk of a pathological depression score**. Some patients have used CBT counselling specifically to manage watch-and-wait anxiety. Patients unhappy with watch-and-wait are entitled to ask for a second opinion.
https://www.thebraintumourcharity.org/brain-tumour-diagnosis-treatment/treating-brain-tumours/adult-treatments/watch-and-wait/ (via search summary)
https://connect.mayoclinic.org/discussion/have-had-a-mri-that-revealed-a-large-structure-that-is-presumed/

**This is an important editorial opportunity.** Most sites present watch-and-wait as good news and stop. Naming "scanxiety" as a recognised, studied phenomenon — and pointing to what helps — is genuinely differentiating content for this audience.

### What triggers a switch to treatment
Symptom development, sustained growth, or encroachment on sensitive structures.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10180371/

## A.7 Treatment

### Surgery
Craniotomy is the primary definitive treatment for symptomatic or growing tumours.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

**Simpson grade** — the extent-of-resection scale a patient may see in their operative note:
- I: complete removal including dural attachment and abnormal bone
- II: gross removal with coagulation of the dural attachment
- III: gross total resection (without dural resection/coagulation)
- IV: partial resection
- V: biopsy or decompression only
Simpson I–III are all considered gross total resection (GTR).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

Surgical adjuncts patients may hear about: functional MRI brain mapping to preserve eloquent cortex; **preoperative embolisation** of feeding vessels to reduce intraoperative bleeding; image-guided navigation; keyhole/endoscopic endonasal skull-base approaches.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

Location determines difficulty: convexity, parasagittal and sphenoid wing lesions generally do well with complete removal; suprasellar, cavernous sinus and skull-base locations carry higher complication rates.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

### Radiation
NCI, grade 1: radiation therapy if tumour remains after surgery; **stereotactic radiosurgery for tumours smaller than 3 cm**; radiation therapy for tumours that cannot be removed surgically. Grade 2 and 3: surgery **and** radiation therapy.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

Modality detail:
- **Single-fraction SRS** — typically tumours <2 cm with adequate distance from critical structures.
- **Fractionated SRS (3–5 fractions)** — larger lesions or those near organs at risk; lower treatment-related oedema than single-fraction SRS.
- **Conventionally fractionated radiotherapy** — 1.8–2.0 Gy/fraction to 50.4–60 Gy; preferred for large tumours or proximity to multiple critical structures.
- Standard adjuvant dose for grade 2: **54 Gy in 30 fractions**. RTOG 0539 high-risk (grade 3) protocol: **60 Gy in 30 fractions.**
- Complications occur in roughly 9% after SRS: radiation necrosis, cranial nerve deficit, oedema, hydrocephalus.
- **Proton beam therapy** is used to limit dose beyond the target.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

**Indications by grade:**
- Grade 1 after GTR: observation preferred. Radiation considered for symptoms from residual disease, high probability of future symptoms, or skull-base location where re-resection would be difficult.
- Grade 2: adjuvant radiotherapy substantially reduces recurrence after both complete and incomplete resection.
- Grade 3: nearly all cases receive postoperative radiotherapy.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

### Drug therapy — the honest answer
**There are no indication-specific FDA-approved systemic therapies for recurrent or treatment-refractory meningioma.**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12333779/

Agents used or studied (historically or in trials): hydroxyurea, alpha-interferon, somatostatin analogues (octreotide — most meningiomas strongly express somatostatin receptor 2A), bevacizumab.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12333779/

EANO 2021 issued a **Level C** recommendation for bevacizumab where no other local treatment option is feasible — i.e. a weak recommendation of last resort, not a standard of care.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article-abstract/23/11/1821/6310843

Current molecularly targeted trials (worth a "research is active" note, with anti-hype framing):
- Alliance **A071401** — a genomically driven phase 2 trial of SMO / AKT / NF2 / CDK-directed agents in progressive meningioma: vismodegib (SMO-mutant), capivasertib (AKT/PIK3CA-mutant), abemaciclib (NF2 or CDK-pathway altered), FAK inhibitor GSK2256098 (NF2-mutant).
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02371 · https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-04141-4
- CEVOREM: everolimus + octreotide showed activity in recurrent grade 1–3 meningioma.
https://aacrjournals.org/clincancerres/article/26/3/552/83246/Everolimus-and-Octreotide-for-Patients-with
- EANO has published a dedicated guideline on **molecular testing of meningiomas for targeted therapy selection** (2025) — evidence that molecular profiling is entering routine meningioma care.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/4/869/7907197

### Imaging beyond MRI
**⁶⁸Ga-DOTATATE / DOTATOC PET** binds somatostatin receptor subtype 2, strongly expressed by most meningiomas. Useful for diagnosis, radiotherapy planning and follow-up; overall detection rate ~92% vs ~90% for contrast-enhanced MRI. Especially useful for indeterminate skull-base lesions where tissue diagnosis is not available, to avoid inappropriate intervention.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-87866-9 · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12770794/

## A.8 Recurrence — including "why can a grade 1 come back?"

**Publishable qualitative framing:**
A meningioma is grown from the meninges. Surgery removes the lump, and where possible the piece of dura it grew from, and any abnormal bone. If any of the cells that produce it are left behind — which is often unavoidable when the tumour is wrapped around a nerve or a major vein — the tumour can slowly regrow from those cells. That is why "benign" and "completely removed" are two different statements, and why follow-up scans continue for years even after a grade 1 tumour.

Supported by: the Simpson grading scheme itself, which ranks resections precisely by *how much of the dural attachment and abnormal bone came out*, and by the fact that Simpson V (biopsy/decompression only) is described as making symptomatic recurrence highly likely.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

**[NUMBERS — EDITORIAL DECISION NEEDED]**
These are recurrence figures, not survival figures. They are the clinical justification for the grade-based plan. Some are expressed at 5/10 years.
- Grade 1 after GTR, no adjuvant therapy: ~9% recurrence at 10 years. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- Grade 2 after GTR without radiotherapy: >40% by 5 years; with adjuvant radiotherapy ~8–11.8% at 5 years. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- Grade 2 after subtotal resection: 91% without adjuvant radiotherapy vs 20% with. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- Surveillance-framework series: completely resected grade 1 — 10% at 5 years, 20% at 10 years, **no recurrences beyond 11 years**; completely resected grade 2 — 24% at 5 years, 50% at 10 years, recurrences continuing throughout; completely resected grade 3 — 63% at 5 years, all recurring before 10 years. https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/12/3/478/7914655
- 39% of atypical meningioma patients experience three or more recurrences, with shortening intervals between them. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/

**Recommendation:** publish the *shape* (grade 1 rarely comes back and, once past about a decade, essentially stops; grade 2 comes back often enough that radiation after surgery roughly quarters the risk; grade 3 comes back often and early) without the percentage table. The "no recurrences beyond 11 years" finding is unusually reassuring and worth stating qualitatively — it is the basis for eventually stopping surveillance.

### Follow-up imaging schedule
- Post-op MRI at **48 hours** to establish extent of resection, and at ~4 months to detect rapid regrowth.
- Completely resected grade 1: annual MRI, with discontinuation of surveillance reasonable if no recurrence after **11 years**.
- Completely resected grade 2: annual MRI indefinitely.
- Grade 3: MRI every 3–4 months for 2 years, then every 6 months indefinitely.
- Incompletely resected, any grade: followed indefinitely.
https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/12/3/478/7914655

**"Your scans will stop one day" is a genuinely under-communicated piece of good news for grade 1 patients.**

## A.9 Risk factors and associations

### Radiation
- Prior head radiation is an established cause. Childhood radiation for tinea capitis carried a **relative risk of 9.5** vs controls.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- Childhood cancer survivors who had intracranial radiotherapy carry a **5–12% risk of meningioma by age 40**, rising with dose.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41984-025-00373-1
- **Latency is long:** mean ~18.7 ± 10.2 years in most series; some sources cite ~30 years; individual cases 8–34 years after childhood irradiation.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11246846/ · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41984-025-00373-1
- Radiation-induced meningiomas are more often **multiple, recurrent and higher-grade**, and are molecularly distinct (more chromosome 1p and 22q loss, less NF2 inactivation).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41984-025-00373-1

**Important honesty note for the page:** the historical exposures that produced most of this evidence (tinea capitis irradiation, atomic bomb exposure, high-dose childhood cranial radiotherapy) are not equivalent to a modern dental X-ray or a CT scan. Do not let a reader conclude their head CT caused this.
https://thejns.org/focus/view/journals/neurosurg-focus/24/5/article-pE7.xml

### Hormones — the most actionable association, and the one with recent regulatory movement
- About **70% of meningiomas express progesterone receptors**; PR expression is reported in 39–88% of meningiomas across studies, while oestrogen receptor expression is <10% and often undetectable.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320886/
- Rapid growth during pregnancy with shrinkage after delivery has been documented.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320886/
- **High-dose progestogens are causally linked.** European regulators have recognised the causal nature of the association between exposure and intracranial meningioma. A March 2024 French study of >18,000 women who had meningioma surgery vs >90,000 controls confirmed risk for cyproterone acetate, nomegestrol acetate and chlormadinone acetate. Pooled odds ratio for cyproterone acetate ~12.36; nomegestrol and chlormadinone lower but still elevated.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12925135/ · https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/referral/nomegestrol-and-chlormadinone-article-31-referral-medicines-containing-nomegestrol-or-chlormadinone-prac-recommends-new-measures-minimise-risk-meningioma_en.pdf
- Concern has extended to medroxyprogesterone acetate and medrogestone.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11979271/
- **Practical patient guidance** (Cambridge University Hospitals patient information):
  - Cyproterone acetate must be **permanently discontinued** if a patient is diagnosed with a meningioma.
  - HRT should be avoided where possible unless menopausal symptoms cause greater distress.
  - **Standard combined oral contraceptives do not appear to increase meningioma risk.**
  - There is some evidence risk decreases after cyproterone acetate is stopped.
  - Evidence on hormonal fertility treatment is insufficient.
https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/hormone-therapy-and-meningioma/
- **Relevant to gender-affirming care:** cyproterone acetate is used in gender-affirming hormone therapy; this needs to be named explicitly and non-judgementally.
https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/hormone-therapy-and-meningioma/
- **Breast cancer link:** an increased rate of meningioma in breast cancer patients is described, but the hypothesis that shared hormone-receptor biology explains it was *disconfirmed* — no association was found between breast cancer hormone-receptor status and meningioma risk.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320886/

### Genetic / NF2-related schwannomatosis
- Alteration of **chromosome 22** is the most common abnormality in meningiomas.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm
- **45–58% of people with NF2-related schwannomatosis develop intracranial meningiomas.**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- **Terminology alert:** "neurofibromatosis type 2" was renamed **NF2-related schwannomatosis (NF2-SWN)** in the 2022 international consensus, because neurofibromas do not occur in the condition, making the old name factually wrong. "Schwannomatosis" is now an umbrella term, subclassified by the gene involved (NF2, SMARCB1, LZTR1).
https://www.ctf.org/news/revised-diagnostic-criteria-for-nf2-swn-announcement/ · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35674741/ · https://nervetumours.org.uk/news/nf2-name-change
- Other predisposition syndromes associated with meningioma: nevoid basal cell carcinoma (Gorlin), MEN1, Cowden, Werner, BAP1 tumour predisposition, Rubinstein–Taybi.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/
- **Molecular subtype–location correlation** worth mentioning: NF2-altered meningiomas cluster in convexity and posterior locations; TRAF7/KLF4/AKT1/SMO/PIK3CA-altered meningiomas cluster at the anterior skull base — which is why a skull-base meningioma often has a different molecular driver from a convexity one.
https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240

# PART B — BRAIN METASTASES

## B.1 The single most confusing thing: it is still the original cancer

NCI states it explicitly: **"Metastatic cancer has the same name as the primary cancer. For example, breast cancer that spreads to the lung is called metastatic breast cancer, not lung cancer."**
https://www.cancer.gov/types/metastatic-cancer

And: "Cancer that spreads from where it started to a distant part of the body is called metastatic cancer." A metastatic brain tumour is staged and treated as stage 4 of the original cancer.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/metastatic-cancer

NCI's CNS PDQ makes the same distinction: "A tumor that starts in another part of the body and spreads to the brain is called a metastatic brain tumor."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

NBTS: a metastatic brain tumour "begins as cancer in another part of the body"; the original site is the primary cancer; these are also called lesions or brain metastases.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/

**Why this is not a pedantic point — it is the whole treatment logic.** If lung cancer cells travel to the brain, they are still lung cancer cells. Under the microscope they look like lung cancer. They respond to the drugs that lung cancer responds to. So the systemic (drug) treatment is chosen based on the *lung* cancer's molecular profile — EGFR, ALK, KRAS G12C, PD-L1 — not on anything about the brain.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

The practical consequence readers need spelled out: **the oncologist who treats your original cancer is still your main doctor.** The neurosurgeon and radiation oncologist are added to the team; they do not replace it.

**A useful counterpart fact:** primary brain tumours almost never do the reverse. Extracranial metastasis from glioblastoma occurs in roughly 0.4–0.5% of cases, attributed to the blood–brain barrier, the absence of a lymphatic drainage system, the dura mater and thickened vascular basement membranes.
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cnr2.1905

So the traffic is essentially one-way: body → brain, common; brain → body, vanishingly rare. That symmetry is a clean explanatory device.

## B.2 How common, and from where

- Brain metastases are **more common than primary brain tumours**, making up over half of all clinically diagnosed brain tumours in adults, and the incidence has risen as systemic cancer therapy has improved and people live longer with metastatic disease.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/ · https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/
- NCI: metastatic brain tumours are more common than primary ones, most often from lung cancer.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**Two different statistics that are easy to conflate — keep them apart:**

**(a) Of people with a given cancer, what fraction develop brain metastases?** (Metropolitan Detroit Cancer Surveillance System, 1973–2001): total 9.6% across all primary sites; lung 19.9%, melanoma 6.9%, renal 6.5%, breast 5.1%, colorectal 1.8%.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2004.12.149

**(b) Of people who have brain metastases, where did they come from?** (tertiary centre, 2008–2018): lung 43.0%, breast 19.9%, melanoma 8.2%, renal 5.0%, colorectal 3.8%.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0967586820314478

NBTS phrases the common primaries as "usually in the lung, breast, colon, kidney, or skin (melanoma), but can originate in any part of the body."
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/

NCI's metastatic cancer page lists breast, kidney, lung and melanoma as cancers that spread to the brain.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/metastatic-cancer

## B.3 How they get there, and where they land

- Most tumour cells reach the brain by **haematogenous spread, nearly always via arterial circulation.**
https://www.medlink.com/articles/brain-metastases
- They lodge preferentially just beneath the **grey–white junction**, because blood vessels narrow at that point and act as a trap for tumour emboli; and at the terminal **"watershed" areas** at the borders of the major cerebral arterial territories.
https://www.medlink.com/articles/brain-metastases
- Distribution: approximately **80% cerebral hemispheres, 15% cerebellum, 5% brainstem** — roughly following the relative weight of, and blood flow to, each region.
https://www.medlink.com/articles/brain-metastases
- NBTS phrases it for patients: most are in the cerebrum, though the cerebellum or brain stem can be affected.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/

**Reader-facing consequence:** metastases sit at the edge of the brain, often near the surface, which is part of why single lesions can sometimes be removed surgically and why they respond well to focused radiation.

**Symptoms** are the general mechanism from §0.1 plus location. NCI lists headache, seizures and dizziness as warning signs of spread to the brain.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/metastatic-cancer
NBTS lists seizures, headaches, behavioural and cognitive changes, and lack of coordination.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/

## B.4 Single vs multiple — and why the count changes everything

**More than half of people with metastatic brain tumours have multiple lesions.**
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/

The number of lesions is the main axis on which treatment options turn.

### Surgery
Recommended for:
- a single (or suspected) brain metastasis **without a known primary cancer** — because tissue is needed to make a diagnosis;
- large lesions causing mass effect and neurological symptoms;
- unknown primary requiring tissue diagnosis;
- patients suitable for immunotherapy (surgery facilitates steroid tapering).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**En bloc** resection (removing the metastasis in one piece) may reduce recurrence and leptomeningeal metastasis risk versus **piecemeal** resection. Postoperative MRI within 48 hours is recommended.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

Multiple metastases with uncontrolled systemic disease may not benefit from surgery.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Historical anchor (Patchell, NEJM 1990):** the trial that established surgery's role — patients with a single brain metastasis randomised to surgical removal plus radiotherapy vs needle biopsy plus radiotherapy. Together with the SRS trials this "changed the nihilistic approach" to brain metastases.
https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJM199002223220802 · https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2022.847110/full

**Patchell II (JAMA 1998), postoperative radiotherapy after resection of a single metastasis:** less recurrence anywhere in the brain (18% vs 70%), less recurrence at the resection site (10% vs 46%), less recurrence elsewhere in the brain (14% vs 37%), and less neurological death.
https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/188127
*(These are local-control figures, not survival. Still: recommend qualitative framing — "surgery alone leaves a high chance of it growing back at the same spot, which is why radiation usually follows.")*

### Radiation thresholds (ASCO–SNO–ASTRO 2022 and derivatives)
- **SRS recommended** for patients with ECOG performance status 0–2 and up to **four** intact brain metastases; **conditionally recommended** for up to **ten**.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314 · https://www.astro.org/provider-resources/guidelines/clinical-practice-guidelines/brain-metastases-joint-guideline
- **SRS alone** (rather than WBRT, or WBRT+SRS) should be offered for **one to four unresected** brain metastases, **excluding small-cell carcinoma**.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314
- **After resection:** radiation (SRS or WBRT) is recommended to improve intracranial control; if there are limited additional metastases, **SRS is preferred over WBRT** for the resection cavity.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314
- Derivative guideline detail: SRS alone for 1–2 resected metastases if the cavity can be treated safely; SRS may be considered for 5–10 metastases with cumulative volume <15 mL; cavity SRS recommended after complete or incomplete resection.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- **Evidence for the 5–10 threshold (JLGK0901):** a prospective multi-institutional study of patients with 1–10 newly diagnosed metastases (largest <10 mL and <3 cm; total cumulative volume ≤15 mL; KPS ≥70), which found SRS alone for 5–10 metastases non-inferior to 2–4 metastases.
https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanonc/article/PIIS1470-2045(14)70061-0/abstract

**Reader-facing framing:** "one or a few" versus "many" is not an arbitrary line — it reflects whether each lesion can be targeted individually with a high dose while sparing everything in between.

## B.5 Whole-brain radiotherapy vs stereotactic radiosurgery — the cognitive debate that moved practice

This is the section where BrainHarbor can be genuinely better than most patient sites, because the shift is real, recent, and rarely explained.

### What SRS and WBRT physically are
- **SRS:** a high dose delivered in a single fraction (or up to 5) precisely to each visible lesion, with rigid immobilisation; the brain between the lesions gets very little.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm
- **WBRT:** a lower dose to the entire brain, typically 30 Gy in 10 fractions or 20 Gy in 5 fractions — treating visible lesions *and* microscopic disease not yet visible on MRI.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

### The trial that changed things (Brown et al., JAMA 2016 — Alliance/NCCTG N0574)
213 patients with 1–3 brain metastases (each <3 cm), 34 North American institutions, randomised to SRS alone (n=111) vs SRS + WBRT (n=102). Primary endpoint: cognitive deterioration at 3 months (decline >1 SD from baseline on at least one cognitive test).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5313044/ · https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/2536637

**Results:**
| Outcome at 3 months | SRS alone | SRS + WBRT |
|---|---|---|
| Cognitive deterioration | 63.5% | 91.7% (difference −28.2%, P<.001) |
| Immediate memory decline | 8.2% | 30.4% |
| Delayed memory decline | 19.7% | 51.1% |
| Verbal fluency decline | 1.9% | 18.6% |
| Intracranial tumour control | 75.3% | 93.7% |
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5313044/

Quality of life at 3 months was better with SRS alone. **Adding WBRT gave better tumour control in the brain but no survival advantage**, and the authors concluded "SRS alone may be a preferred strategy."
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5313044/

**This is the honest trade-off to publish:** WBRT controls disease in the brain better — including spots too small to see — but at a measurable cost to memory and word-finding, without living longer. Given that, many teams now choose the option that protects thinking, and treat new spots with more focused radiation later if they appear.

**Caution for the writer:** the "63.5% vs 91.7%" numbers make SRS alone look worse than readers expect — *most people in both arms declined on at least one test.* If numbers are published at all, that context is mandatory, or the page misleads. Consider publishing only the direction and the specific domains (memory, word-finding), not the percentages.

### WBRT has not disappeared — it got safer
When WBRT is needed, two modifications now come as standard for suitable patients:
- **Hippocampal avoidance** — shaping the radiation to spare the hippocampi.
- **Memantine** — a drug given alongside.
ASCO–SNO–ASTRO: for patients with favourable prognosis and brain metastases ineligible for surgery and/or SRS, WBRT is recommended **with hippocampal avoidance where possible and the addition of memantine**.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314

**NRG Oncology CC001 (phase 3, 518 patients, 2015–2018):** HA-WBRT + memantine vs WBRT + memantine. The hippocampal-avoidance arm had significantly better MoCA cognitive scores at 3, 6 and 12 months; better ECOG performance and quality-of-life scores at 6 months; larger preserved hippocampal volumes; and better white-matter integrity on imaging — with **no differences in tumour response, severe adverse events, or survival**. Also fewer patient-reported symptoms (fatigue, difficulty remembering, difficulty speaking).
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.19.02767 · https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(23)00438-8/abstract · https://www.nrgoncology.org/Home/News/Post/nrg-oncology-trial-shows-hippocampal-avoidance-during-whole-brain-radiotherapy-for-brain-metastases-reduces-risks-of-cognitive-toxicity-and-neurologic-symptoms/

**Where WBRT is still avoided:** in asymptomatic patients; routinely after surgery or SRS; and in patients with poor performance status where supportive care alone is appropriate. It is considered mainly for multiple metastases unsuitable for SRS.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Terminology alert for old paperwork:** a patient (or a relative recalling a family member's care from 15 years ago) may expect WBRT as the default, because it was. The page should say directly that practice has changed and why.

### Radiation necrosis — the late complication
- Occurs in about **7–24%** of patients after stereotactic radiotherapy, because of the high local dose.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11383763/
- It is increasingly relevant precisely because people are living long enough after treatment for late effects to appear.
https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(23)07647-2/fulltext
- **It looks like tumour regrowth on MRI.** Advanced MRI techniques and amino-acid PET are suggested to distinguish treatment-related change from tumour progression.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- Management: corticosteroids first, though up to 20% of lesions are refractory to steroids, vitamin E and pentoxifylline; **bevacizumab** for symptomatic necrosis not responding to steroids; **laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT)** as an alternative to open surgery for inadequate response to medical treatment.
https://www.redjournal.org/article/S0360-3016(23)07647-2/fulltext · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27583180/ · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-020-03570-0

**Reader-facing point that prevents unnecessary terror:** a spot that grows on a scan after radiation is not automatically the cancer coming back. It can be the treated area reacting. Telling the two apart may need a different kind of scan.

## B.6 Drugs that cross the blood–brain barrier

The historic assumption was that systemic drugs don't work in the brain. That has changed for specific molecular subgroups, and the change is now embedded in guidelines.

### The guideline position — the big shift
For patients with **asymptomatic** brain metastases who are eligible for CNS-directed systemic therapy, multidisciplinary and patient-centred decision-making to determine whether **local therapy may be safely deferred** is conditionally recommended. Local therapy should *not* be deferred unless deferral is specifically recommended.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314

Derivative guidelines describe this as a key paradigm shift — deferring local therapy in favour of systemic therapy for asymptomatic brain metastases, a significant departure from historically WBRT-focused approaches.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

### Why some drugs work in the brain and others don't
Large molecules such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab do not meaningfully penetrate the blood–brain barrier at approved doses; small-molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors and antibody–drug conjugates, because of their smaller size, are more likely to cross. Newer-generation ALK inhibitors (alectinib, brigatinib, lorlatinib) were designed with greater BBB penetration; lorlatinib is a third-generation TKI specifically designed to cross.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11320077/ · https://dailynews.ascopubs.org/do/cns-metastases-and-her2-positive-breast-cancer-therapeutic-paths-can-we-take-after

Response can differ between intracranial and extracranial sites because of penetration differences and tumour heterogeneity — i.e. **the cancer in your brain and the cancer in your body can behave differently on the same drug.** That is worth saying plainly; patients find it bewildering.
https://doi.org/10.3390/cancers14010017

### By tumour type
**NSCLC (asymptomatic/oligosymptomatic):**
- EGFR-mutant: **osimertinib** (or icotinib).
- ALK-rearranged: **alectinib, brigatinib, ceritinib** (lorlatinib in later lines).
- PD-L1 ≥50%: immune checkpoint inhibition alone; PD-L1 <50%: chemo-immunotherapy.
- **KRAS G12C:** adagrasib and sotorasib were added to NCCN for brain metastases from KRAS G12C-mutated NSCLC.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/ · https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889715/

**Melanoma:**
- **Ipilimumab + nivolumab** — an option for all patients with asymptomatic brain metastases, and preferred regardless of BRAF status.
- **Dabrafenib + trametinib** for asymptomatic BRAF V600E-mutant brain metastases.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**HER2-positive breast cancer:**
- **Tucatinib + trastuzumab + capecitabine** for progressive disease; systemic therapy with delayed WBRT for asymptomatic patients.
- **Trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXd)** showed a best intracranial response rate of **73.3%** in patients with active brain metastases from HER2-positive breast cancer.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/ · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9499862/

**HER2-negative breast cancer:** capecitabine, eribulin, carboplatin or bevacizumab on progression.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Small cell lung cancer:** platinum-based chemotherapy ± immune checkpoint inhibition. Note SCLC is explicitly *excluded* from the "SRS alone for 1–4 metastases" recommendation.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/ · https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314

**MSI-H/dMMR or TMB-H (>10 mut/Mb):** pembrolizumab included for isolated brain metastases (NCCN category 2B — i.e. lower-level consensus).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889715/

**Anti-hype guardrail for this section:** every one of these is subgroup-specific. The correct patient takeaway is *"ask whether your cancer has been tested for markers that come with a brain-active drug"* — not *"there are pills that treat brain metastases."*

## B.7 How teams weigh brain disease against body disease

The decision is not "how bad is the brain tumour" in isolation. Factors named in the guidelines:

- **Performance status.** SRS recommendations are conditioned on ECOG 0–2. Supportive care without WBRT is the recommendation for poor performance status.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- **Whether the systemic disease is controlled.** Multiple brain metastases with uncontrolled systemic disease may not benefit from surgery.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- **Number and total volume of brain lesions** (see §B.4 thresholds).
- **Whether a CNS-active systemic option exists** for that molecular subtype — if yes, and the patient is asymptomatic, local therapy may be deferred.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314
- **Symptoms.** Steroids only in symptomatic patients; WBRT avoided in asymptomatic patients.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- **Prognostic tools:** the **GPA (Graded Prognostic Assessment)** score is referenced as a prognostic tool in guidelines. *Concept only — do not publish its survival strata.*
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/
- NCI's factors for metastatic tumours: number of brain lesions, location, response to treatment, and whether the primary tumour is progressing.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**Screening:** for high-risk asymptomatic patients — lung cancer, stage IV melanoma, HER2-positive or triple-negative breast cancer — screening brain MRI is recommended. Neuroimaging is mandatory for any cancer patient with new neurological symptoms.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Follow-up:** neurological examination every 2–3 months (or earlier if progression suspected), with brain MRI at similar intervals.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Imaging protocol:** brain MRI with pre- and post-contrast T1, T2 and/or T2-FLAIR, and diffusion-weighted sequences.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

## B.8 Leptomeningeal disease

**Definition:** the spread of tumour cells within the leptomeninges and the subarachnoid space — i.e. into the fluid layer around the brain and spinal cord rather than as a solid lump inside it. Synonyms a patient may encounter: **neoplastic meningitis, carcinomatous meningitis, leptomeningeal carcinomatosis.**
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Which cancers:** most commonly breast, lung and melanoma. Most patients with leptomeningeal metastasis also have concurrent brain metastases (breast 36–66%, lung 66–82%, melanoma 69–87%).
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Why the symptoms are so scattered:** because the disease is distributed along the CSF pathways rather than located at one point, it produces symptoms at multiple, unrelated levels of the nervous system at once. Mechanisms:
- **Cranial nerve involvement** by direct tumour infiltration → double vision, visual disturbance, hearing loss.
- **Hydrocephalus** from CSF obstruction → raised intracranial pressure symptoms.
- **Cauda equina syndrome** from lumbosacral nerve root compression → motor and sensory deficits, bladder dysfunction.
Presenting symptoms: headache, nausea, vomiting, mental status change, gait disturbance, cranial nerve palsies, radicular weakness, spinal or neck pain.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Diagnosis:**
- Detailed neurological evaluation + **MRI of the brain AND the whole spine** with gadolinium (0.1 mmol/kg, imaged 10 minutes after injection). Brain: 3D T1, FLAIR, DWI, T2, post-contrast 3D T1 and post-contrast 3D FLAIR; spine: sagittal fat-suppressed T2, sagittal T1, post-contrast T1. Slice thickness ≤1 mm brain, ≤3 mm spine.
- **Lumbar puncture with CSF cytology.** Ideally >10 mL of CSF, at least 5 mL, processed within 30 minutes of sampling. Tumour cells are detected in **60.5–83%** of contemporary cohorts at diagnosis — so **a negative tap does not rule it out**, and a second LP is recommended if the first is negative or equivocal.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Diagnostic categories** patients may see:
- **Type I (confirmed):** positive CSF cytology or leptomeningeal biopsy; subtyped IA–ID by MRI pattern (linear, nodular, combined, or hydrocephalus/normal).
- **Type II (probable/possible):** typical clinical and/or imaging findings without positive cytology, in someone with a cancer history at reasonable risk, after excluding alternatives.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Treatment:**
- **Intrathecal chemotherapy** — drug delivered directly into the CSF, because systemic drugs may not reach it. Standard agents: methotrexate (10–15 mg twice weekly, then weekly, then monthly), cytarabine (10 mg), thiotepa (10 mg). Administration via a **ventricular reservoir (Ommaya)** is preferred to the lumbar route when feasible; aseptic technique, CSF withdrawal before injection, slow administration at ~1 mL/min.
- **Systemic therapy** with CNS-penetrant agents chosen on the primary tumour's molecular profile (e.g. osimertinib for EGFR-mutant lung cancer).
- **Focal radiotherapy** for nodular disease and symptomatic sites; **WBRT** 30 Gy/10 or 20 Gy/5 for extensive disease, palliative in intent; **craniospinal irradiation** limited by toxicity, with proton therapy showing potential benefit. NCCN 2024 incorporated craniospinal irradiation for leptomeningeal disease.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/ · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11889715/
- **Hydrocephalus:** ventriculoperitoneal shunting relieves symptoms — in a cohort of 190 patients, 83% experienced clinical improvement and 79% had no complications. Programmable or on/off valves allow intrathecal drug delivery to continue.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/
- Steroids at the lowest dose for the shortest time; non-interacting anticonvulsants; no primary seizure prophylaxis.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Follow-up and what counts as improvement:** standardised neurological exam every 2 months for 6 months then every 3 months if stable; cerebrospinal MRI every 6–12 weeks on the same scanner/field strength; CSF studies every 2–3 months (or at each intrathecal injection). Response requires conversion of previously positive to negative CSF, maintained ≥4 weeks. **Clinical stabilisation is the typical best response, because most neurological deficits are irreversible.** Imaging progression without clinical decline may not warrant an immediate treatment change.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10619142/

**Editorial note:** this is the hardest content in the brief to write at 6th grade without either terrifying or falsely reassuring. The publishable honest framing is: *this is a serious complication; the goal of treatment is usually to hold symptoms steady and keep you comfortable and functioning, rather than to make everything go back to how it was.* That statement is directly supported by the guideline's own response definition.

## B.9 Brain metastasis as the *first* sign of cancer

In **33–66%** of cases, brain metastases are the first sign of a systemic tumour.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5977273/

Workup when the primary is unknown:
- CT chest — strongly considered, especially with a smoking history or other lung cancer risk factors; CT abdomen and pelvis and a bone scan are usually also warranted.
https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/management-brain-metastases
- **FDG PET/CT** is the most suitable and accurate modality for identifying an unknown primary in patients with metastatic brain disease; in one study it identified the primary in 20 of 26 patients (77%).
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5977273/
- Surgery/biopsy is indicated specifically to obtain tissue when the primary is unknown.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**This is the bridge between Page B and Page C.** A person told "there are spots on your brain scan and we think they came from somewhere else, but we don't know where yet" belongs on both pages.

## B.10 Questions brain-metastasis patients need answered

1. Is this a new cancer, or my cancer that has moved?
2. Which doctor is in charge now?
3. How many spots are there, and how big?
4. Does my cancer have any marker that means a drug could work in my brain?
5. Why are you *not* radiating my whole brain? / Why *are* you?
6. Will radiation affect my memory? What is being done to protect it?
7. What is the steroid doing, and how long will I be on it?
8. If a spot grows later, does that mean the cancer is back, or could it be the treatment?
9. What symptoms mean I should call you today?
10. Can I still drive/work?
11. Is my case going to a tumour board? (See §C.6.)

# PART C — "WE DON'T HAVE A NAME FOR IT YET"

For someone told there is *something* on their scan, with no diagnosis. This page's job is to make an unbearable, information-free waiting period feel structured and survivable.

## C.1 What actually happens between "we found something" and a name

The pathway, in order:

1. **A scan shows an abnormality** — often a CT done for another reason, or an MRI ordered for headaches, seizure, or a neurological symptom.
2. **Better imaging.** MRI with gadolinium contrast is the primary diagnostic tool; CT, PET and SPECT may be added. PET can help distinguish primary from metastatic tumours.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
3. **Referral to a specialist team.** The referring clinician transfers imaging and clinical information to a neuro-oncology panel; panellists (neurologist, neurosurgeon, neuroradiologist with neuro-oncology expertise) are notified. In one described triage model, the panel meets three times per week and returns a consensus radiological differential diagnosis and advice **within two working days**.
https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/8/5/559/6316211
4. **A decision about tissue** — biopsy, resection, or neither (watch).
5. **Pathology.** Frozen section during surgery, then permanent sections, then immunohistochemistry, then molecular tests.
6. **Integrated diagnosis** issued (see §C.4).
7. **Tumour board** review and a treatment plan.
https://braintumor.org/news/brain-tumor-board-101-answers-to-frequently-asked-questions/

**Why this list matters:** the reader currently experiences this as silence. Naming the steps converts "nothing is happening" into "step 3 of 7."

---

## C.2 Why imaging alone often cannot say what it is

Imaging narrows the possibilities; it frequently cannot close them.

**The classic worked example is the ring-enhancing lesion.** Its differential includes demyelinating disease, radiation necrosis, metastases, abscess, glioblastoma and other tumours, infarction and contusion — taught with the mnemonic "DR MAGIC."
https://appliedradiology.com/articles/brain-abscess

**Tumour mimics that regularly require a biopsy:**
- **Tumefactive demyelination** — single or multiple large (usually >2 cm) brain lesions that mimic tumour, abscess, metastasis or infarct, and frequently cause diagnostic uncertainty leading to brain biopsy, particularly in people with no known demyelinating disease.
https://www.ajronline.org/doi/10.2214/AJR.20.23226
- Features that hint it is *not* a tumour: an "open ring" enhancement pattern (with the continuous part on the ventricular side), little mass effect, minimal or no perilesional oedema, increased ADC, and low perfusion values.
https://www.ajronline.org/doi/10.2214/AJR.20.23226
- **Brain abscess** — an infection that looks like a tumour, and is treated completely differently.
https://appliedradiology.com/articles/brain-abscess
- **Dural-based metastasis vs meningioma** — an entire published imaging scoring system exists to try to separate these two on MR and CT, which tells you how hard it is.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12080040/
- The **dural tail** sign, though present in ~72% of meningiomas, is not specific and occurs with other lesions.
https://insightsimaging.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s13244-019-0697-7

**Publishable framing:** an MRI is a very good photograph. It shows shape, size, position, how much contrast the tissue takes up, and how much water is moving in it. It does not show what the cells *are*. Several very different things — a tumour, an infection, an area of inflammation, an old stroke, a reaction to previous treatment — can produce a similar photograph. That is not your radiologist hedging. It is the actual limit of the technology.

## C.3 What a biopsy or a resection adds

**Two different operations with two different purposes:**

- **Stereotactic biopsy** — a computer and a 3-D scanning device guide a needle through a small hole in the skull to remove a tissue sample; used for deep or hard-to-reach tumours.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
- **Open biopsy / craniotomy** — a section of skull is removed; allows tumour removal at the same time when the tumour is accessible.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**How the choice is made:** mainly on clinical status, lesion location and extent, and patient preference. Gross total resection is associated with better long-term outcome but carries perioperative risk. Biopsy is for tissue diagnosis only and is more suitable for frail or multimorbid patients and for tumours in highly eloquent areas.
https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/medicine-and-dentistry/stereotactic-biopsy

**Numbers for biopsy, in modern series:** diagnostic yield **91–98.4%**; transient morbidity 2.9–9%; permanent morbidity 1.5–5%; mortality ~1%. Small, deep lesions in eloquent brain carry higher rates of both morbidity and non-diagnosis; even brainstem and midbrain lesions can be sampled with low risk of permanent deficit and high yield.
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2022.822362/full · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8490258/

**Why biopsy matters even when the answer seems obvious:** brain biopsies in tumour-like lesions significantly changed diagnosis, management and outcomes.
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3138437/

**What the tissue is used for:** histological grading, proliferative index (mitotic activity), and molecular profiling revealing predictive DNA mutations.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

## C.4 Why the results take days to weeks — and what is happening in the lab

This is, editorially, the most valuable section in the whole brief. Patients experience this wait as abandonment. It is in fact a queue of sequential laboratory processes, each with a real duration.

### The stages, with real turnaround times

| Stage | What is happening | How long |
|---|---|---|
| **Frozen section** (during surgery) | Tissue is snap-frozen, cut and stained while the patient is still in theatre so the surgeon knows they have sampled the right thing | Mean **26 ± 5.6 minutes** (range 20–62) https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12185993/ |
| **Permanent sections** | Tissue is fixed and processed through a series of chemical solutions, embedded, cut thin and stained — this gives a far more accurate picture than the frozen section | "Up to a couple of weeks" https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-83328-2_8 |
| **Immunohistochemistry** | Antibodies are used to stain for specific proteins, identifying cell type and lineage | Conventional automated IHC: **2–4 hours** per run (but sits in a queue behind processing) https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-83328-2_8 |
| **FISH** (e.g. chromosome tests) | Fluorescent probes count copies of specific chromosome regions | Mean **11 days**, median 8 https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829 |
| **Gene panel / NGS sequencing** | The DNA is read for specific mutations | Mean **23 days**, median 21, range 14–45 https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829 |
| **Methylation array profiling** | The chemical tags on the DNA are read and matched against a reference database of tumour types | Mean **23 days**, median 19, range 13–40 https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829 — conventional profiling ~7–8 working days of actual processing, but arrays are run in cost-efficient batches of ~8 samples, so cases queue https://academic.oup.com/nop/advance-article/doi/10.1093/nop/npad040/7229197 |
| **Whole genome sequencing** | The entire genome is read | Mean/median **58 days** https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829 |
| **Integrated diagnosis issued** | All of the above are combined into one layered report | Median **15 days (2021) rising to 21 days (2024)** from surgery to final integrated diagnosis in a UK national audit; the 14-day benchmark was met by 48% of centres in 2021 and only 30% in 2024 https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829 |

**Three things that follow, all of which patients need told:**

1. **The wait is normal and is being measured.** There is a 14-day national benchmark; most centres miss it; this is a known system pressure, not your case being forgotten.
https://academic.oup.com/nop/article/13/3/465/8263829
2. **Batching is a real cause of delay.** Methylation arrays are run in batches of about 8 to control cost, so a sample may sit waiting for the batch to fill. That is a mundane, comprehensible reason — far better than the vacuum patients fill with catastrophe.
https://academic.oup.com/nop/advance-article/doi/10.1093/nop/npad040/7229197
3. **The first answer may change.** The frozen-section answer during surgery, the answer after permanent sections, and the final integrated diagnosis after molecular testing can differ. Patients are frequently given a provisional answer and then a different final one, and experience this as the doctors "changing their story." Naming it in advance defuses it.

**Why molecular tests are done at all** (the justification for the wait): WHO CNS5 lists DNA methylation profiling as a desirable or even essential method for accurately diagnosing several tumour types, and requires molecular alterations for integrated diagnosis.
https://academic.oup.com/nop/advance-article/doi/10.1093/nop/npad040/7229197 · https://academic.oup.com/noa/article/7/1/vdae228/7942502

**Where this is going:** nanopore sequencing workflows can produce a molecular classification within a **30-minute intraoperative window** and comprehensive profiling within 24 hours, and have been prospectively validated multicentre. Frame as emerging, not available everywhere.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-025-03562-5 · https://academic.oup.com/nop/advance-article/doi/10.1093/nop/npad040/7229197

## C.5 "Benign" vs "malignant" in the brain — and why "benign" is a misleading comfort

**The general oncology meaning:** benign = does not invade surrounding tissue and does not spread to distant sites; malignant = does.

**Why that definition partly breaks down inside the skull:**

- The skull is a closed box. A non-invasive, non-spreading mass still raises intracranial pressure and still compresses whatever is next to it (§0.1).
- Location outranks biology. "What really determines the impact of a benign brain tumour is where it sits" — even benign tumours can affect vision, hormone balance or movement if they press on critical areas, and a benign tumour in a vital area can be life-threatening.
https://www.jeffersonhealth.org/your-health/living-well/understanding-benign-brain-tumors
- "A non-cancerous mass pressing on your brainstem can be just as life-threatening as an aggressive tumour in a less critical region."
https://baptisthealth.net/baptist-health-news/what-is-a-benign-brain-tumor-symptoms-and-when-to-see-a-doctor
- The term is explicitly described as misleading, because these tumours can cause significant complications if untreated.
https://www.jeffersonhealth.org/your-health/living-well/understanding-benign-brain-tumors

**The classification data also complicate the word.** CBTRUS separates "malignant" from "non-malignant" rather than "benign"/"cancer": non-malignant tumours are ~74% of all brain/CNS tumours (19.19 per 100,000) vs malignant ~26% (6.86 per 100,000).
https://cbtrus.org/cbtrus-fact-sheet/

**Note the vocabulary shift and use it:** the registries say **non-malignant**, not *benign*. That is a deliberately more honest word — it says what the tumour does *not* do, without promising it is harmless. BrainHarbor should adopt "non-malignant" and explain the difference from "benign."

**Also note there is no staging system.** NCI: "There is no standard staging system for brain and spinal cord tumors."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

This is a real and frequent point of confusion, because everyone knows cancer "has stages." Brain tumours are described by **type and grade**, not stage. Metastatic brain tumours *do* have a stage — but it is the stage of the original cancer (stage 4), not a brain stage.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/metastatic-cancer

## C.6 Primary vs secondary — the fork in the road

**Primary:** "Tumors that start in the brain are called primary brain tumors. Primary brain tumors may spread to other parts of the brain or to the spine."
**Metastatic (secondary):** "A tumor that starts in another part of the body and spreads to the brain is called a metastatic brain tumor."
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**Which is more common:** metastatic brain tumours are more common than primary brain tumours, making up over half of clinically diagnosed brain tumours in adults.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**What clinicians do with the fork:**
- Multiple lesions at the grey–white junction in someone with a cancer history strongly suggests metastasis (§B.3).
- If there is no known primary, the search moves to the body: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, bone scan, FDG PET/CT.
https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/management-brain-metastases · https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5977273/
- PET can help distinguish primary from metastatic tumours.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
- If the primary cannot be found, tissue from the brain lesion is obtained.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10864137/

**Why the fork matters to the reader:** it decides which specialty leads, which drugs are on the table, and whether the rest of the body needs scanning too.

## C.7 The tumour board — the meeting nobody tells patients about

- **What it is:** a multidisciplinary meeting where specialists review complex or unclear brain tumour cases and recommend the approach.
- **Who is there:** neuro-oncologists, neurosurgeons, radiation oncologists, pathologists and neuroradiologists as standard; sometimes clinical researchers, palliative care specialists, social workers, neuropsychologists and geneticists.
- **When a case goes:** before surgery (to decide the approach or whether to operate at all); after surgery (to review pathology and biomarker results); when imaging is unclear (progression vs scar tissue vs treatment effect); and to identify clinical trial opportunities.
- **Patients do not attend.** But they should tell their team their quality-of-life concerns and goals beforehand, because those are what the board is trying to serve. The primary clinician explains the recommendation afterwards.
- **It is advice, not an order.** "The final decision about care is made between the patient and their doctor."
- **Two questions patients can ask:** Does this hospital have a regular tumour board? Will my case be presented at it?
https://braintumor.org/news/brain-tumor-board-101-answers-to-frequently-asked-questions/

## C.8 Second opinions — including on the pathology itself

Most patient material treats "second opinion" as a clinical-judgement thing. The stronger, less-known point is that the **diagnosis itself** can change on expert review.

- In consultative surgical neuropathology review, **major disagreements occurred in 12% of cases and minor disagreements in a further 12%**.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25972322/
- Novel molecular tests contributed to the diagnosis in **55% of major-disagreement cases**; poorly differentiated tumours benefited most from second review, through specialised immunohistochemistry, NanoString and targeted NGS.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25972322/
- In a paediatric CNS series, diagnostic discordance was found in 30 of 141 challenging cases sent for expert review (~21%).
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28675683/
- Centres without specialist neuropathology and molecular infrastructure benefit substantially from second-review referral.
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28675683/

NBTS's question list includes asking who to consult for another perspective, whether to visit an NCI-designated centre, who the top experts in that tumour type are, and how to access one's own medical records.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/diagnosis-treatment/diagnosis/key-questions/

**Patient-facing framing:** asking for a second opinion is not an accusation. For unusual or hard-to-classify tumours, a specialist centre may run tests the first laboratory could not, and that can change the answer.

## C.9 What to do and who to ask while waiting

Assembled from the guideline and patient-organisation material above:

**Practical:**

## C.10 Questions for the "no name yet" reader

1. What did the scan actually show — where, how big, and is it inside the brain or on the covering?
2. What are the possibilities you are considering, including the ones that are not cancer?
3. Can imaging alone tell us, or will we need tissue?
4. If we need tissue, is that a needle biopsy or an operation, and why that one?
5. Which tests have been sent, and when do you expect each one back?
6. Will there be a first answer and then a final answer? What might change between them?
7. Is my case going to a tumour board? When?
8. Who do I call between now and then, and what symptoms mean I call today?
9. Is this something that started in my brain, or something that came from somewhere else? How will you tell?
10. If it turns out to be unusual, will the tissue be sent for a specialist second opinion?
11. Can I have copies of the scan and the report?

# PART D — Disagreements, ambiguities and things that look outdated

## D.1 Genuine scientific disagreement

**1. Brain invasion as a stand-alone grade 2 criterion for meningioma.**
It is in the 2016 revision and retained in CNS5 as a stand-alone criterion for grade 2.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10339387/
But: "the prognostic role of brain invasion has recently been questioned"; "the prognostic relevance of brain invasion as a stand-alone criterion is still unclear"; several studies found no clear association between brain invasion and recurrence/progression; it is "not uniformly accepted."
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10339387/
cIMPACT-NOW update 8 responded by requiring molecular testing before grading brain-invasive otherwise benign (BIOB) meningiomas.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776
**Implication for BrainHarbor:** if a reader's report says "brain invasion," do not let the page imply this is settled bad news. State that it triggers extra molecular testing and a closer plan.

**2. HRT and meningioma risk.**
CUH patient information says menopause hormone therapy "seems to increase the risk of meningioma," while noting the evidence is mixed and that oestrogen-only preparations may carry slightly higher risk than combined.
https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/hormone-therapy-and-meningioma/
A growth-rate analysis of incidental meningiomas in women on oestrogen HRT is a separate line of evidence.
https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-74344-x
Contrast with the **high-dose progestogen** evidence, which regulators have accepted as causal.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12925135/
**Implication:** do not merge these. High-dose progestogens = strong, regulator-endorsed, actionable. HRT = weaker and mixed. Standard combined oral contraceptives = no apparent increase.
https://www.cuh.nhs.uk/patient-information/hormone-therapy-and-meningioma/

**3. Mitotic count units.**
Older reports and many reviews use "per 10 high-power fields" (4–19 for grade 2, ≥20 for grade 3). CNS5/cIMPACT-NOW now uses **per mm²** (≥2.5 and ≥12.5 respectively, assessed over 1.6 mm²). These are meant to be equivalent but a reader comparing two reports will see different numbers.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/2/319/7745776

**4. LITT vs bevacizumab for radiation necrosis.**
Both are used; studies suggest LITT gives longer overall survival and better long-term lesion volume reduction, but this is comparative observational evidence, not a randomised head-to-head.
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11060-020-03570-0
**Implication:** present as "two options, chosen by circumstances," not a ranking.

## D.2 Numbers that look contradictory but are not

- **Meningioma share of tumours: 41.7% vs 37.6%.** CBTRUS 41.7% is of *all brain and other CNS tumours* (US registry, malignant + non-malignant). The 37.6% figure in reviews is of *all primary intracranial tumours in adults*. Different denominators.
https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/26/Supplement_6/vi1/7797290 · https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://www.ajnr.org/content/46/2/240
- **Meningioma incidence 23.02 vs 10.15 vs 10.99 per 100,000.** 23.02 is the rate in adults aged 40+ specifically; ~10.15 is the average annual age-adjusted rate across all ages; 10.99 is the rate for "tumours of the meninges" as a grouping in a different data window.
https://cbtrus.org/cbtrus-fact-sheet/ · https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/meningioma/ · https://academic.oup.com/neuro-oncology/article/27/Supplement_4/iv1/8285946
- **Incidental meningioma prevalence 1.6% vs 0.52%.** The Rotterdam 1.6% is "benign primary tumours, mainly meningiomas" in an older population cohort; 0.52% is a pooled meta-analytic estimate of incidental *meningioma* across 37,697 people of mixed ages. Different definitions and different age structures.
https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa070972 · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00701-021-04919-8
- **Brain metastasis incidence by primary: 19.9% (lung) vs 16.3% (lung, 5-year cumulative).** Different study designs and denominators.
https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.2004.12.149 · https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/cncr.10541
- **Grade distribution 85–95% / 5–10% / 1–5% vs 80% / 18% / 2%.** The first is a literature review range; the second is a single neurosurgical centre's patient-facing material, which will over-represent operated (and therefore higher-grade) tumours. Prefer the review range and say "most."
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

## D.3 Sources that are outdated or use retired terminology — handle with care

**NCI's Adult CNS Tumors PDQ patient version is the biggest problem.** It is authoritative, licensing-clean and highly citable, but:
- It uses **Roman numerals** ("grade I meningioma", "Grade II and III meningiomas") — retired by CNS5.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
- It still uses **"hemangiopericytoma"** ("grade II and III meningiomas and hemangiopericytomas") — a term retired in CNS5 in favour of solitary fibrous tumour.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq · https://pubs.rsna.org/doi/full/10.1148/radiol.221885
- It states grade II and III meningiomas "are likely to spread within the brain and spinal cord" — phrasing that will read to a patient as "it will spread," which overstates it.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq
- Its metastatic brain tumour treatment section is framed around **"whole-brain radiation therapy ± surgery"** for 1–4 lesions, which is behind the 2022 ASCO–SNO–ASTRO position that SRS alone should be offered for 1–4 unresected metastases.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq · https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314
- It lists **bevacizumab for recurrent glioblastoma** as the targeted-therapy example, with no mention of CNS-active targeted agents for metastases.
https://www.cancer.gov/types/brain/patient/adult-brain-treatment-pdq

**Action:** cite NCI for definitions and for the treatment *categories*, but take grading language from WHO CNS5/cIMPACT-NOW and radiation recommendations from ASCO–SNO–ASTRO. Do not mirror NCI's Roman numerals.

**Mayfield Clinic** (excellent, detailed, patient-pitched) also uses Roman numerals and gives a grade distribution skewed by surgical case mix. Use it for location–symptom mapping and surgical technique; not for grading language or epidemiology.
https://mayfieldclinic.com/pe-meni.htm

**NBTS meningioma page** publishes 1-, 5- and 10-year survival percentages. Do **not** carry those over — they violate the no-prognosis rule. Its incidence figures and the "found incidentally" framing are usable.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/meningioma/

**NBTS metastatic brain tumors page** describes treatment as surgery/radiosurgery "potentially followed by whole brain radiation therapy," and "multiple lesions may receive WBRT alone." That is closer to pre-2016 practice than to the 2022 guideline. Use its definitions; take its treatment sequencing with caution.
https://braintumor.org/brain-tumors/about-brain-tumors/brain-tumor-types/metastatic-brain-tumors/ · https://ascopubs.org/doi/10.1200/JCO.21.02314

**Patchell 1990 / 1998, Brown 2016, JLGK0901 2014** are historical anchors. Cite them as *why practice is what it is*, not as current recommendations — the current recommendation is the 2022 guideline.

**Anything pre-2021 on meningioma grading** predates the molecular criteria (TERT, CDKN2A/B) entirely. Anything pre-2024 predates cIMPACT-NOW update 8's mm² thresholds and 1p-loss rule.

**"Neurofibromatosis type 2"** appears throughout the meningioma literature including sources cited here (e.g. the 2023 radiation-therapy review, Mayfield). The condition was renamed in 2022. Use NF2-related schwannomatosis and note the old name.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10477988/ · https://www.ctf.org/news/revised-diagnostic-criteria-for-nf2-swn-announcement/
